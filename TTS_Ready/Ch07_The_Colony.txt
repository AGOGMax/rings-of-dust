RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Seven

The Colony

She found the colony by accident. Nobody found it on purpose, except Tyler.

The transponder signal was on a frequency she didn't recognize — not in any registry she'd filed with, not on the standard Federation band, not on the miner's auxiliary band she checked when she was past the main belt. She'd picked it up three days out from Titan on a routing she'd taken because it was shorter and she was low on time and not yet low on fuel, which turned out to be the first wrong decision in a series of wrong decisions. By the time the signal resolved into something intelligible, she was low on both.

The colony was on an asteroid — a real one, not a station rock. Natural iron-silicate formation, roughly three kilometers across, with a spin so slow you couldn't feel it. The habitation structure ran along the inner surface of a carved tunnel through the asteroid's middle: an old mining excavation that someone had sealed and pressurized and built something inside. The approach was marked with hand-painted indicators rather than navigation beacons. She landed in a bay that had been bored by hand drill, looking at marks on the rock that said, in several languages: LEAVE YOUR WEAPONS AT THE DOOR.

She wasn't carrying weapons. She'd sold them at Titan.

Her name was Jill.

Jill was a black market runner — scavenger, anti-establishment to the marrow, the kind of person who moved around the edges of the systems other people built and took what fell off the trucks. She was punk rock in a universe run by people who looked like her cousins.

---

The man who met her at the bay entrance had his hands in his pockets and the expression of someone who was curious about her in a way that wasn't threatening and also wasn't quite safe. He was tall. He had the kind of face that meant something in any room he was standing in — not handsome exactly, but arranged in a way that made you pay attention to what it was doing. He wore a work shirt and work boots and nothing about his appearance suggested authority, which she understood later was deliberate.

"You ran out," he said. Not a question.

"I'm short. Not out. I need two cells to make the run back to Titan supply."

He looked at her for a moment. "We have cells. They're not for sale."

"I can trade." She had a manifest. She handed it over.

He looked at it the way someone looks at a map of a city they don't live in. "We don't need any of this."

"Then what do you need?"

"Mostly," he said, "for people not to want things from us." He handed the manifest back. "Come inside. Eat. We'll talk about what you actually came here for."

His name was Tyler Durden.

"I know how this reads from the outside," Tyler said. "A soap factory that became a death cult on an asteroid nobody wanted." He did not smile. "I don't argue with the description. I just keep walking down the tunnel."

---

The tunnel was lit with strips of amber light that gave everything the color of an afternoon she couldn't identify — somewhere warm, somewhere on a planet with weather. The habitation ran along the length of the bore: sleeping quarters, a communal kitchen, a workspace full of FuelCell equipment that she looked at for a long time as they walked past.

"You make them," she said.

"We manage them," Tyler said.

"There's a difference?"

"That's the question," he said.

The kitchen had real food. Not ration blocks — actual food, grown and prepared,
the kind that had taste and texture and temperature. She sat down and ate and the
first bite hit her like a door opening into a room she'd forgotten existed. Bread
with a crust. Something green that was not a paste. A soup that tasted like it
had been someone's afternoon to make. He sat across from her and watched without speaking. He refilled her water when
it was empty. He did not ask a single question until she set the fork down.
During the energy crisis, most of humanity had settled into ration blocks —
processed, adequate, and without memory of anything better. The colony did not
explain the table. They just fed her.

When she was done, he said: "There's one way to get cells here."

She waited.

"You make them. The same way Elsa made the first ones — by hand, from raw DarkX, in the room she built over the cave floor. We don't sell them and we don't give them. They belong to the person who made them."

"I've handled Darker Matter cells for six years."

"Handling them is not the same thing." He looked at her. "When Elsa started, there were no Darker Matter cells to handle. No process to read, no industry, no laboratory that had ever made one — the world still ran on the old fuels. She didn't learn this from anyone. She came out here with a set of old teachings and a theory about the material, and she found out what DarkX was, and what it would do, and how to make it hold — alone, at cave-scale, years before anyone in a clean lab on Earth stumbled onto a piece of what she already knew."

"How long."

"Three or four days to make them. Eleven days to sync." He watched her work through the math. "You're not waiting eleven days to find out if they work. You're waiting eleven days because that's how long it takes."

She thought about her contract window. She thought about leaving with two cells she had made herself, by hand, the way Elsa Klein had made the first ones, three days past the moon that people back on Earth spent their whole lives dreaming about reaching.

"Show me," she said.

---

Tyler took her below.

Forty meters down, through a passage cut by hand in the colony's third year, the tunnel opened into the cave.

She stopped at the entrance.

The walls were dark and irregular — natural formation, a cave the rock had made over millions of years without consulting anyone. The DarkX had run through the stone here once in every direction: not a pocket or a seam but a vein system threading through the asteroid's interior like a nervous system. It had been dry for seventeen years now — the material gone, the asteroid's whole supply spent. But the rock remembered, and the cave
still held the quality of a room where something important had happened.

"When the 369 arrived, the asteroid was harder than anything they'd modeled," Tyler said. "The pressurization kept failing. The thermal systems took longer than projected. People died in the first weeks and kept dying — cold, equipment failures, the ways a hostile environment finds every gap." He paused. "It took most of a year for the numbers to fall to eleven. One at a time, over months."

Jill said nothing.

"They hadn't brought provisions for the return trip. That wasn't an accident." He said it simply, the way you state something that has always been true. "They flew past Titan — past the moon that people back on Earth spent their whole lives dreaming about reaching — and they didn't save anything for the swim back. That was the commitment they made before they left, and three hundred and fifty-eight of them kept it completely." A pause. "By the time eleven were left, the ship's heat and food stores were gone. No extraction window. No path out. An asteroid and a partially pressurized tunnel and eleven people at the end of everything they'd brought with them."

He looked at the vein in the wall, long spent.

"Elsa had a set of teachings with her. Old world — from a woman in the Order whose records she'd carried since before they left. The core law: do what thou wilt shall be the whole of the Law. Which doesn't mean what it sounds like. It means: find the single irreducible purpose your existence is here to serve and pursue it without allowing anything to veer you from it." He paused. "And a ritual at the center of that practice — Liber Samekh. The invocation of your own highest self. Your truest purpose made conscious and speakable."

She waited. The cave air was cold against her arms. She could feel the shape
of the vein in the wall the way you feel a river through the ground it
carved.

"She fasted for three days first. Cold water, nothing in the body that wasn't essential. You're trying to remove the noise so you can hear the signal." He looked at the cave floor. "She came down here and practiced the ritual in the dark. Standing with her arms outstretched in the posture the practice requires. Vibrating the names." He was quiet for a moment. "Thee I invoke, the Bornless One. Thee that didst create the Earth and the Heavens. The names go on for a long time. You vibrate them from the chest, not the throat — you feel them in the body more than you hear them. The ritual isn't a petition. It's a declaration. This is what I am. This is what I came here to do. I am still here to do it. Spoken into whatever is listening, until the noise is gone and only the signal is left."

He looked at the far wall, at the thin exhausted trace of the old vein.

"She was mid-ritual when something moved."

He said it without drama. The way you report what a careful scientist wrote down.

"A thread of it — there, where the vein ran thickest then. Moving through the crack in the wall toward her. Not the way liquid moves downward, not the way gas diffuses outward. Moving the way something moves when it has decided where it is going." He paused. "She kept going. Kept the names, kept the posture. She understood that stopping would be the wrong response."

Jill looked at the crack in the wall — narrow, irregular, the DarkX that had moved through it long since spent.

"More of it came. Slowly. In amounts it chose. A tendril, then another. It gathered in the space between her and the wall and it stayed there." He looked at her. "She finished the invocation. And then she held out the jar."

He let that sit.

"The DarkX moved into it. Not all of it — a portion. What it chose to give. She didn't reach for it. She didn't extract it or scrape it or force it into the container." He paused. "You understand why that matters."

Jill thought about the Polaris. What everybody in any transit corridor knew about the Polaris — the harvester opened, the sample forced from the plasma, the chain reaction that took three hundred and sixty-nine people.

"It chose to separate," she said.

"It chose to separate. Small amounts, slowly, into a vessel it had decided to enter. That's the whole difference." He turned back toward the passage that led up out of the cave. "Elsa didn't discover a technique. She was given one."

---

She stood in the cave after he left.

The vein in the far wall held nothing now, the asteroid's DarkX long since spent through decades of making and sacrifice. She could feel the shape of what had been here — not a presence anymore, more like the impression a presence leaves in a room. She thought about Elsa in this cave, in the dark, speaking her purpose into it, and something in the wall beginning to move toward the sound of her.

She thought about the children running in the late afternoon light.

She thought: I don't know what I am yet either.

Then she went up to make the cells.

---

The preparation room was built directly over the cave floor. Elsa's worktable. Her magnets on their rails — two iron blocks that shifted the tools on the far wall when they powered on. Her glass jar on the rack, worn at the lip from forty years of the same hands setting it down. The wooden cup for measuring salt, smooth along the rim. The salt from the asteroid's eastern seam — milky-white silicate, the compound Elsa had found in year one and built the entire process around.

Tyler opened the DarkX case.

She had handled Darker Matter cells for six years — compressed, stabilized, sealed, inert. She had never seen it raw.

It moved.

Not dramatically. A slow rearrangement of itself — obsidian, not quite liquid and not quite gas — tendrils forming and dissolving along the surface and then reaching. Toward the light. Toward her. The air around it was colder than the rest of the
room and smelled like mineral and something older — the way deep stone smells
when you break it open for the first time. She could feel it in her teeth before
she felt it on her skin. The color was not quite black: the color of a shadow in a room with no clear source, present everywhere and originating nowhere.

"She said not to move back," Tyler said.

Jill did not move back.

She held the open jar toward the case the way Tyler showed her — not reaching in, not extracting. Presenting the vessel. Waiting.

The plasma leaned toward the jar. A tendril separated from the mass — slowly, deliberately, in the amount it chose — and moved into the glass.

She held very still.

More came. Small amounts, the DarkX parceling itself out in portions that felt considered. When enough had gathered she placed the salt in with it — the plasma moved toward the salt immediately, settling into the mineral structure. She sealed the jar. Tyler activated the magnets.

The field came on and the plasma threaded deeper into the salt — dark filaments impregnating the silicate lattice, finding the geometry the cold would eventually lock. A structure beginning at the intersection of two materials that had been waiting for each other.

Then the visions began.

---

Not suddenly. She was watching the filaments move and the lattice form, and then she was somewhere else.

Children. Late afternoon light on a planet with weather. Three of them, maybe four, running freely — without looking behind them. Their faces turned toward her mid-run and she saw her own cheekbones, her own jaw, her own coloring. Not children she had been. Not memory. Something she had not yet become. They ran past her and did not stop.

The light changed.

A table set for a dinner that had never been about hunger. The weight of silverware belonging to people who had always had it. Faces she recognized the way you recognize a face in mirrors — shared architecture, the same nose, a way of carrying the jaw. Family in some technical sense she had never claimed.

Their hands moved with the ease of people who had never calculated what a gesture would cost. One of them looked up, looked in her direction, looked through her — the quality of vision belonging to people who have never needed to notice what they're overlooking. Then looked away.

She had felt this before. Not from these people. From cousins she'd been left with twice when her mother was working. Who had not been unkind — indifferent, which was harder. Who communicated through no words at all that she was a guest in a world they owned.

She had been twelve. She had not understood yet that this was the outline of something much larger than a dining room.

The harmonic of the magnets. The cold of the preparation room. She came back.

Elsa had written about this in the sections of the notebooks Petra kept sealed. It does not show you things that are not real. It shows you what you are already carrying. I did not know what I was carrying until it showed me. I did not frame this as a side effect. I framed it as instruction.

Jill looked at the jar. The lattice was advancing along the interior wall.

She sat down in Elsa's chair and did not move for a long time.

---

The cell was fully formed by day three — the lattice complete, the exterior structure set during the second night phase when the rotation carried the preparation room away from the sun and the temperature dropped to near absolute zero. No industrial containment. No liquid nitrogen. The asteroid's own cold. The cold the DarkX had always known.

She held the sealed jar on day four and felt it — a low resonance, below hearing. The sensation of something searching for a frequency just out of range.

Syncing. It would do this for eleven days. She would feel it the whole time.

---

She walked the tunnel. She ate in the kitchen. She watched Petra run her logs without comment and did not ask about the notebooks and noticed Petra notice this.

On the second day, a woman named Yun sat with her in the work bay while they ran cable for the Void-Walkers' tunnel rigging. Yun had been at the colony for three years and had the quality of someone who had thought very carefully about something and arrived at a position she was comfortable defending.

"The two groups," Jill said. "The ones who were here before Tyler and the ones who came after."

"Loyalists," Yun said. "Petra's people. They maintain what Elsa built — the science, the cells, the cycle." She set down the cable spool. "Elsa mapped all of it. Years of observation, no commercial pressure, just watching. The first cells took forty-five days — she had no liquid nitrogen, only the cold the asteroid itself provided. The deep cold of a rock that had not been near a star in an era anyone could calculate. The DarkX responded to that cold. Forty-five days for each cycle to complete, each one Elsa's alone, each one a full asteroid rotation of patience. Then she found that the extreme cold — the near-absolute-zero of the night phase, when the rotation carried the preparation room into the dark — cut the cycle to eleven days. The asteroid's own cold did what liquid nitrogen would do decades later in a laboratory."

"And now?" Jill said. "You told me the rock's been dry seventeen years."

"Now we get the raw material the long way around." Yun rolled the spool back up. "The Collective runs us Darker Matter crystals — cells they've depleted off Alpha's grid, crystals they can't sit on forever anyway. We sit on them. After eleven days a crystal gives up the lattice and reverts to raw DarkX, plasma again, and that's the thing Elsa's whole process was built on. We make the cells from that, in her room, the way she did." A pause. "It wasn't always like this. When the asteroid first ran dry, the making just stopped — Elsa was already gone, there was no DarkX and no maker, and the sacrifices stopped with it. The colony didn't starve over it. We had more gold by then than three generations could spend. We bought what we needed and we lived. But the cave went quiet. For years nobody walked the east tunnel."

"So what started it again."

"Tyler." She looked toward the tunnel. "Six years ago he came in off a supply hauler carrying his own grief and a crate of factory cells — the commercial kind, the kind the rest of the world burns without thinking about it. He'd read everything Elsa ever wrote. He stood in the main tunnel and made the most coherent argument Petra had ever heard for why a cell should be given to the void instead of managed, and then he carried one down the east tunnel and let it go. Eleven days later a gold asteroid came back. The ceremony's been running again ever since — Petra's people for the science of it, Tyler's people for the meaning."

"Not how. Why," Jill said.

"Not how. Why." Yun nodded slowly. "Elsa understood the mechanics completely. Tyler asked what the mechanics were for."

Jill thought about the cave. About what moved toward the sound of a woman declaring her purpose in the dark.

"That first time, Petra told him he was wrong," Yun said. "He agreed she might be right. He let the cell go anyway. He didn't argue with the word sacrifice — he just disagreed about what was lost." A pause. "When the gold came back — twenty meters across — Petra did the math herself. She told him he was still wrong. She still means it."

"But the math is right."

"The math has not missed once," Yun said.

In the carry case at Jill's hip, the cell searched for its frequency and found a little more of it each day.

---

On day five Tyler brought her to where the sacrifice cells were racked at the far end of the preparation room — the four that had been running the longest, casings crusted with exterior crystal growth, valuable by any measure she had ever used to measure anything. He talked about giving and receiving and the difference between the two. She held her understanding at a distance she wasn't yet sure she wanted to close. She felt the resonance of her own cell searching at her hip and said nothing about it.

On day seven the Void-Walkers gathered at the east tunnel.

She stood at the entrance and watched — cells carried with both hands, the walk into the east tunnel, the long quiet after each one went past the point where the rock's gravity let go. The tunnel ran toward the black hole the asteroid sat at the edge of. Past that threshold the hole's pull took over from the rock's, and whatever crossed the line went into it and did not come back. She watched their faces after. Not grief, not relief. The specific quality of completion.

She felt the resonance of the cell in the carry case shift — deeper, as if the proximity to the void's threshold changed the quality of what it was searching for.

She went back to the kitchen. She held her cup and felt the cell syncing and watched the colony move through its evening.

---

Day eight: the night phase.

The asteroid's rotation was thirty-one hours. During the long hours facing away from the sun the outer chambers went cold — not life-support-failure cold, not vacuum, but the cold of an enormous rock in space giving back what it had stored. The preparation room faced the dark side. Elsa had placed her first completed jars directly on the asteroid's exterior surface during these hours — beyond the colony wall, in the dark, where the temperature dropped to near absolute zero. The asteroid's own cold. The cold that had been here since before anyone arrived with a jar and a handful of salt and the direction of the material itself.

She sat with the carry case on her lap and felt the syncing shift in the cold. Something settling more fully into its frequency. She stayed through the full night phase. On day nine she came back before the rotation began. On day ten she fell asleep in the chair and woke in the warmth of the day cycle to find the resonance unchanged and right.

---

Day eleven.

She was in the tracking room at 0411 station time when the proximity alert lit. The room was small — three screens, a console,
standing room for maybe eight. There were twelve people in it. Nobody had told
them to come. The air was warm from the bodies and the equipment, and someone had
brought coffee, and nobody was drinking it.

She had reviewed the logs — same vector, six consecutive events, each within the margin Elsa had calculated in her notebooks decades ago. She understood the physics. She stood in the tracking room with that understanding complete and watched the screen the same way everyone else was watching it: waiting for what she already knew was coming, which was not the same as being ready for it.

The asteroid resolved from the dark side of the tracking field and slowed in the way objects in space do not slow.

Jackpot. Quietly. The word for what it was.

The room held the silence of people who have stopped arguing and are simply watching something be true. Petra was beside her — had arrived without Jill noticing.

She was in her seventies — white hair cut close, the posture of someone who had made peace with exactly the amount of space she took up. She was one of the original eleven. Three hundred and sixty-nine people had set out with Elsa Klein. Eleven survived. Petra had been twenty-five when they launched. She remembered the number of the dead. She did not say it aloud.

"The first time," Petra said, "there were eleven of us."

She said it to the screen.

"We had been down to two meals a day for six weeks. The heat was rationing itself — we were burning the last of what we'd carried out, and when it was gone we were going to freeze in the dark. Elsa had come back from the cave four days before — three days in there, alone, with nothing but the ritual and what came through the wall to meet her. She built the first cell in three days." She paused. "Understand what that cell was. It was the only source of energy we had left. It could have run the heat. It could have held the pressure, kept the lights, kept eleven people breathing through the worst of it — it was enough to save us. She carried it to the east tunnel and let it go into the dark instead. None of us stopped her. None of us said anything, because there was nothing that covered what we were watching — people at the very end of everything, giving away the one thing that could have kept them alive." A pause. "Eleven people who had flown past Titan and not looked back. Who had buried three hundred and fifty-eight others in this rock and kept going. Who had carried no provisions for a return trip and had known that before they left."

The asteroid's mass and density resolved into numbers on the screen.

"Eleven days later she was holding the monitor when the return appeared on the sensor. A gold asteroid — twenty meters across, solid gold, on the same vector she had calculated in her notebooks. She did the math before any of us understood what we were looking at. When she finished she set the monitor down on the rock and put her face in her hands." Petra was quiet for a moment. "Not crying. Just holding it. The way you hold something when it's finally over."

"What did she do with it?"

"She ordered supplies," Petra said. "Food, heat cells, pressurization equipment. Everything the colony needed to stop dying and start building." She looked at Jill. "That was the test. Not the cold and not the dying — anyone can die. The test was whether eleven starving people would give away the one thing that could have saved them, on nothing but the faith that something would answer. When the gold came back, Elsa understood that it had. That was the moment the colony made it past its trial. Not when she found the cave. Not when she built the first cell. When the void answered."

In the carry case at Jill's hip, the cell went quiet for the first time in eleven days.

---

She checked her cells that afternoon. Standard post-sync inspection — the kind of thing you did after eleven days to confirm the cycle had completed and the casings were stable. She had made two. She needed two to make the run back to Titan. She opened the carry case on the work bay table and looked at them under the amber light.

The first cell was clean. Interior crystals formed, sync complete, charge nominal. A good cell. The kind that would get her to Titan and back without trouble.

The second cell had something on the outside.

She stared at it. She picked it up and turned it in the light. The exterior of the casing — the base, where nothing should have been — was crusted with a dense, structured crystal formation. Dark and heavy, catching the amber in fractured points. Not internal growth. Exterior. On the outside of a cell she had made eleven days ago in a room above a cave on an asteroid at the edge of a black hole.

She had handled FuelCells for six years. She had never seen this.

Petra was at her bench. Jill brought the cell to her without saying anything and set it down under the lamp.

Petra looked at it for a long time.

She picked it up with both hands. She turned it the way Jill had turned it — slowly, under the light, reading the crystal formation the way she read everything: without hurry, without assumption, with the specific attention of a woman who had been looking at cells for longer than Jill had been alive.

"Jackpot," Petra said. Quietly. The way she always said it.

"What does that mean."

"One in seven hundred and forty." Petra set the cell down. "On any given sync cycle, one cell in seven forty produces an exterior deposit. It never repeats on the same cell. One chance. One cell." She looked at the formation. "You made two cells. One of them hit."

"What are the odds on two cells."

"Absurd." Petra did not smile. "But the odds have never been the interesting part."

Jill looked at the crystal formation. It was large — larger than anything she had seen described in the literature, larger than the deposits Tyler had shown her on the colony's long-locked Jackpot cells. She said this.

Petra nodded. "Three hundred and thirty-three times the crystal mass that was used to assemble the cell. That's the standard Jackpot yield — what Alpha documents in their batch logs, what the Collective tracks in their own records." She paused. "This is not the standard yield."

Jill looked at her.

"This is four times that."

She let that sit.

"The colony's cells produce a different Jackpot than the ones made in laboratories," Petra said. "We have documented it across every Jackpot event since Elsa's first — the exterior deposit here, at this asteroid, in proximity to the void, is consistently four times the yield of an equivalent cell produced anywhere else. Four times three hundred and thirty-three." She picked up the cell again. "One thousand three hundred and thirty-two times the original assembly mass. On the exterior. One time. Your cell."

Jill looked at the crystal formation under the amber light. The weight of it in Petra's hands was visible — not just mass but consequence. A single cell, made by hand in three days, had produced more Darker Matter on its exterior than Jill had transported in six years of black market running.

Tyler appeared in the doorway. He had not been summoned. He looked at the cell in Petra's hands and his face did the thing his face did when the math confirmed something he had already believed — not triumph, not vindication, just the quiet settling of a man whose framework had received another data point.

"The void knows what was given to it," Tyler said. "It knows who made the cell. It knows where the cell was made. The yield is not random. The yield is a response."

Petra set the cell down. "The yield is consistent with a proximity variable related to the gravitational signature of the east tunnel's terminus. The mechanism is physical. The amplification is documented."

"Both," Tyler said.

"I am not conceding both."

"You never do."

They looked at each other the way they always looked at each other — six years of the same argument, the same data, the same conclusion arrived at from opposite directions.

Jill looked at the crystal formation on the exterior of the cell.

She thought about what it was worth. On any market she had ever worked — more than the Hound's Tooth, more than every job she had ever run, more than anything she could name in a number. Darker Matter crystals at 1,332 times the assembly mass, sitting on the outside of a casing she had made with her own hands in a room above a cave where a woman had spoken into the dark and the dark had answered.

She thought about carrying the crystals out of here.

She couldn't.

Not because anyone told her not to. Not because Tyler asked her to leave them. Not because Petra said the colony needed them. Nobody said any of those things. The colony had more gold than it could spend in a generation. They did not need Jill's Jackpot yield.

She couldn't take the crystals because taking them would feel like pulling a plant out of the ground it grew in. The cell had been made here, in this room, from material that had come from this asteroid, in proximity to the void that ran through the east tunnel. The Jackpot had happened here. The amplification — whatever it was, physics or intention or both — had happened here. The crystals on the exterior of that casing belonged to this place the way the rock belonged to this place. Carrying them to Titan felt wrong in a way she could not articulate and did not try to.

"I'm leaving the harvest," she said. "The crystals stay here. I'll take the cells."

Petra looked at her.

Tyler looked at her.

Neither of them said anything. Petra picked up the Jackpot cell and carried it to the preparation chamber. She harvested the exterior crystals with the care of someone who had done it a thousand times — separating the dense formation from the casing without disturbing the interior, setting the crystal mass on the rack beside the colony's own Jackpot yields. She returned the cell to Jill, clean now, the exterior bare, the interior still charged and ready.

Jill put both cells back in the carry case.

Two cells. Enough to get home. The crystals stayed where they belonged.

---

She left the next morning.

Tyler was at the east tunnel entrance. Not blocking the way.

"You're going to tell people," he said.

"I don't know who I'd tell."

"You'll find someone." A fact he had already accepted. "Elsa thought it should stay here. I think it can't."

Jill held the carry case with the two cells she had made. She thought about Petra's face in her hands. About the children running in the late afternoon light on a planet with weather. About the table with the candles and the twelve-year-old in the doorway being looked through. About the crack in the cave wall and what came through it toward the sound of a woman declaring her purpose in the dark.

She thought: I never saved anything for the swim back either. I just didn't know that's what I was doing.

She didn't say any of this.

"Which one of you is right?" she said.

He looked toward the east tunnel, and the void beyond it.

"Ask me in eleven days," he said.

---

Nobody had told her to look at the grave carved into the rock at the end of the main tunnel. She had passed within forty meters of the marker without reading the name.

That was the thing about the colony. You were not a leech if you came in honest — if you ate the food they offered and asked the questions that were yours to ask and left with what they chose to give you. You were something else if you came looking to take without staying long enough to understand what you were taking from.

Most people who found the colony left with two cells and the memory of an amber-lit tunnel and food that tasted like somewhere they couldn't name.

Some of them came back.

The ones who came back were always looking for the same thing: to be in the room on the eleventh day, when the screen lit and someone said, quietly, the only word that fit.

Jackpot.
