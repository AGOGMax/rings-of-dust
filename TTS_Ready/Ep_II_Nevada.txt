EPILOGUE II

The town was called Eureka. It did not appear on any map he had been given, which was the point. Population four hundred and something. Situated at the end of a two-lane road that gave out at the foot of a range of hills nobody had named anything useful.

He had spent nine weeks in a Federation holding facility outside Reno. No charges filed. No hearing scheduled. Then one morning the door was open and a man he had never seen before handed him an envelope. A bus ticket. Four hundred dollars in cash. A driver's license with his photograph on it and a name that was not his.

He had been given the name Dale Custer, which was not a name he would have chosen for himself.

He had read the envelope as extraction. Alpha took care of its own. That was the arrangement, and he had kept his side of it for twenty years. He assumed the destination was a place to wait until the noise died down.

The bus had dropped him at a filling station. The attendant looked at him like something that had arrived already wrong. Taylor had not slept on the bus. He had the pocket watch in the inside breast pocket of his jacket, where he always kept it, and he had held it through the jacket fabric for most of the drive. There was nothing else to hold.

He had known, walking into Eureka from the filling station, that it would not take long. A town this size knew what it had lost and knew what a new face meant. The hospital was one old building on the northeast edge of town, unchanged since the pioneers put it there. The sign was still there. The sign would probably outlast the town. Eleven people had died when the supply chain collapsed — not all at once, drawn out over two years, the kind of dying that a small town does not recover from. He knew the number. He had been briefed on the region as a logistics parameter, years ago, in a room full of men who called it acceptable attrition in the transition corridor. He remembered the phrase because it was precise and he had always respected precision.

There was one road out of Eureka and it was the road he had come in on. He had noticed that from the bus window. He noticed it again now.

He did not have long. He could feel the geometry of the town organizing itself around his presence.

A man came to him before the others. He was not young. He wore a denim jacket with nothing on it. He stopped at a distance that was not quite conversational.

"The watch," the man said.

Taylor did not speak.

"Marcus wants what belongs to him."

Taylor reached into his jacket. The brass was warm from being against his chest for the better part of three days. He had carried it for longer than he had carried anything else in his life. He looked at it for a moment — the case, the weight of it, the engraving he had never had explained to him — and he understood, in the act of handing it over, who had sent him here and why.

Not Alpha. Not the Federation. Marcus.

The road attack on Cole and Ricardo had been Taylor's own call. Outside the mission. Unsanctioned. Men with guns on a highway because Taylor had wanted the problem solved early, and it had not been solved, and worse than that, it had been noticed. The machine did not tolerate improvisation. The envelope had not been extraction. It had been delivery.

The man took the watch without looking at it and walked back toward the filling station.

The town closed in from the edges.

Taylor stood in the road and did not move. He had been precise his entire career. He had not been wrong about that. He had simply been precise in service of something that had always been larger than he was, and he had never been told the shape of it, and now he was standing in the center of it with nothing left in his jacket pocket.

Nobody else would come. Marcus did not need to send anyone else. The town would do it, and the town would take its time.

The machine disciplines its own errors.
