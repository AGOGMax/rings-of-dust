RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Eighteen

Ray's Cargo

Six blocks from the hardware store was a park.

Ray had identified it in the early days of the Mikov Forsinko operation, on his
fourth perimeter walk, when he was still getting his cover right — the practiced
rhythm of a scientist on contract, efficient and unhurried, the lunch routes and
errand routes and the way a man who belongs somewhere moves through a
neighborhood versus the way a man who's watching it does. The park had two
benches facing a fountain that no longer worked. The fountain was shaped like a
heron — long-legged, long-necked, beak angled down toward a basin that had been
dry for years. It was concrete, gray-green, the color of things left outside
without attention. Whoever had cast it had been careful about the feathers: they
were carved individually along each wing, distinct, one by one, the work of
someone who had looked at a real heron and wanted to get it right. Whoever had
set the base had gotten the angle slightly wrong. The heron listed four degrees
toward the east and had been listing that way long enough that the tilt no longer
read as a mistake — it read as the bird's opinion.

Ray had liked it because it was the kind of detail that felt permanent in its
imperfections — no one was going to fix the heron — and permanent things made
good landmarks.

Squirrel was on the second bench.

He was reading a newspaper that was four days old in a language that was not his
first language — Ray had figured this out on the second day of the Panama
operation, from the way Squirrel's eyes moved along the lines, the slight
recalibration at the end of each paragraph. He had not mentioned it. Mentioning
things like that was not Ray's job. Noticing them was.

Red was at the far edge of the park, watching the street.

Ray sat down on the first bench. He did not look at Squirrel. He looked at the
heron.

He had been three blocks from the facility when his hands had started to shake.
He had been three and a half blocks when he had identified what the shaking was
— adrenaline clearing, the body filing its report. He had walked the six blocks
to the park with his hands in his pockets and his pace at
contractor-leaving-for-dinner, and the whole walk he had been thinking about his
colleagues.

He sat on the bench.

"Did you get it?" Squirrel said. He said it to the newspaper.

"Yes."

"The full protocol?"

"Disposal Protocol Eleven and the project archive up to the current quarter.
Seventeen months of records." Ray looked at the heron. "They named it Eleven.
Which means there are at least ten others."

Squirrel was quiet for a moment.

"Hand," he said.

Ray put his right hand in his jacket pocket, felt the contact of Squirrel's fingers
closing around the drive. He brought his hand back out empty.

"You're shaking," Squirrel said.

"I know."

"Are you hurt?"

"No." Ray looked at the fountain basin, which had been dry for long enough that
something had grown in it — a thin patchy green, the kind of life that moves
into empty spaces.

A pigeon landed at the edge of the basin. Ray watched it investigate the growth and leave.

"There was something else," Ray said. "Before I found the protocol. In the 2030 batch logs." He was quiet for a moment. "One word. An annotation on a standard yield record."

Squirrel set the newspaper down without folding it. He did not look at Ray.

"Jackpot." Ray looked at the heron. "A technician wrote it in a log from 2030. Whoever they were, they probably thought they were being clever — the batch had performed well, the annotation fit. They had no way of knowing it was my word. I never told anyone I'd said it. I said it in front of my locker at work, to nobody, when the cell read out on Day Eleven and I understood what I was looking at. Two hours later I was walking my department head through the data. The next morning I was carrying a box out of the building." He paused. "Seeing it in their records — that was when my hands started shaking. Not the walk out. Not the six blocks. That word in a log from 2030, used casually, by someone who had never heard of me." He was quiet. "They built their entire operation on my discovery. They silenced me and continued. And somewhere in year two, someone on their team had the same reflex I did — said the same word, the word I thought was mine, and wrote it down without knowing." He looked at his hands. "After that I understood the full shape of it."

The lining of his jacket shifted as he
moved, and Ray caught what he'd caught in Marbella and twice in Panama and did
not mention — the embroidered outline stitched against the inner fabric, dark on
dark, a wrench in gold thread barely visible in the crease. He looked at the
fountain.

"Disposal Protocol Eleven," Ray said. "What I read — what I had time to read,
which wasn't all of it — it's not just the stabilization process. It's not just
one product line or one research division. It's a supply chain decision. They're
managing the rate at which solutions reach the market. They have eleven viable
technologies at varying stages of deployment readiness, and they're holding
seven of them back until the price point and the market conditions meet specific
internal thresholds." He paused. "The energy crisis is an inventory decision."

"The mechanism runs on physics," Ray said. "Every time you break open a sealed cell to harvest the crystals — deplete it — ten percent of the Darker Matter vaporizes the instant the seal breaks. Escapes into atmosphere. Gone. There is no way to prevent it. It's a fixed cost of any depletion operation. Alpha's internal term for it is the depletion coefficient." He looked at the heron. "The Disposal Protocol destroys eleven to thirteen percent per batch. One to three points above the unavoidable baseline. Close enough to natural attrition to disappear into the expected waste column on any audit that isn't looking for the delta." He paused. "Seventeen months of records. Every quarter. They have been running this below the detection threshold since the program began."

Squirrel nodded.

"They didn't cause it on purpose," Ray said. "Not the original crisis. The
banking assault on the crypto grid did that — and the Polaris made it worse. But
once the shortage was established, once the infrastructure collapsed enough to
create dependency, they started managing the recovery. They decide how fast the lights
come back on. They decide when." He looked at the heron. "The world has been
sitting in the dark waiting for solutions that have been on a shelf in Panama
for years, and the reason they're on the shelf is because the timing isn't
profitable yet."

"That's going to be important," Squirrel said, "for what happens next."

"What happens next."

"The proof goes out. Through the parallel network — the same grey market your
FuelCell research has been circulating in for two years. Decentralized. No
single point of control for Alpha to suppress. It's going to reach about two
hundred thousand people in the first forty-eight hours." Squirrel paused. "After
that, it's not ours to manage."

Ray thought about two hundred thousand people. He thought about what they would do
with seventeen months of Disposal Protocol records and the names of eleven
technologies and the internal communications of a company that had decided how
fast humanity could come in from the cold.

He thought about the people in that building — colleagues who had been generous
with their time and their expertise, who had walked him through processes they
were proud of, who had no idea what was being done around them with the work
they contributed to.

"There are people in that building who had no idea what they were building," Ray
said.

Squirrel was quiet.

"I was in there long enough to know that. To be certain of it." Ray looked at
his hands. They had stopped shaking but he was watching them anyway. "They were
collegial. Genuinely helpful. The kind of people who enjoy their work and want
other people to enjoy it too." He paused. "They're going to come to work and
find out that someone used their trust as operational cover." He stopped. "That
does something to a person."

"It does," Squirrel said.

"I want you to understand that I know that." Ray looked up. "I didn't abstract
it. I knew, going in, what it would cost them. I knew and I went in anyway and I
got the drive and now I'm sitting here telling you that I know." He paused. "I
just need — I needed to say it to someone who would hear it."

Squirrel was quiet for a long moment. He picked up the newspaper. He set it back
down.

"I hear it," he said.

The pigeon came back to the fountain and left again. Two women went past on the
path behind them, talking in rapid Spanish about something domestic and urgent.
The afternoon had the quality of a city that didn't know anything had just
changed, which was accurate, because nothing had changed yet — the drive was in
Squirrel's pocket and the relay hadn't gone out and in eighteen hours the world
would still look like the same world it had been when Ray walked into a hardware
store in Casco Viejo under a name that wasn't his.

And then it wouldn't.

"I've been thinking about the flywheel since I left the building," Ray said.
"The curve." He turned the math over in the way he always turned the math over,
the way he had been turning it over since the oak table and the FuelCell and
the first data point that shouldn't have been possible. "Link the cells
together and hold them past the eleven-day window. Thirty-three days of
continuous linkage: ten times the internal yield, ongoing, as long as they
stay connected. You could monetize that for decades if you wanted to — and
they will want to."

He stopped.

"But the thing they're really afraid of isn't even in the drive yet," he said.
"Not the proof. Not the protocol records. The flywheel — and I can't prove the
flywheel. Not yet. The thirty-three-day number is solid; I pulled it straight
out of their own data. After that, their data just stops. But the curve was
still climbing when they quit measuring it, and I think if you keep the cells
linked long enough — and take them somewhere there's no gravity holding the
growth down — it doesn't just climb. It breaks. The cells start giving back on
their own, on a cycle, for as long as you leave them linked." He looked at his
hands. "If that's true — and I believe it is — you cannot manufacture a shortage
of something that produces its own abundance on a clock. Once people understand
that, the scarcity model has nowhere to go." He
paused.

"The official statement is that the shortage is structural," Ray said. "A supply problem — something the Federation and Alpha Corp are working to resolve together. Something almost resolved. That is what anyone following the public record has been told." He looked at the heron. "That is what people believe." He paused. "This was never a supply problem. It is a race to control the grid.
Alpha on one side. Everyone who knows what these cells actually are on the
other. They have been winning that race for a decade by making sure nobody
knows it is happening."

Squirrel was watching him.

"But the plateau," Ray said. "The ceiling on the growth curve — I can model
the mechanism, I can show the suppression records, I can tell you what I know
should happen in zero gravity. But I haven't run it. I haven't been able to run
it." He looked at the heron. "The plateau disappears in zero gravity — I'm
certain of it. The data points to it. But certainty without the experiment is
still a model. It's not proof."

He had a name for it now — the theory he couldn't prove from the ground. "The Antigravity project," he said. The flywheel in zero gravity. If the growth plateau disappeared without gravity holding it down, everything changed.

Squirrel was quiet for a moment.

"You've been running on that for a long time," Squirrel said.

"Yes."

"Ray."

He looked at Squirrel.

"There's someone else working on this theory. The zero-gravity variable." Squirrel
paused. "She's been running the same curve from a different direction. She has
data you don't have." He let that settle. "She's on her way here."

Ray looked at him.

"Here," he said.

Red leaned back on the bench. "You know, most people get extracted from a company that tried to kill them and they go somewhere quiet. You went back inside wearing a fake name and got promoted."

Squirrel, who had not smiled once during the entire Panama operation, did not smile now. "She has the notebooks. She has the zero-gravity data. She doesn't know what you found yet. Don't explain it to her — let her see the overlap herself."

"The transit house." Squirrel picked up the newspaper. "She's not from inside. She moves things." He paused. "Go."

Ray did not move immediately.

He sat with the heron for one more moment — the four-degree list, the empty
basin, the thin green growth at the edges. He thought about the colleagues in
the building. He thought about the proof in Squirrel's pocket. He thought about
a curve he had been running in his sleep for two years, and a woman he had never
met who had apparently been running the same one.

He stood up.

His hands were in his pockets. His pace, when he walked away from the bench, was the same unhurried
pace of a man who belonged somewhere — or who had learned well enough, across
enough time in enough cities, how to look like he did.

---

The transit house was three blocks into the market district, above a cell repair
shop with a handwritten hours sign taped to the inside of the glass. Red was
there — expected. The woman at the small table near the window was not.

She was younger than Ray would have imagined, though he hadn't known to expect
anything. Work jacket, nothing on it — no patches, no insignia, no indication
that she had recently come from an asteroid colony the Federation had flagged as
a religious extremist cult. Her own wire-bound notebook was open in front of
her, with the quality of a notebook that gets opened the moment its owner sits
down. She had a pen behind her ear.

A transit case sat on the table beside her.

"Jill Von Grutzen," Red said, and stepped back.

Ray looked at the name a moment — the way you look at something that lands slightly off-center from where you expected it.

"Von Grutzen," he said.

"Jill Von Grutzen," she said, setting the transit case on the table. She saw the look. "Yes, it's German. No, I don't want to talk about it. No Oktoberfest jokes. No Sound of Music. If you mention strudel I will leave."

"Yes." She said it without weight. It was a fact she had always carried: the Vergluds had changed their name, and the name they had changed it from was hers. The same root. A shared ancestor far enough back that it was more genealogy than biography. She was, distantly, related to Marcus Verguld. She had never advertised it. She had never hidden it. "Distant," she said. "Very distant. And they went one way and my family went another."

She looked at Ray. He looked at her. Neither of them seemed in a hurry.

She put her hand on the transit case and slid it across the table toward him.
"From Petra." She paused. "She said you'd know what to do with them."

Ray sat down. He opened the case.

Four notebooks. Cloth-covered, dark blue, worn at the corners from years of
daily handling — the kind of wear that only accumulates on something opened and
read and set down and picked up again, over years, by the same pair of hands.
A strip of tape along the spine of the first, penciled in block letters:
E. KLEIN — VOL. I.

He looked at it for a moment.

He looked up at Jill.

"I'll need a few hours," he said.

"I know," she said. "I've read them."

Red left them.

---

He read through the night.

The runs were numbered. Three hundred and sixty-nine of them — meticulous,
cross-referenced, dated in her block-letter hand. The 11-day cycle. The
negative pressure variable. The 33-day lock mechanic. The 180-day threshold.
All of it mapped, years before Alpha Corp had named any of it, years before he
had sealed a FuelCell in his locker on a Friday afternoon because he hadn't
felt like carrying it across campus.

She had been here first.

He turned pages. The clock moved past two, past three. He was somewhere in the
two hundreds when he noticed the handwriting in the final notebook had changed
— heavier at the down-strokes, a slight rightward lean at the margins. Not a
different hand. The same one, writing faster, or with more on her mind. He found
the transition entry and understood.

She had run a zero-gravity test.

One run. Unauthorized. A Directorate survey vessel during a provisioning window
— she had logged it as material stability documentation and the officer hadn't
read the form closely. She had sealed a cell, held it in zero gravity for the
full observation window, and documented what she saw.

The crystals had not plateaued.

In standard gravity they always hit a ceiling — growth rate increased but leveled
off. She had noted this across dozens of runs and always marked it as a design
constraint. In zero gravity, same cells, same environment, no other variable
changed: no ceiling. Crystals grew outward in every direction equally,
continuously, with no preferred geometry and no surface to stop them.

She wrote the result once. Then again in the margin, in a different ink.

She called it the zero condition.

Her final notation was a single line, set apart from the preceding data by a
half-page left blank:

The ceiling is gravity.

Ray sat with the case open on the table and Elsa Klein's four notebooks in his
hands. Outside, Panama City was moving through the gray hour before sunrise. The
market district below was shifting from the sounds of late night into the first
sounds of morning.

She had found it. Documented it. Left it for whoever came next — in four
notebooks worn at the corners from years of handling on an asteroid the
Federation had registered as a soap factory, and nobody in the institutional
science world had gone looking for them.

He had the Panama data. She had the zero-gravity confirmation.

He put the notebooks carefully back in the case. He closed it.

He sat.

---

Ray was awake before Jill. He lay still until the light at the edges of the
window blind was enough to read by, then got up and sat at the table with the
transit case and did not open it.

She woke forty minutes later. She looked at him from the cot, then at the case,
then at him again — the calibration of someone deciding what kind of morning
this was.

"Cafe," she said. It was not a question.

---

The market district had been going for hours by the time they found a table
outside. The street was already at full volume: a cart selling shaved coconut and
paper cups of something red, a man in a plastic chair listening to a radio with
one good speaker, two dogs sorting out a disagreement in the alley across the
way. The humidity was absolute. The awning above them provided shade and nothing
else.

They ordered café con leche and whatever the man inside said was ready, which
turned out to be hojaldre with eggs and a small clay dish of ají. Jill ate with
the efficiency of someone who had learned not to assume she would finish her
meal. Her notebook was open beside her plate. She was already writing.

Ray put his hand on the transit case.

"She found it in twelve minutes," he said. "One run on a provisioning window.
No clearance." He paused. "She was right."

"I know she was right."

"I mean I've been running the model. For months." He looked at the case. "The
plateau is a gravity effect. Not a material limit. Zero gravity removes it
entirely — crystals grow outward in every direction with no preferred geometry
and no ceiling." He paused. "I had the Panama data. The mechanism, the
suppression, the batch logs. What I didn't have was confirmation that the
zero-gravity variable changes everything. The notebooks are the confirmation."

Jill turned a page in her own notebook without reading it.

"Alpha Corp took the 11-day cycle and ran it every time," Ray said. "They
harvested. Reset. Harvested. Reset. They never locked long enough to find out
what was underneath the cycle, because the money was in the harvest. You can't
monetize the lock. You can only monetize the open." He paused. "Elsa locked it.
She just didn't have access to zero gravity at scale."

Red appeared at the edge of the awning. She pulled out the empty chair without
asking and sat down.

"One hour," she said. "Maybe less." She looked at both of them. "Alpha knows the
drive is gone. They identified the vector this morning. They have people in the
city."

Ray was still.

Jill closed her notebook.

"How long before they reach this district?" Ray said.

"One hour. Maybe less." Red looked at him. "Squirrel says the park. Now."

---

The market district was full by the time they moved through it — the morning
density of carts and foot traffic and the smell of something frying in oil a
few doors down. Ray walked with Jill two steps ahead, the transit case in his
hand. She matched his pace without adjusting to it, which told him something
about how much ground she had covered in her own life carrying things that
couldn't be left behind.

They reached the park from the east path. The heron was as Ray had left it:
four degrees, empty basin, the thin green at its edges catching the morning
light.

Squirrel was already there. He was not on the bench. He was standing near the
fountain, looking at it the way people look at things they are not really seeing
because they are thinking about something else. He looked up when they came.

He took them in — Ray, the case, Jill beside him — and nodded once, the small
precise acknowledgment of a man who has arranged a number of things that have
now arrived in the correct order.

"Station Libre," Squirrel said. "The Collective's orbital station. Zero gravity.
Properly equipped." He looked at Jill. "The Collective has been running it on
parts that should have been replaced years ago. That's a known problem. The
question is the funding."

"Taken care of," Jill said.

Squirrel looked at her.

"I own the colony infrastructure now," she said. "The FuelCell supply, the
gold windfall surplus. I'll upgrade it." She paused.
"I'll go with him."

Squirrel was quiet for a moment. He looked at Ray.

Ray looked at the heron. The 180-day curve. He had been running it in his sleep
for months. Elsa Klein had mapped the same problem from the ground floor up —
same variables, no way to test the ceiling. The curve needed zero gravity and
enough run time to document completely. He thought about a Directorate survey
vessel, twelve minutes, a window that wasn't hers, a woman writing the result twice
in two different inks.

He thought: we need to go up.

"We need to go up," Jill said.

"Yes," he said. "We do."

Squirrel looked at Ray for a long moment.

"Ray."

He looked at Squirrel.

"You can stop now," Squirrel said.

Ray sat with this.

He sat with it the way he sat with difficult data — patiently, without trying to
rush to the next thing, letting the information exist before he decided what it
meant.

You can stop now.

The equations were still running. Two years was long enough for a model to
become involuntary. He thought about his colleagues in the building — the ones
who had explained things freely, who had been generous in the way people are
generous with someone they believe is working toward the same thing. They had had
no idea what was being done around them. He had walked out with their work in his
pocket, gone six blocks with his hands shaking, and handed it over. That part was
finished. The part where he had sat across from them at lunch and said nothing —
that part he kept.

The Forsinko cover was finished. No more dead drops. No more careful reading of
badge logs and maintenance schedules and the learned rhythm of a building's
security rotation. What came next was open science — zero gravity, documented
runs, a station with parts that worked, two notebooks and a Panama archive and
a curve that was going to close the case on a decade of manufactured shortage.

What came next was open.

He felt, distantly, that he might cry. He was the kind of person who found this
annoying and also, when it happened, unavoidable. He pressed the side of his
thumb against his eye and looked at the heron — the beak still angled down at the basin as if the water
were coming, as it had been for however many years this park had been going on
without it — and he thought about all of the things he had been holding.

The FuelCell on the oak table. The notebook. The 33-day yield data. The curve
that climbed and would not stop. The colleagues in the building who would come in
today and find a different world than they had left. The drive. Elsa Klein's final notation,
one line, a half-page of white space before it, written by a woman on a survey
vessel with twelve minutes and enough courage to look directly at what she was
seeing.

He had been holding all of it.

He put it down.

Not carefully. Not in any particular order. He just — let it down. The way you
set something on a table when the room is right.

He breathed.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay."

Ray looked at the heron one more time. The feathers on the east-facing side of
the wing were more worn than the others — the side that had been taking the
lean, year after year, the weather working on it in a direction nobody had
noticed because nobody had come to fix the angle. It was going to keep tilting
into that. There was something in that he found companionable. He did not know
why. He decided he did not need to know why.

He looked at Jill. She was watching the heron too. She had the expression of
someone who has just arrived somewhere after a long route and is taking one
moment to note the landmark before moving on.

"Show me the Panama data," she said. "On the way."

He picked up the case.

They walked out of the park together, into the morning density of the market
district, in the direction of the relay point and whatever came after. Behind
them, Squirrel stood at the fountain for another moment — the still quality
of a man who has arranged the last piece of a long thing and is allowing himself
one quiet moment to register that the pieces are in place.

He folded the newspaper and left.

---

There were three days before the transit window to Station Libre opened. On the
first of them, Squirrel found Ray at the safe house and set a folded paper on the
table. "You asked me about Oren. Months ago." A pause. "You're leaving the planet
in three days. If you're going to do this, do it tonight, do it once, and don't go back." He looked at him. "We'll have someone on the street. If it turns,
you walk away, and it's the last we ever speak of it."

Vince's building was in El Cangrejo, a courtyard full of bird noise. Ray knocked.
The chain. The door, open the width of it.

Vince's face in the gap, older than it had been a week ago. "They told us you
weren't real. Mikov Forsinko. There's no such person. Security pulled your access
and asked us the same four questions all afternoon." Not angry — something worse.
Bewildered, a little afraid. The specific fear of a man who has just learned the
ground he stood on was painted on. "Who are you."

"My name is Max Vision. People call me Ray. I used to work where you work. I
found the thing you're about to find out about, and Alpha fired me and sent
someone to kill me for it, and the people who reached me first are the only
reason I'm standing here instead of in the ground." He kept his hands where Vince
could see them. "I lied to you for four and a half years. I liked you the whole
time. Both of those are true. I came to tell you the second one to your face —
before the first one becomes the only thing you remember about me."

Vince looked at him a long moment through the gap. Then the door closed — and Ray
heard the chain slide, and it opened again, all the way.

The apartment was a scientist's apartment: books in stacks that were really a
filing system, a table that had given up being a dining table, good coffee
equipment and nothing else good. Vince did not offer him any. He stood with his
arms crossed and let Ray talk, the way he had once let Ray talk through a
feedstock-purity model on the lab floor — the same attention, turned now on
something with no clean answer.

So Ray gave it to him plainly. Not as an accusation. As a briefing — the one
Vince had spent four and a half years being positioned never to receive.

The shortage was not real, not the way the world had been told. The science of
making the cells stable had been finished for years; Vince knew that better than
anyone, he ran it every day, eleven days at a time, like a man tending a clock.
What Vince had never been shown was the other column. Eleven to thirteen percent
of every batch the facility produced was destroyed on purpose — logged,
scheduled, deliberate. Calibrated to the point, to keep global supply a few
percent under demand, which kept the price where it needed to be, which kept the
winters cold and the factories dark and the relief funds flowing, year after
year, into a problem that had been solved and then carefully un-solved.

And the relief funds did not stay relief funds. Alpha had spent seven years
filing government grants — billions, drawn from crisis money — to develop a
technology they already had. Ray's technology. The recipe Vince had been
refining, reformatted and resubmitted as ongoing research, so the appearance of
the search could be funded long after the finding had been buried.

"You were the proof it was real research," Ray said. "Not because you were lying.
Because you weren't. You were the most honest man in that building, and that's
exactly what made you useful. A liar gets caught. A good scientist doing good
work, who has no idea what's happening two floors up — nobody catches him,
because there's nothing in him to catch. They built the whole thing so the people
doing the science would never be in the same room as the people doing the math."
He paused. "I read both columns. That's the only real difference between us. I got
into a drawer you were never meant to open."

Vince didn't say anything for a while. Ray watched him do the thing good
scientists do when the data won't fit the model: not deny it, not perform shock —
just sit with it and let it cost what it cost. His face changed the way a
landscape changes when a cloud moves off it. Nothing dramatic. Everything
different.

"The third-quarter numbers," Vince said finally. Quietly. "The ones we
celebrated. The ones I poured you a drink over." He looked at his hands. "I was
proud of those."

"I know. You should have been. The work was good. The work was always good." Ray
let that be true, because it was. "It just was never the point."

"Why are you telling me this," Vince said.

"Because somebody told me once. Seventy-two hours before it was too late." Ray
stood. "You don't decide tonight. There's a number. Someone answers." He set it
on the table — a handwritten sequence, no name. "I'm sorry, Vince. For the part
that was a lie. Not for the part that wasn't."

He left, and he did not look back up at the fourth-floor window. That was the
discipline of it: you make the offer, and you let the person be free to take it
or not. The not was theirs to keep.
