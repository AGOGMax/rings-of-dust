THE FIRST SPARK
Part Zero: The Von Grutzen Woman
Iceland, 1938


The sponsors arrived in three separate cars from Reykjavik, which was not how
Marta had organized it. She had arranged one vehicle — a supply truck, canvas
sides, adequate for the eight of them and the equipment. What arrived instead
was a black Horch with diplomatic plates someone had forgotten to remove, a
hired Citroën with a cracked windscreen, and a Land Rover that had been painted
matte gray, as though whoever owned it had anticipated needing camouflage in the
interior of Iceland.

She watched them come up the track from the ridge.

The track ran along the eastern edge of the Hengill geothermal field, past the
second of three collapsed craters, and ended at the flat shelf of frozen ground
where she had been standing for forty minutes. The sky was the color of old
pewter. The smell of sulfur had been in the air since Reykjavik — you stopped
noticing it and then you noticed it again. The cold was specific: a cold that
came up through the soles of your boots before it came down through your collar.

Marta had been here before. Twice, in the preceding seven years, on smaller
expeditions that had accomplished nothing except confirming what she already
believed. The first time she had come alone and stood at the entrance for half
an hour before she understood what she was feeling. The second time she had
brought one other person — Friedrich, who was dead now of causes unrelated to
the mine — and they had gone as far as the first chamber. That was enough to
know what was in there. That was more than enough.

She had spent the seven years since finding the people with enough left to fund
what the confirmation required.

This was not a straightforward task. The old structures were gone or going.
The families she approached had lost their countries, their titles, their legal
standing in the states that had swallowed their estates. What remained was
money — some of it hidden, some of it moved, some of it in forms that were
technically no longer legal tender but retained value in the specific economies
of people who understood what such things were worth. And belief. What remained
above all was belief: the specific, pressurized conviction of people who had
watched everything they were told was permanent dissolve in a generation, and
had concluded that the thing they had been missing was access to a source of
power that the visible structures of the world did not control.

They were not wrong. This was the part that Marta had difficulty explaining to
people who found her associations embarrassing.

The cars stopped. The doors opened.

Count Istvan Fekete came out of the Horch first, pulling his coat closed — a
wide man in his sixties, Hungarian, with the particular bearing of someone
accustomed to rooms that arranged themselves around him. He had lost his estate
in 1919, his title in 1920, and his country's particular version of the world
in 1921, and he had spent the intervening years with the unspent energy of a man
who had been told his whole life that the energy was the point. He believed in
the resonance because he had experienced it twice at gatherings in Vienna and
once, powerfully, at a ceremony in Bucharest that he had attended out of social
obligation and left three hours later in a state he still could not describe
precisely.

From the Citroën came Vera Sorokina — Russian, fifty-one, formerly of a family
that had owned eleven thousand acres in the Ural foothills — and beside her,
Reginald Croft, who was British, who had known Crowley, who had been a member
of three separate occult orders in the past twenty years and had the air of a
man who had found all of them somewhat disappointing. He was the only one of
them who had published anything — a monograph, privately printed, on the
relationship between telluric currents and what he called "energetic inheritance."
The monograph was specific in ways that had made Marta trust him despite
everything else about him.

The Land Rover disgorged three more: Baroness Eleonora Kessler of a Bavarian
line so old it predated the concept of Bavaria; a man named simply as Hartmann,
who was Marta's lawyer and had stopped understanding her years ago but continued
to serve her out of a loyalty whose origin even he could not fully account for;
and Pieter Van den Berg, a Dutchman with industrial money in shipping who did
not believe in any of it and had invested because Marta had shown him enough
to make the not-believing feel like a position he could no longer entirely
defend.

The researchers had arrived the day before. They were already at the entrance.
There were nine of them: three geologists, two physicists, a chemist, a
historian of Icelandic volcanic formations, and two men whose exact credentials
Marta had not verified because their other qualifications were more relevant.
They had equipment: recording devices, measuring instruments, two cameras, and
a set of containment vessels made of a ceramic compound that a physicist at the
University of Berlin had designed based on specifications Marta had provided.
He had not asked where the specifications came from. Marta had not explained.

"It is cold," said Fekete, arriving at her side.

"Yes," said Marta.

"I understood Iceland to be cold. I was not prepared for this specific cold."

"You have been standing outside for forty seconds."

He made a sound that was not quite agreement. He looked at the mine entrance —
a low horizontal opening in the basalt face, reinforced with timber beams that
the first expedition team had installed in the spring. The lamps inside were
already lit. You could see the warm yellow edge of the light from where they
stood.

"Tonight," he said.

"Tonight."

The ritual required the full moon, which would arrive at half past eleven.
The blood of a firstborn calf — they had brought one from a farm outside
Reykjavik, a transaction that had required Hartmann's help with the paperwork —
and mirrors arranged in a specific configuration that Marta had inherited from
her mother, who had inherited it from her mother's mother, who had been the
first of the line to understand what it was that the family carried.

The mirrors were for the resonance, not for the reflection. This was the thing
that people with no education in such matters consistently misunderstood. The
mirror was not a symbol. It was a functional component. The resonance moved
through reflected light in a way that it did not move through direct light, for
reasons that Croft's monograph had begun to articulate and that Marta suspected
the physicists she had hired would confirm within the next seventy-two hours,
if the entry went as she anticipated.

The belief was not a metaphor.

She had spent a great deal of her life explaining this to people. She had given
up explaining it to most of them. What she had not given up was the work itself.

---

The ceremony was held on the flat ground forty meters south of the entrance,
in a depression that the terrain formed naturally, as though the earth had
prepared it. The moon came up exactly when Marta had calculated it would. She
had been calculating moon tables for thirty years. This was not mysticism. It
was astronomy.

The calf was dispatched cleanly. Marta had hired a man from the farm who knew
how. The blood was caught in a ceramic vessel — one of the containment vessels
repurposed for the night's work. The mirrors were placed according to the
inherited configuration: seven of them, in a pattern that Marta's great-
grandmother had first documented in a journal that Marta now kept in a bank
vault in Zurich, its pages handled only with cotton gloves, its contents
memorized by Marta in their entirety when she was fourteen years old.

The sponsors stood at the edge of the configuration.

Sorokina had closed her eyes before the blood component and had not opened them.
Croft was watching everything with the concentrated attention of a man filing
observations. Fekete was still. The Baroness Kessler had her hands folded in
front of her and her expression was the expression of a woman who has waited
a long time for this specific moment and is choosing to receive it without
display. Van den Berg was standing slightly outside the formation and looking
at the ground and Marta had decided to let him.

The resonance arrived the way it always arrived: not as sound, not as sensation
exactly, but as a quality of attention — as though the air itself was listening.
Then something shifted in the way the moonlight fell on the mirrors. Not the
angle. Not the reflection. Something in the texture of the light, in the way
it arrived on the far surface of each mirror and continued somewhere that was
not geometrically possible.

The calf blood in the vessel began to produce a very faint heat.

Marta knew this. She had seen it before. The others had not.

She heard Sorokina draw a breath.

She heard Van den Berg say, in Dutch, a word that translated approximately to
that's not.

The resonance lasted six minutes. Marta had timed it before. It always lasted
six minutes at this proximity, on a clear night, at the full moon. She believed
that the six-minute duration was not a property of the ritual but a property
of the source — that the source was cycling on a schedule she had not yet
fully determined, and that what the ritual accomplished was simply to make
the cycle perceptible to human senses that were not otherwise calibrated for it.

When it ended, no one spoke for a moment.

Then Fekete said: "That is what you have been trying to show us."

"Yes," said Marta.

"And inside the mine—"

"It is continuous in there. Not cyclic. The six minutes is what leaks out."

She watched him process this.

"You have been inside," he said.

"As far as the first chamber. Yes."

"And you came out."

"Evidently." She picked up the vessel. The blood had cooled. "The researchers
go in at first light. We will have preliminary data within twelve hours. What
they find — what they measure and document and bring out — will determine what
we build."

"What we build," the Baroness Kessler repeated. Not a question. A recognition.

"The ones who go in first," said Croft. "What will happen to them."

Marta looked at him. He was asking the thing the others were not asking, which
was the thing she would have asked in his position. This was why she had included
him.

"I don't know," she said. "I know what happened to Friedrich. I know what
happened to the survey team that entered in 1929, before I became involved. I
know what the data from those entries looked like." She put the vessel down.
"I know what we are trying to build. The entry is the cost of the confirmation.
The researchers understand this."

"Do they," Croft said.

"They were told what I know. They chose to come anyway. That is a kind of
understanding."

He considered this. He did not appear satisfied. He also did not object
further, which Marta had learned to accept as the equivalent of agreement from
a man like Croft.

---

First light came at half past four.

The researchers were at the entrance already, equipment loaded, lamps
checked. Their team lead was a geologist named Arne Sigurdsson who had
worked Icelandic formations for twenty years and who had a quality of
specifically Icelandic pragmatism — an indifference to the dramatic — that
Marta found useful. He did not ask unnecessary questions. He checked his
instruments, confirmed his team was ready, and looked at Marta.

"We will send signal on the hour," he said.

"Yes."

"If the signal stops—"

"Then it stops," she said. "Document everything until it does."

He nodded. It was the nod of a man who had made his calculation and settled
it. He turned and spoke briefly to his team in Icelandic. Two of them
responded. One of them laughed at something, quietly, the way you laugh when
the laugh is more for yourself than anyone else.

They went in.

Marta watched the last of them pass the threshold — the timber frame, the
darkness beyond the lamp-line, the specific quality of the air at the entrance
that she had noticed both times she had stood here before. Not cold, exactly.
Not warm. Something in between that was not temperature. A pressure at a
frequency the body recognized before the mind could name it.

She stood at the entrance for a long time after they were gone.

The sponsors were at the vehicles, most of them. Croft was at her shoulder,
ten feet back, watching as she was watching. Hartmann had opened a thermos and
was pouring coffee into a cup and extending it toward no one in particular.
Van den Berg had his hands in his pockets and was looking at a piece of volcanic
rock he had picked up from the ground, turning it over.

The lamp-light from inside the entrance dimmed and brightened as the researchers
moved deeper. Then it dimmed and did not brighten again.

They were past the first chamber now.

Marta put her hand flat on the timber frame of the entrance. The wood was cold.
Through the cold, very faintly, at the edge of perception — the resonance. Not
the cyclic six-minute version. The continuous version. The source.

She had located it. She had organized the means to reach it. She had stood here
twice before and she stood here now and she would stand here again when they
came out with what she had sent them in for.

She had spent thirty years building toward this morning.

Her great-grandmother had spent her whole life building toward a version of it
that hadn't had the mathematics.

The line does not break, Marta thought. It passes forward. You pour yourself
into it and it carries you whether you are present to witness it or not. This
is the mechanic. This is the real thing underneath the ritual and the mirrors
and the blood — the blood is to make contact, the mirrors are to make it
perceptible, but the thing itself is older than both. The thing itself is
simply: what you build continues. The structures you leave outlast the body
that built them. What you pour forward does not stop when you stop.

Her grandmother had known this.

Her mother had known it and not known what to do with it.

Marta had spent her whole life learning what to do with it.

Inside the mine, the lamp-light was gone.

She took her hand from the timber.

She turned around.

"Coffee," she said to Hartmann.

He poured her a cup.

She stood with it and faced the entrance and did not move.

She would wait here until they came back. She had waited for longer.
