RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Thirteen

The Faithful

The fifth day. She had arrived with her cells. She had not left.

She told herself it was the docking bay's proximity to the main tunnel — easier to reach the ship quickly if she needed to. She told herself it was the food, which was still real and which she was not done eating. She told herself she was running preliminary diagnostics on the Hound's Tooth before the return run to Titan, which was true in the limited sense that she had opened the diagnostic panel and then sat looking at it without actually running anything.

She was staying because on the fourth night Tyler Durden had sat across from her in the kitchen and talked for two hours, and she had listened, and when she went back to her ship she had not been able to sleep.

Not because she believed him. Because she couldn't find where he was wrong.

---

The woman who had been easiest to talk to was Yun. She had started saving Jill a place at the cable runs, which was the colony's version of friendship.

"You don't think it's strange," Jill said. They were in the work bay, running cable. The Void-Walker installations — the rigging at the east end — required regular maintenance, and everyone rotated through it. The cable was
heavy-gauge and stiff in the cold — it fought the bends, and Jill's fingers were
numb at the tips from feeding it through the conduit clips. The rigging at the
far end hummed at a frequency she could feel in the fillings of her back teeth.

"Which part," Yun said.

"Any of it. All of it. The sending things into a void and waiting for gold."

Yun looked at her for a moment. "Do you think it's strange that the cells sync on an eleven-day clock? That one of seven hundred and seventy-seven always has the Jackpot on it?"

"That's mechanics."

"It's mechanics we can't fully explain," Yun said. "Tyler says the line between mechanics and miracle is just the distance between what you've measured and what you haven't yet."

Jill had heard versions of this argument before. Usually they led somewhere she didn't want to go. "And the gold."

"And the gold," Yun said. "Which is also mechanics. Which Elsa mapped." She set down the cable spool. "The void at the end of the east tunnel has a specific gravitational signature. When a stabilized cell crosses the threshold where the gravity lets go, the Darker Matter interacts with the void — redirects inbound asteroid traffic along a vector Elsa calculated forty years ago. It's in her notebooks."

"So it's physics," Jill said. "Not faith."

"Both," Yun said. "Elsa understood the physics. Tyler understood what the physics was for." She picked up the spool again. "The cells are real. The gold is real. The question Tyler asks is: why. Why does the void respond this way to this specific material? Why eleven days? Why always one in seven hundred and seventy-seven?"

"Nobody knows."

"Tyler thinks the cells are in a conversation with something," Yun said. "The void, the ancestors, the chain of who we came from and who we'll become. He thinks the eleven-day clock isn't random. He thinks it's a heartbeat."

Jill was quiet.

"You don't have to believe it," Yun said. "You just have to notice that the gold keeps showing up."

---

Tyler took her to the preparation chamber on the fifth afternoon.

It was a room she hadn't seen yet — small, off the main tunnel, lit differently from the rest of the colony. Not surgical-white like the lab spaces. Warmer. The kind of light you associate with things that are meant to be seen slowly.

Four FuelCells were racked along one wall. Not ordinary units — each one a
Jackpot cell, the one-in-seven-forty that had produced the rare exterior crystal
deposit on its eleventh day. One chance. One cell. These were the four best the
colony had ever made, and their single exterior deposits were dense enough that
each cell was worth more than anything Jill had ever carried in her cargo hold. The crystals caught the amber light and threw it back in
small fractured specks across the ceiling. The cells hummed together — a low
concordance that sat in the chest the way the sync resonance had sat in hers on
the first day. The air near them was faintly warm and smelled like mineral and
something electrical, like the air before a storm that hasn't decided whether to
arrive.

Tyler stood beside them the way you stand beside something you're about to part with.

"We don't sacrifice every Jackpot," he said. "We choose. The best ones. The ones that have been running the longest and producing the most." He looked at her. "You're thinking we're throwing away value."

"You are throwing away value," she said.

"We're giving it back." He touched one of the cells — not possessively. The way you touch something before releasing it. "Elsa believed that energy doesn't disappear. It moves. The Darker Matter goes into the void, the gold comes from the void, the cells keep producing. Something on the other side of the exchange is receiving what we send." He paused. "She believed that people work the same way. That the energy of a life — everything you built, everything you gave — doesn't disappear when you die. It flows forward. Into your line. Into the people who come after you."

Jill looked at the cells. "She believed she'd come back."

"She believed all of us come back," Tyler said. "Into our own bloodlines, if we've given enough. Into the people we love. Into the hands of whoever is willing to continue the work." He was quiet for a moment. "She's buried forty meters from where we're standing. She came out here and built something and died in it. I never met her. But I've read every word she wrote, and I understand why she came out here instead of staying where she was."

"Where was she before?"

"Working inside the system that became the machine. She left before it had a name," he said. "She came here. She spent the last eleven years of her life building an understanding of the cells that nobody inside those walls ever achieved." He looked at Jill. "She gave everything she knew to the void. The notebooks she left behind are what we still have."

Jill looked at the four cells, their crusted exteriors, the Darker Matter slow-growing in the amber light.

"When is the ceremony," she said.

"Two days," he said.

She did not ask why she was being told this. He did not explain.

---

She asked Petra about the notebooks that evening.

Petra was at her bench, logging the day's readings. The bench was lit by a single
adjustable lamp — the kind with a brass neck that someone had bent to exactly the
right angle years ago and no one had moved since. The pen scratched across the
page in a rhythm that suggested Petra had been writing these logs long enough
that her hand no longer consulted her mind about the motion. She did not look up.

"No," she said.

"I'm not asking to keep them."

"I know what you're asking." Petra set down her pen. "You want to understand if the physics in those notebooks is real enough to explain the gold, and if it is, you want to know whether that changes your opinion of what Tyler is doing with it."

Jill waited.

"The physics are real," Petra said. "Elsa was the most rigorous scientist I have ever worked beside. Every number in those notebooks is correct. The void's gravitational signature, the cell interaction, the eleven-day redirect — all of it verifiable, all of it exact." She picked up her pen again. "The other parts of the notebooks are not something I show to people who are leaving."

"What other parts."

Petra looked at her directly for the first time. "The parts that are harder to verify," she said. "The parts that she wrote at three in the morning after she lost her parents and her colleagues stopped speaking to her and she had been out here long enough to stop needing their approval." She held Jill's eyes for a moment. "The parts about why she came here. What she believed the cells were. What she believed happened when you gave something you couldn't afford to lose."

She looked back at her readings. The pen moved across the page in strokes that were unhurried and exact.

"Tyler arrived four and a half years ago," Petra said. Not a change of subject — a continuation. "Alone. He had a pack and a commercial FuelCell — the first any of us had seen. Nothing else. No tools. No provisions. No plan beyond getting here."

"Why here?"

Petra's pen stopped. She did not look up, but the quality of her attention changed — the way a room changes when someone in it decides to be present instead of occupied.

"His wife died," Petra said. "Her name was Marla Singer. I don't know how. He has never said and I have never asked. What I know is that he arrived at this colony carrying the kind of grief that has already burned through everything flammable and is now just heat with nowhere to go." She resumed writing. "He had Elsa's teachings — not the science, the belief. The idea that what you give does not end. That it goes somewhere. That the void receives."

"He came here to grieve."

"He came here to find a framework in which his wife's death was not the final sentence." Petra's pen moved across the page. "The colony had stopped sacrificing by then. Elsa was dead. The mine was dry. The notebooks were sealed. The eleven of us who remained had the math and the memory and nothing to carry down the tunnel. We had become, in the Federation's language, a manufacturing outpost. In our own language, we had become people who remembered something we could no longer do."

She set the pen down.

"Tyler had the FuelCell. He studied the notebooks. Then he asked me if the east tunnel was still accessible. I said yes. He asked me if anyone would stop him. I said no. He asked me if I thought it would work."

"What did you say?"

"I said I thought Elsa would have tried it." Petra looked at Jill. "He walked the cell down the tunnel that evening. I watched him go. He did not ask anyone to come with him. He did not announce it. He just went."

"And?"

"Eleven days later, a gold asteroid appeared on the tracking screen. Twenty meters across. Same vector as all of Elsa's documented returns." Petra picked up the pen. "Tyler was in the tracking room when it appeared. He looked at the screen for a long time. Then he sat on the floor with his back against the wall and he did not move for an hour. I brought him water. He did not drink it."

She wrote another line.

"The second sacrifice was the next day. He had a second cell. He walked it down the tunnel the same way — alone, no announcement. Eleven days later, another asteroid. He has not missed since." A pause. "Every time he walks down that tunnel, he is thinking about Marla. The belief came after. The grief came first. I want you to understand that about him before the ceremony."

She looked back at her readings.

"When you come back," she said, "ask me again."

Jill opened her mouth and then closed it.

She went to her ship. The pilot's seat was cold — the Hound's Tooth had been
sitting powered-down for five days and the seat leather had taken on the
temperature of the rock around it. She sat in the dark. The instrument panel was
off. The only light was the amber glow leaking in through the bay door seal. She
could hear her own breathing, and beyond it, the faint hum of the colony
settling into its night cycle, and beyond that, nothing.

She sat there for a long time.
