RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Twenty-Three

The Top Floor

Vance sat in the parking lot of Alpha Corp's Los Angeles tower for twenty minutes before he went in.

That part mattered. Not to Marcus. Not to Alpha Corp. To nobody who would be in that room. But he sat there, and before he went in he thought about the hundred steps down into the dark, and the mold smell, and the clerk's face when she said his vault was emptied last month with the specific neutrality of someone who understood that the paperwork was more interesting than the people in it.

He thought about DeMint's face on the day he'd taken the assignment. A man who trusted the wrong person in the right way for the wrong reasons.

He thought about eight years, and what they had been for, and whether any of it counted.

He decided it did.

He went in.

The facade was dark glass and flat planes — the rest of the building unlit. The windows on the top floor beamed like a lighthouse. Vance pushed through the front doors, nodded to the night security on his way through, and rode the elevator to the fourteenth floor.

The corridor was long and low-lit, the carpet the gray of a place designed to be forgotten. No signage. The suite at the far end had a door that was not quite closed — a thread of television light running along the floor where it met the frame. Vance walked the corridor and pushed through it.

The television wall was running — dozens of screens, different feeds, the reflected light flickering in patterns across the face of the man in the chair. The chair was enormous, swiveled to face the screens, tall-backed, the kind of chair that was not quite a throne because Marcus had enough taste not to need a throne, but that communicated everything a throne would have communicated. He did not turn when Vance came in.

"How was the desert?" Marcus said.

Vance stopped. He had calibrated, on the drive back, for several versions of this conversation. He had not calibrated for Marcus already knowing. "You know why I went."

"I know why you went. I know what you found." Marcus still had not turned. "I know you went in a truck you signed out under your own name. I know you spent eleven minutes in the Hall of Records. I know what the clerk said to you." A pause. "I know you called DeMint's office from the drive back and were told he wasn't available."

Vance did not move. "The vault was emptied last month. Your trucks."

"My trucks," Marcus agreed, as though confirming a minor point.

"The iRIS work is gone. Conrad's prototype research is gone. Eight years of sealed pre-production records." Vance took two steps toward the desk. "I know what that equipment was for. I know what it can do to the crew on that ship. I have enough to court-martial you and Alpha both — out of what's already in the public record. I don't need the vault."

"Since when," Marcus said, "do you make decisions for me."

He turned.

He was not angry. He was not theatrical. He was the version of Marcus that people who only knew the public version of Marcus never saw — the version that did not perform, because it did not need to. The television light moved across his face in slow patterns.

"You were very useful," he said. "For a long time. A man in Vance Black's position, at Vance Black's access level, who brings me the right information at the right time — there is real value in that." He set his elbows on the desk. "But useful is conditional. And conditional things are not permanent."

Vance said: "You have a problem. The compound's effects on the crew are going to surface before Saturn. If Cole Nova—"

"Cole Nova is going to die at Saturn."

The room was very quiet.

Vance said nothing.

"The mission is not to harvest DarkX," Marcus said. "The mission ends with the Golden Dawn going dark at Saturn's rings. Taylor knows his job. The crew serves the job until the job is done. Cole Nova's job is to die in it. Neatly. The Polaris file closes. The Rapture hands twenty years of energy research exclusively to Alpha — and his death puts a sympathetic final paragraph on all of it. After the Rapture, the ring plane seals. Whatever is inside those rings stays inside them — unreachable, untouchable — for the better part of a generation. The Golden Dawn goes dark inside a crime scene that cannot be examined for twenty years." He said it without volume, without ceremony, with the unhurriedness of a man confident in the arithmetic. "You know this. You've known most of it for two years. What changed?"

Vance said: "Conrad."

A pause.

"Conrad's dead."

"I don't think he is." Vance moved to the edge of the desk. "I think he got something out before Alpha picked him up. I think whatever he got out is on that ship. And I think the reason your vault got cleaned is that someone in your structure doesn't want me to find out what Conrad's prototype can do to iRIS when it's close enough to DarkX."

Marcus looked at him for a long moment.

Then he smiled — just slightly, just at the corners — the way a man smiles when he realizes the problem is going to be simpler than he thought.

"I appreciate the hypothesis," he said.

"I'm not done—"

"Pull out your phone, Vance."

"Excuse me?"

"Your phone. Pull it out."

Vance did not reach for his phone. He heard the sound instead — the specific mechanical click of a revolver's hammer being drawn back under a desk. Not dramatic. Not announced. Just: a sound, and then the understanding of what it meant.

He braced himself. He had known, somewhere below his working knowledge of how this would go, that there was a version of this conversation that ended here. He had decided, in the parking lot before he came in, that knowing that was not sufficient reason to not come in.

Marcus took aim at the center of his chest and squeezed the trigger once. The shot came fast, without preface, in the middle of a sentence Vance had been thinking about completing.

He doubled over. His legs gave way. The floor came up to meet him and he let it. The filing cabinet was very solid against his back. The ceiling tiles were an inoffensive beige, the choice of someone who had never had to look at them.

Marcus stood and came around the desk. He looked at Vance with an expression that was not satisfaction and was not grief and was something close to mild professional curiosity.

"You were going to keep being right," Marcus said. "That's the unfortunate part."

Vance looked up at him. He had, in the last several seconds, arrived at a specific clarity that he understood would not last. "You have no soul," he said. It was not an accusation. It was a statement of fact. The fact, in this moment, was not satisfying, but it was accurate, and he had always preferred accurate.

Marcus set the revolver to Vance's forehead and pulled the trigger once more.

---

He stood in the quiet office for a moment, the television light still moving, and pulled out his phone.

He ran through the expression three times before he dialed — blank face, then the eyes going liquid, then the catch in the voice, then releasing. He ran it again. Better.

He placed the call.

"Yeah — hi?" The voice came out fractured and high and very convincing. "This is Marcus Verguld at Alpha Corp? There's — there's been a shooting, and I don't — oh my gosh, please hurry."

He reported the intruder. He reported the threat. He reported fearing for his life, which had not for a moment been true, and did so in the specific register of a man to whom fear was a sound he could produce without feeling.

When he hung up he let the expression go.

He sat on the edge of his desk in the quiet and looked at nothing for a moment.

Then he started laughing. The laughter built slowly and became genuine and finally bent him double, hugging his ribs when it started to hurt. It was the best he had felt all week.

He sat with it until it was done. Then he straightened, smoothed his shirt, and went back to the screens.

There was still a great deal of work to do.

---

The building was in Los Angeles.

This was not where Emily had expected it to be. She had expected something
offshore, something in a country without convenient jurisdiction, something that
matched the scale of what she had been dismantling for the last two months. But
Rachel, who had been precise about everything else, had said: Los Angeles. A
converted office building in the tech corridor west of downtown. Alpha's
primary domestic operations center, which ran under a subsidiary name that
appeared in no public registry. The kind of building that looked, from the
street, like any other glass-and-steel tenant floor.

The kind of building that a fifteen-year relationship between Alpha and the
Federation Council had kept invisible.

The DeMint task force had been in position since four in the morning. Six agents
in civilian clothes, two in Federation uniform, Red coordinating with
DeMint's team by comms from a vehicle a block east. Rachel had arranged her
own people on the perimeter — Emily did not know how many, and Sommi had stopped
explaining things she didn't need to know for operational reasons she trusted
implicitly now.

Emily was in the lobby.

She had been in the lobby for eight minutes. The lobby had a security desk and a
building directory and a coffee cart that had not opened yet. The directory
listed tenants by floor number and suite letter but not by name. Emily had
counted fourteen floors. She knew which floor she was going to.

Sommi had handled Marcus's assistant. Emily did not know the details — Sommi had
described it in three sentences at the motel the night before, each sentence
shorter than the last, and when she had asked a follow-up he had said "That's
the brief" and gone back to checking the comms relay. What she understood was
that the assistant had cooperated, that the cooperation had required a difficult
conversation, and that Sommi had conducted it with the clipped efficiency of
a man who does not enjoy the work but does not leave it undone. The access code
Emily used to enter the building came from that conversation.

The elevator opened.

She got in.

---

The fourteenth floor was not what it looked like from the street.

The corridor was long and low-lit, the carpet the exact shade of gray that
said we chose this color to be invisible. The air had the quality of filtered
recycled air that had been filtered and recycled for years — not unpleasant,
just neutral, the smell of a place that had been designed to have no smell. The
suite at the far end of the corridor had no signage.

Marcus was inside.

She knew this because Sommi had confirmed it twelve minutes ago from the
vehicle-mounted thermal scanner that the Collective team had repositioned at
three-thirty.

This was not unusual. The task force had flagged it as evidence that Marcus was
aware something was moving — he had retreated to a secure location, which meant
he had information and was evaluating it here. He had not tried to run.

Emily thought about this as she walked the corridor. Marcus did not run. Marcus
had been the most powerful civilian in the Federation's unofficial hierarchy for
fifteen years, and powerful men of a certain kind did not run. They assessed.
They adapted. They made calls. They had contingencies for contingencies.

He had not, Emily thought, made a contingency for her.

She used the access code.

The door opened.

---

The suite was empty.

She stood in the doorway for three seconds. The desk was there. A tablet,
face-down, on the surface. A chair pushed back the precise distance of a man who
had stood up without rushing. The window faced east, and the early morning light
came through it in the hard angular way of a sun that hadn't fully risen, turning
the empty chair precise against the glass.

He had been here. The air still had the quality of a room a person had recently
been in.

Emily looked at the tablet. She did not touch it.

She took out her radio.

"He's gone," she said. "Fourteenth floor, east suite. He was here. He's not here
now."

A pause. Then Sommi's voice: "Secondary exit flagged on the east side, sub-level.
Red is on it."

Emily stood in the empty room. She looked at the chair. She looked at the window.
The streets below were beginning to animate with the first deliveries, the lights
of buildings coming on one by one as the sun came up.

She said: "He's not running."

Sommi said: "I know."

"He's making a call."

"I know."

---

She was in the lobby when DeMint's coordinator's phone rang.

The coordinator — a man named Yuen, who had the quality of someone who had been
in coordination rooms for twenty years and had stopped being surprised by
anything — looked at the screen. He looked at Emily.

"It's for you," he said.

He put it on speaker and set it on the lobby desk.

The line was clear. No static, no compression — close, or using something very
good.

"Emily," Marcus said.

She looked at the phone. Sommi was three feet away, watching the street. Yuen
had stepped back.

"Marcus," she said.

"I know DeMint's task force has been in position since four." His voice had the
particular quality it always had — controlled, unhurried. "I know the proof from
Panama went into the parallel network forty-seven hours ago. It's been in about
two hundred thousand people's hands since yesterday morning." A pause. "Your
scientist is thorough."

"Where are you."

"Somewhere quiet." A pause. "I'll be in a position to be found when I'm ready to
be found. Which will be before the preliminary hearing. I'm not going to make
DeMint chase me around the planet. I'm just not going to let him dictate the
terms of my first conversation with his counsel."

"You're going to cooperate."

"I'm going to give a complete account." He said it the way he said most things —
without warmth, but without evasion. "Not because I've had a change of heart. I
want to be clear about that. I'm not contrite. I'm realistic. The machine is
done, and a complete account is the only leverage I have left. The officers who
took specific directives — I can name them. The procurement lines — I can
document them. The Alpha side of the flywheel arrangement — I have records going
back eight years. DeMint will want those. Rachel will want the ones that go
further."

"You understand she'll use them."

"Of course she will. That's what Rachel does. That's what I always did. The
difference between Rachel and me is that the thing she's building has a better
design than the thing I built." A pause. "I should have seen that sooner."

He said it the way a person says something they have already arrived at privately
— the sound of a conclusion being stated aloud for the first time. He had been
there for a while.

"Your brother," Marcus said. "Is he alive?"

"Yes."

"The woman. Sharon Teez."

"Yes."

The pause that followed was brief. The kind that closes an account rather than
opens one.

"Chen," he said. "My assistant. She'll be protected?"

"Yes."

"Good." He was quiet for a moment. "When your brother gets back — tell him he
was the right choice. Not for the reasons I chose him. For the reasons it turned
out he was."

The line ended.

---

Yuen had already moved to the other side of the lobby, speaking quietly into his
own phone. The building was being worked, floor by floor — evidence teams going
through rooms he had left prepared for them. He had known they were coming. He
had left everything they would need.

"He'll surface before the hearing," Emily said.

"Yes," Sommi said.

"He was never going to run."

"No."

She thought about the proof from Panama, in two hundred thousand people's hands.
She thought about Rachel spending fifteen years building something inside the
machine's walls, using its blind spots, placing people in its joints. She thought
about Sommi in a canvas jacket — a small wrench emblem stitched in dark thread
above the left breast pocket, nearly invisible unless you knew to look.

She thought about all the people who had been choosing, one at a time, to build a
different thing.

She thought about Sharon somewhere above the atmosphere, on a ship she hadn't
seen, looking at the same planet from the other side of it. There you are. — S.
Four words routed through the Meridian, ninety minutes old by the time they
arrived. The most precise thing anyone had said to her in months.

"What do we do now," Emily said.

"There's a flight to San Diego at eleven." Sommi looked at her. "Your brother
lands in six weeks, give or take the retrieval window."

Emily looked at the street.

Six weeks felt, at this particular moment, like the right amount of time. Long
enough to breathe. Long enough to stop moving and let the moving catch up with
her. Long enough to sit somewhere quiet and think about all of it — the pier,
the desert, the motel in Barstow, the eleven hundred miles, the burner phone,
the road atlas.

Emily stood in the lobby. The building came apart around her, floor by floor — quietly, properly, with the weight of evidence that had been building since Conrad disappeared from a keynote in 2033.

She had been close enough to it long enough to see the shape of it. Alpha Corp didn't destroy people carelessly. It found the best ones — the ones with the specific capability it needed — and it inverted them. Conrad, built into someone who designed a system of control. Cole, conscripted to fly a mission designed to end with him dying in it. Sharon, turned into something that could survive without surviving. The shape was always the same: find the exceptional, exploit the capability, discard or reprogram the ones who wouldn't break clean. She had watched it from close enough to smell it. She had not broken clean. She had broken differently.

She thought: you're stronger than you think.

She thought: I know. I know I am.

She had not believed it, yet, when she had thought it in a gas station east of
Indio. She had been working toward believing it.

She believed it now.

She picked up her bag.

She walked through the lobby doors into the morning.
