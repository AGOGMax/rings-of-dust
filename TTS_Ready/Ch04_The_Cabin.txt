RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Four

The Cabin

The morning light filtered through the tall pines. Cole hefted his old ax over
his head and split the last log round. The splitting crack was subdued in the
early spring moisture. His breath clouded in the crisp air. He paused,
straightening to scan the treeline. The low sun peeked over the granite peak
just east of his cabin. He rubbed his hands together, let out a sudden and
glorious howl, and continued to the comfort of home.

The door creaked open, and Bitboy groaned. The firelight flickered on his brown
and white fur as he scurried over to Cole. His mechanical limbs hummed softly as
he reached around Cole's thighs, sighing in obvious discomfort. One arm tugged
at Cole's sleeve, guiding him to the warmth.

Cole knelt by the fireplace and added a freshly cut log. The sap boiled and
hissed until the wood was dry enough to burn. Cole's fingers brushed against
Bitboy's fur.

"You little bastard," Cole said. "What do you have?"

As if dropping a one-gallon glass bottle of ethanol next to an open fire would
obliterate his wrongdoing, Bitboy did just that: rolled on his back and forced
all six limbs into the air like a dead bug. With a brilliant puff, Cole's
expectation of a relaxing day went up in flames, along with the lingering fumes
from a well-finished bottle that should have lasted the damned monkey at least a
few days. The smell was unmistakable — raw ethanol and something sour underneath
it, the sour reek of a body processing fuel it wasn't designed for.
Bitboy's fur was damp at the muzzle and his mechanical limbs ticked faintly, the
servos cycling at uneven intervals.

Cole blamed the Federation.

Meanwhile, the world outside grappled with a crisis of its own. The airwaves
carried the manufactured accent belonging only to news anchors.

"Good morning, California. This is Rachel Vayes for Public Square News. The
energy crisis, which has left millions struggling from resource scarcity and
power outages, may finally be seeing a solution. During the opening day of
Defying Gravity 2033: Innovations in Combating the Energy Crisis, the tech titan
Alpha Corporation promised a long-term solution. Although initial
disappointments stemmed from the keynote speaker's disappearance, Alpha defied
the gravity of the situation by announcing that new energy-efficient implants
would be available on a subscription basis. More on the disappearance of
43-year-old Dr. Stephen Conrad tonight at ten."

Cole reached for the remote. Rachel Vayes. She had been running Alpha's
preferred version of the energy shortage for three years — the one where the
crisis was complex, the solution was coming, and Alpha happened to be the people
working on it. He had never once heard her question the premise. Bitboy returned
from a meandering trip to the kitchen and, with snacks in hand, sat in wonder at
the crackling fire and the boiling, spitting sap. Cole cracked open a beer on
his way to the medicine cabinet. If selling second-hand Federation tech on the
black market had been a rich man's game, Bitboy's thirst and the price of
Naloxone-E kept Cole distant from a golden throne. Sharon had explained it to him once, over bourbon: Alpha bought every aftermarket parts patent the Federation used, tripled the price, and called it authorized distribution. The gray market grew in the gap. Sharon had been the gap. While walking back to the
warmth of the living room, Cole pulled his phone from his pocket and searched
for "Heavy Hitter" in his contact list.

"I need you to walk me through it again. I have the shot, but what's the rest?"
Cole asked.

"It's a good thing I'm about there then. Isn't it?" Ricardo replied.

“Hell will freeze over. Sharon will marry me. Heck, the lights will stay on past
midnight — all before you drag your ass out here after five years,” Cole said.

"I know. I know," Ricardo replied. "I'm under strict orders to keep this
conversation analog. I'll be there in about an hour."

"Orders? And here I thought you were coming to visit an old friend or, better
yet, to pick up your damn pet."

"I have a 30-rack of Natty Light," Ricardo said.

"That's a start," Cole replied. "But Bitboy and I don't drink anymore. We're
watching our figures."

"Come on, man," Ricardo insisted. "Just one beer."

"You're trouble, Hitter," Cole said. "Just one beer!"

With the Naloxone-E in hand, Cole turned his attention from the phone to Bitboy,
who was lost in the dance of the flames and snacks. The glow of the fire cast shadows across Cole's aging face. He looked at the
Naloxone-E in his hand. He looked at Bitboy. The dosage was the problem — too
little and the monkey stayed under, too much and the shock could stop his heart.

"all right, buddy," Cole said. "Just a short prick."

Bitboy, sensing the shift in Cole's demeanor, turned, his eyes narrowing in
confusion and then widening in understanding. An unwelcome whirr came from
Bitboy's arms, and the blades peeked out. There was a moment of understanding
even a junky monkey could grasp, and Bitboy fell willingly, yet tense, into
Cole's arms. With a gentle touch, Cole injected the Naloxone-E. Bitboy twitched.
His grip on Cole's sleeve loosened. His breathing steadied, and Cole's hands
steadied with it. Bitboy's eyes flickered with clarity.

"I'm going to just toss you out in the snow next time," Cole said. "Do you
understand me, Monkey?"

A quiet settled between them, broken only by the occasional pop and hiss from
the fire. Bitboy, lucid but weakened, leaned into Cole, and Cole draped an arm
around Bitboy. As they sat in the warmth of the living room, Cole's gaze drifted
into the fire.

The crisp air filled Ricardo's government-issued SUV with a scent of pine and
dust. A nearby stream babbled its way through the valley. He handled the winding
country road carelessly like only a pilot could. The unmistakable sound of tires on a dirt road carried for miles.

Gravel crunched under the tires as Ricardo pulled into the driveway. Before him
stood a single A-frame cabin. Ricardo chugged the last of a lukewarm energy
drink.

Cole cracked the front door and winced at the bright sunlight dazzling across
the numerous pines with their dusting of snow. His eyes scanned the environment
before settling his sight on his visitor.

"Coley Coley no-bowley," Ricardo said from the doorway, the way he always said it — not a greeting exactly, more a philosophical position he had held for years and felt compelled to restate at every available opportunity.

"Hey, Nova, I hope you don't mind. I let myself past the gate," Ricardo said.

Ricardo was the embodiment of the world Cole left behind. A Federation man
through and through. With his height and heft, he filled his fatigues just
enough to accentuate massive arms and shoulders while leaving room in the
midsection to hide his growing gut. Next to his large body, he carried a heavy
black briefcase, and he looked like a 4th grader with a lunch pail.

"You didn't get past anything," Cole said. "I clocked you on the gate's camera
and buzzed you in."

"Always have to be the winner, eh, Nova?"

"Just works out that way, Hitter. My sister and girlfriend will be here soon.
Behave."

Ricardo joined Cole on the wraparound porch and clasped his large hands around
Cole's shoulders. The two men looked into each other for only a moment, and the
years between them melted away. Cole grunted and patted Ricardo away from their
friendly embrace.

"Bitboy is by the fireplace," Cole said. "Go on and take a look. I'm going to
wait for the girls."

Ricardo pulled a beer from the pouch pocket on his jacket and cracked it open
with a crispness that echoed off the granite walls around them. Then, from
another pocket, he retrieved another and tossed it to Cole before disappearing
into the cabin. Ricardo had seen The Big Lebowski more times than he could count and considered it a philosophical text. He cited it the way other people cited scripture — in analogies, in the framing of difficult situations, in lines dropped into conversation whether or not anyone recognized them. The Dude was not a joke to Ricardo. The Dude abided. That was the whole architecture of Ricardo's worldview in four words.

The sound of another vehicle crept closer and closer. Cole waved them over when
Sharon and Emily finally arrived in a puff of dust. Both women climbed out of the van and stood in the dust, blinking at the trees. Cole slowly opened his
beer with a hiss instead of a crack and quickly drank a giant's share. He
watched Sharon and sent an effortlessly booming whistle through the quiet valley
around them.

Sharon stretched and looked up at the porch and had,
for approximately two seconds, the sensation of a memory reassembling
itself from the parts she'd filed in the wrong drawer. A transit bay at Port
Authority Fourteen. A manifest dispute that was not his problem. A port official
who had been publicly wrong, and a man who could not help making that his
problem anyway — had walked forty feet out of his way, involved a stranger,
cited the exact weight classification code from memory, and then looked at her
with an expression that was not gratitude and not satisfaction but something
quieter and more specific. The look of a person who had been right about
something and wanted nothing from it. She had been thinking about that look, off
and on, for four years. She had not expected it to be on a porch in the Sierra
Nevadas holding a beer.

"Hold your damn horses," Sharon said. "Some of us give our bodies love from time
to time."

"Put on a few pounds," Ricardo's voice carried from inside the house, "and I'd
take care of that for you."

Emily turned red to the ears and covered her mouth with both hands. Cole, on
the other hand, closed the door.

"Girls, we have a visitor," Cole said. "I'd like you to meet Bitboy's deadbeat
dad. This disgusting pervert is Heavy Hitter."

Still outside, the three embraced until Emily squirmed out to allow Cole and
Sharon a moment of affection. Cole and Sharon absorbed each other. Cole's hands
wrapped around Sharon's lower back, and he lifted her into a longing kiss. With
her back turned, Emily did her best to pretend she wasn't intruding at that
moment.

"How'd she do?" Cole asked in a soft tone that sounded like a secret.

Emily rounded the corner and pressed her shoulder blades against the wall, the
way she always did when a room got smaller than she wanted to be in it.

"Cole, honey," Sharon looked up into his eyes, "she was incredible. She'll rule
the world once she figures out how capable she is."

"I'm glad to hear it," Cole said. "But what happened to the guy if it went so
well?"

"The guy?" Emily said, popping her head around a nearby corner. "What, uh, what
guy?"

"Your contact, E," Cole said. "You obviously got the tech. But what happened to
Dr. Conrad?"

"Babe," Sharon said, "you must be more specific. Stephen was a bit of a mess. He
gave us this whole doom prediction and this memory stick. Other than that, it
mostly went down as expected."

Sharon handed Cole the van keys, and both women stood silent. Cole held the
memory drive, rubbing his thumb across the hull of the Enterprise. The
repetitive motion left a dark silver stain on his skin.

He didn't think about why he kept rubbing it. The fire popped behind him.
Someone — Sharon, Emily — was talking in the kitchen, the words arriving at a
distance that had nothing to do with the room's dimensions. He'd been doing it
for forty seconds before he noticed, and another twenty before he stopped.

Five years in the cabin, and the thing he'd never told anyone — not Ricardo,
not Emily, not Sharon — was that the part of the Polaris he missed most was not
his crew. He missed his crew in the full human way, the way that came with faces
and voices and specific remembered moments he couldn't close his eyes without
seeing. That grief had its own room.

This was a different room.

Gracie had been in the ship. She'd been in the ship's systems, and the ship was
gone, and the morning after he'd made it back, he'd opened the app out of habit
and gotten nothing. Not an error. Nothing. The way a phone number simply doesn't
exist anymore. He hadn't told anyone about that part. There was no language for
it. She wasn't a person. There was no paperwork for that kind of loss. You
couldn't put it on the memorial placard.

Ricardo, drunk on Natty Light two winters ago, had supplied a word for it.

Space divorce, Ricardo had said, with the solemnity of a man who believed he'd
said something hilarious. You're a space divorcée, Nova. Five years in a cabin
in the woods. Classic post-space-divorce behavior. I've seen it a hundred times.

You've seen it zero times, Cole had said.

I've seen it in you one time, and that's a hundred percent of my data.

Cole had laughed. He'd laughed because Ricardo was right and because laughing
was easier than the thing underneath laughing, which was that Gracie had been
the one who noticed when he hadn't slept and the one who catalogued his silences
without comment and the one who knew, without asking, when he was running the
Polaris incident in his head again. And she had been gone since the Polaris. And in the
years since, without anyone filling that function —

He had looked up one afternoon and Sharon was sitting across from him at the
table.

She had driven three hours without telling him she was coming. She had brought
blackberry preserves from the farmer's market and a road atlas she claimed was
for a road trip she was planning, and she'd sat down and said absolutely nothing
about why she was there, and she'd been there for four days, and she'd noticed
every single thing she noticed without saying a word about it.

He had not said anything about it either.

It wasn't the same thing. Sharon was a person. Gracie had been something else.
But the function of it. The being-attended-to. Cole had spent
thirty-two years not knowing he needed it, four years learning to take it for
granted, and a year in a cabin learning what the absence felt like.

He rubbed his thumb across the hull of the Enterprise one more time and looked
at the stain on his skin.

Lock for every key, Conrad had apparently said.

Cole looked at the silver mark on his thumb and did not yet know what to do with
that.

"He was weird, okay," Emily said, "but nice too. He said to tell you there is a
lock for every key," raising her hands in confusion.

"So neither of you know where he went?"

The women looked at each other and shook their heads.

"Okay. Well, he's missing," Cole said. "Didn't even show up for his speech.
Thought maybe you had all run off together."

"How should we know where he went?" Emily said. "Is that something we should
know?"

Cole shook his head and opened the door. The smell of bacon and pancakes hit
them at the door — heavy, warm, the kind of smell that makes a hungry person
stop talking mid-sentence.

"Who's hungry?" Ricardo said. "Looks like these degens have been partying all
night...for, like, years. And from the van and sweatpants, I'd bet you girls
could use a meal."

Bitboy lay in agony next to the dying fire. Ricardo's case sat open on the table
next to several accouterments. Sharon added a small jar between the butter and a
container of organic maple syrup. Cole set down his empty beer can and reached
for the case Ricardo had brought with him. He unpacked the stomach pump from
within it and saw Emily holding Bitboy with the care of a nurturing mother. Cole
wondered where she learned that as he tiptoed toward the two. He kneeled and
nodded to Emily. They transferred Bitboy to Cole's firm embrace, and Bitboy
nuzzled into Cole's chest.

Emily slid the tip of her finger under Bitboy's chin. She gently pressed up
until their eyes met, and Bitboy went limp with safety. Cole gently slid the
long tube down the macaque's throat as the poor animal twitched in discomfort
layered on pain. The device hummed to life, pulling putrid sludge up with every
beat. As it worked, Cole glanced at the empty can.

"Hey, Hitter," Cole said while leaning over Bitboy, "beer me."

"You have the grace of a peasant," Sharon said.

The machine emitted a steady hum, purging the toxins from Bitboy's system.
Emily, her face set with determination, carefully removed the tube from Bitboy's
mouth. She moved to the sink, the sound of running water mingling with the
crackle of the fire. She scrubbed her hands and glanced over her shoulder,
noting Ricardo's handiwork. The kitchen was a scene of organized chaos: plates
piled high with bacon and a mountain of pancakes, while six empty beer cans
stood sentinel on the counter.

"A girl could get used to this," she said, her smile widening as she caught
Ricardo's eye.

Ricardo leaned against the counter with a beer in hand and wearing a lopsided
grin. "Shit, sis, this place is stocked like a bomb shelter. So, you know, I
could get used to this too."

Across the room, Cole knelt beside Bitboy, his rough hands surprisingly gentle
as he caressed the small creature. Lulled by the fire's warmth and rhythmic
crackling, Bitboy's eyes fluttered shut. Cole watched as Bitboy's breathing
steadied and deepened. He then carefully laid Bitboy in a plush dog bed
positioned just within the fire's glow. The front door creaked behind, and
Sharon stepped in, boasting a devilish grin commanding attention. She moved with
deliberate grace, her steps quiet but assured. She placed a hand on Cole's
shoulder and drew his gaze away from Bitboy.

"I have a surprise for you," she said. "Come sit with me and close your eyes."

Cole raised an eyebrow, but he complied. Sharon took his hand, her touch light
but insistent, and led him towards the kitchen table. He settled into the chair,
fingers splayed over his eyes, peeking through them. Sharon gently guided his
hand away from his face and pressed it to her chest. He could feel her heart
flutter beneath his palm.

"Okay, open them," she whispered.

He blinked against the light and saw a dark purple jar nestled beside a stack of
steaming pancakes, promising something special inside. Cole's eyes softened, and
a smile spread across his face.

"You spoil me," he said.

"Only because you deserve it," Sharon replied, kissing his forehead. The kiss
lingered.

Cole came around the corner into the living room and Ricardo and Bitboy were watching Fight Club. Different scene than last time — the one with the chemical burn. Same movie. Every time. Neither of them looked up. Neither of them acknowledged that this was unusual. It was not unusual to them.

The fire crackled.

"Are you real? I don't deserve you." Cole said, his hand still resting on her
heart.

Sharon smiled, squeezing his hand tighter against her chest. "I got this at the
farmer's market. The last blackberry preserves they had!"

"Mmm! Quick, open it for me," Ricardo interjected, eager as ever.

Cole opened the jar with a satisfying pop. Sharon dipped her finger into the
bittersweet jam and then into Cole's mouth. He melted into his chair, savoring
the taste, his lips lingering on her finger as she slowly pulled it away.

"Jackpot," Ricardo said, a spreading mischievous grin. "I haven't seen you this
happy since our first time behind the stick. Let me try that moon goo. Maybe
later, we can zip around in whatever plane you have hidden here."

"I'm not flying," Cole said, standing abruptly. "Not later. Not ever."

Ricardo's smile faded. "Then you're not going to like why I'm here," he said,
his tone becoming serious. "Although I don't entirely know myself."

Sharon fixed her most penetrative glare on Ricardo. "Did you know she's involved
with those crackpot Collectivists?" Sharon asked.

Sharon had moved parts alongside the Collective for years without ever pretending she was one of them. They'd given her a patch. She'd put it in a drawer.

Ricardo's face fell. "Yes, I knew."

"And you didn't think to tell me?" Cole asked, his voice rising.

"It's not my place, Cole. She's an adult and makes her own decisions," Ricardo
replied calmly.

She set her fork down on the table, her eyes
watering. She stood up without a word and walked to the kitchen sink, her hands
moving automatically as she touched the empty plates, scratching the crumbs off
pans.

Emily grasped the sides of her
head, let out a silent scream, and returned to the living room—her face red but
otherwise composed. She sat on the couch, facing away from her brother.

Cole said nothing and continued to pet Bitboy, who had joined him on the sofa.
His mind was on the letter and the thumb drive on the table. Until this
moment, his thoughts had been on the monkey, pancakes, and the metric volume of
fuel needed to reach Mars—anything other than what these items held for him.

"And burn my apartment down when it self-destructs? No thanks," Ricardo said,
shaking his head. "I don't know what's in there any more than I'd know how many
lives I've saved via lovemaking."

In the following pause, Cole's jaw set. He looked at each of them and found
nothing he could use. Emily's gaze
wandered around the log cabin, taking in the sight of dirty laundry mounds and
empty cans that littered the floor. Sharon and Ricardo looked back at Cole,
their expressions unreadable.

"They can say or do whatever they want. It's been five years," Cole said. "Five years! Do they think they can put a leash on me
and run me around like a dog?"

Emily glanced at him, her voice softening. "I don't know if I should feel like
I'm in danger or satisfied."

Cole's heart raced. "How do you mean?"

"That's the first real emotion I've seen out of you in months," Emily said with
a smile playing on her lips.

"Tell us you're ready," Sharon demanded, eyes locking onto Cole's.

Bitboy turned to look at Cole. The metal implements in his arms twined
unnaturally around the muscles. Cole's mind reeled, wondering what secrets the
envelope held. At the dining table, Ricardo tried to get Bitboy's attention.
Still, the monkey ignored him, perhaps because of Ricardo's earlier abandonment
or simply because Bitboy had forgotten him. Instead, Bitboy's eyes remained
locked on Cole, his mechanical body whirring and tensing in sync with his
master's agitation.

Ricardo stood, sighing as he walked from the dining table to the couch, the
envelope held out in front of him. He extended the envelope toward Cole, who
snatched it from Ricardo's fingers. Cole glanced at Emily and Sharon, then
shrugged and extracted crisp white papers from within. Two small captain
insignia tumbled out and landed on his lap as he did. His breath caught, and for
a moment, he stared at them, the gleaming symbols of authority and recognition.
His fingers hovered over the insignia, eyes widening with longing and disbelief.
He picked up the insignia, feeling their cold metal against his skin. He had
imagined this moment countless times, but this felt like a cruel bribe. His mind
flashed back to the lives lost under his command. The insignia dug into his
skin. He tossed them on the table, feigning disinterest, and moved on.

The Federation's emblem in the upper left-hand corner of the letter sent a
shudder down his spine. Sharon rested her head on her hand, arm draped over the
back of the sofa. Her eyes were fixed intently on Cole as his gaze darted across
the page, index finger trembling as it followed each line. He began to read
aloud, his voice unsteady, the weight of the words sinking in with every
syllable:

From the Office of Federation Chancellor Geoffrey H. DeMint

Dear Mr. Nova,

The Federation now stands at a critical juncture, and your unparalleled
expertise is urgently required. After extensive deliberation, the Council has
concluded that your unique qualifications are essential to our mission. Enclosed
is a copy of a legally binding document with your signature dated September 12,
2021, which waives your rights as a United States citizen in exchange for your
service in the Stellar Council Forces. Your duty to the Council remains in
effect and paramount.

We regret to inform you of the escalating energy shortage crisis, which
threatens the very fabric of our society. The severity of this condition is
unprecedented, and we seek your immediate involvement with utmost urgency. Your
skills and experience make you uniquely positioned to address this dire
situation.

You are hereby ordered to prepare for immediate deployment. Report to Federal
Space Command Headquarters. Be advised that your movements are under constant
surveillance. The Federation relies on your swift and unquestioning compliance.
The stakes have never been higher, and the survival of humanity rests on your
shoulders.

This is your moment, Mr. Nova. The world is watching. We need the old Cole
Nova—the leader, the hero, the man who can turn the tide. Your legacy awaits.

Be advised: Saturn's outer rings will enter full gravitational closure on
November 22, 2033 — the Rapture window. After this date, no vessel can
access the outer ring formations until the next alignment cycle. Retrieval
must be complete before this date. There will be no extension.

Sincerely,

Geoffrey H. DeMint, Federation Chancellor

He had stopped reading at the Rapture date. Read it again. The number
sat in him the way a number does when it becomes a countdown: November 22,
2033. He did the math without paper. It checked out. It had always been
going to check out.

He made himself remember it. Then Cole crumpled the letter and dropped it into
the steel waste bin without looking.

"Bastards," he said. Quietly.

The room was still. Then: "I need more time." His voice broke on the last word. The sudden clatter of dishes from the dining area caught his
attention. "Where's Bitboy?"

The macaque perched on the table with a stack of pancakes the size of a tall top
hat, clutched in his tiny hands. He grinned sheepishly, crumbs littering his
fur. Cole and Emily shared a laugh bubbling up from their mutual despair. Sharon
and Ricardo joined in, their laughter tinged with nervousness. Sharon's mirth
quickly turned to rage as she noticed a blue trail of stains leading to Bitboy's
many fingers and the now-empty jar of blackberry preserves. The juice had turned
his steel claws a dark blue, with a hint of the color marking his lips and
tongue.

"You dirty ape! Is nothing sacred to you?" she cried, her voice rising in
frustration.

Cole turned away from the scene, his smile fading. He strode purposefully to a
cluttered desk and jammed the thumb drive into the side of an old laptop. The
screen flickered to life. Files opened across the display in a frenzy — data scrolling faster than anyone
could read. He leaned in, scanning the information with growing
unease.

Ricardo walked over and peered over Cole's shoulder. The data scrolling on the
monitor included information on Cole that he had long buried in the recesses of
his memory: missions, failures, and personal details he wished to forget. Dirt
on Ricardo that could bury him twice over, both scandalous and damning. Notes on
Emily that reduced her fears to a mere footnote in a much larger, darker
narrative of corruption and cover-ups.

One encrypted file sat at the bottom of the list, tagged "Dark Matter Research."

"Bitboy, play," Cole said.

Bitboy scampered to the laptop. His mechanical fingers moved across the keyboard
with precise efficiency, eyes glowing with soft light as he interfaced with the
encryption. The room waited. Then the screen blinked, and the files opened.
Bitboy returned to his pancakes without ceremony.

Cole's eyes moved across the screen. Alpha Corp's internal correspondence.
Patent filings — hundreds of them — acquired, classified, and shelved. Disposal
Protocol Eleven: 11 to 13 percent of viable Darker Matter product deliberately
destroyed per batch to manage pricing. Government grants worth billions,
diverted from crisis relief into Alpha's research divisions. Surveillance
reports — his own name appearing in files dated years before the Polaris.

Emily leaned over his shoulder. "They caused it," she murmured. "The shortage.
They caused it."

Nobody moved. The fire popped once behind them.

Cole scrolled. More files. The suppression was systematic — every viable
alternative acquired and buried, every independent researcher either hired or
silenced. The energy crisis was not a failure of science. It was inventory
management.

He closed the laptop. The room was quiet in the way a room gets quiet when
everyone in it has just learned the same thing and nobody knows who should speak
first.

---

The sun dipped behind the Sierras. The golden light filtered through the
windows, landing on the piles of luggage compiled throughout the day.

Bitboy climbed into the back seat out of habit. Cole and Ricardo swung the
luggage into the SUV's open hatch. They stood across from one another, Cole on
the passenger side and Ricardo on the driver's.

"You know, you and Sharon make a great couple," Ricardo remarked, his voice
tinged with sincerity and envy. "You two remind me of the people in picture
frames—you know, the ones that come in the package—the ones you throw out and
replace with people you care about."

"We'll take that as a compliment. I guess?" Cole replied, a small smile playing
on his lips.

Sharon joined Cole at his side, pulled his face towards hers with both hands,
and delivered a perfectly timed, fully committed kiss.

"Those picture frame models don't come close to our hotness," she said. "Isn't
that right?"

"You know it!" Cole said.

Bitboy mimicked their affection from the back seat, rubbing his palms together
and making kissing sounds. Ricardo's forehead flushed.

"Been training at all? What's your cholesterol like?" Ricardo said while he
cracked open a tall energy drink with a single hand. "Can you even fit into a
flight suit?"

"Let's get that head rush," Cole said in his best Astro jargon. "If the payload
doesn't make it home, ain't nobody gettin' paid! We'll be home in bed while the
thrusters are still hot enough to fry an egg."

Sharon leaned back with wide eyes, shaking her head. "You boys have fun with
your 'Astros only' cult."

Cole spotted a hummingbird feeder hanging near the porch and quickly strode
over, ripping a length of copper wire from it. He picked up a small pebble from
the road, its surface smooth and weathered. He twisted the wire around his pinky
with nimble fingers, securing the pebble at the top and fashioning a makeshift
ring. He returned to Sharon, who watched him with curiosity.

"For the first time, I want to give you something to hold onto," Cole said,
placing the makeshift ring in her hand. "I'll bring back something extraordinary
to replace this. Keep it safe for me?"

Sharon's eyes softened, and she closed her fingers around the ring. "I will,"
she whispered. "You'd better come back to me, Cole Nova."

Emily patiently waited on the porch swing for her turn to speak to Cole. She
watched him hold Sharon, and Sharon gripped him in return. They held one another
with simple determination.

"Take E to deliver the crystals; they're only stable for six more days," Cole
said. "It's the same spot as always. Leave the van and take the train back."

Cole looked up from the nest it had made in Sharon's hair along her neckline and
saw Emily gently swinging, wrapped in one of their mother's god-awful knitted
blankets.

"Guiding light in my life," Emily said, a tear streaming down her cheek.
"Distance and time mean nothing against the bond we share. Look to the ground as
I will always be there waiting for you, guiding you. Keep our dreams alive, and
remember, you're always in my heart. I love you, big brother."

"Okay, nerd. I will if you will," Cole said, taking his sister in his arms and
softly shushing her quiet sobs. "Leave the hero stuff to me. Take care of
yourself, okay? I'll see you when I get back. I promise."

Ricardo got in the driver's seat. As he pulled away, a dust cloud flew around
the rental car. Cole watched Emily and Sharon in the rearview mirror until they
were too small to recognize.

"It's already cold. Doesn't the heater work?" Cole complained.

"Fine, fine. I'll turn up the heater just for you, princess," Ricardo said.
"They've had me shuttling supplies to the asteroid miners every other year.
Short jumps to orbit stations through meteor storms and everything."

Cole watched the trees pass—the silhouette of peaks and valleys formed against
the night sky and sleepy mountains.

"Man, you had to be there this one time. Asteroids hit my wings like buckshot.
It was rad. The tail almost fell off about a thousand feet before I hit the
runway, but my bird held together. Hah! Man, you should've seen it."

Cole shut his eyes tightly and tried to breathe through the rising, ominous
visions before they could fully overtake him. With a slow inhale and a long
exhale, he brought his awareness back to the present moment; his mood now
soured.

"Can we not talk about accidents?"

"I should have retired years ago. How was retirement?"

"Was?"

"Hate to break it to you, silly goose," Ricardo said, patting Cole on the
shoulder. "But retirement was over the moment you stepped into this SUV."

"Listen, man, I don't get a lot of human interaction, and that's just how I like
it. It's just been the girls and Bitboy for the last half-decade." The chimp
grunted at his name and popped his head between the front seats.

"You forget we're brothers, Nova." Ricardo's smile faded briefly, then returned
as he turned to look at Cole. "As your brother, it's my duty to annoy the
ever-living shit out of you until you lighten the fuck up."

"Keep your damn eyes on the road, bro," Cole said.

Ricardo hissed dryly like a steam engine running out of water. The dry cough
conveniently became a tool for breaking the awkward moment before it could
stretch into oblivion. Or maybe he just realized how thirsty he was. He reached
for his energy drink, which was no longer in the center console. He looked at
the empty cup holder.

For the mere span of two heartbeats, his eyes left the road, hands released from
the steering wheel, and the vehicle chose to do its own thing. Ricardo calmly
but nonchalantly regained control of the wheel and eventually returned to the
original lane it had drifted from.

"Are you testing me?" Cole asked.

"Just having some fun, mountain man," Ricardo said. "You do remember what that
is, right?"

"No," Cole answered without a hint of amusement. "What were you looking for?
I'll get it so you can focus."

Ricardo let that land, then let it go. He drove for a moment in silence, then
spoke to the windshield like he was telling the story to himself.

"You remember Kowalski?"

Cole said nothing.

"Titan Station. The customs inspector with the lazy eye — the one who'd hold
your paperwork up just to watch you sweat." Ricardo shook his head slowly,
already fighting the grin. "We're looking at a thirty-hour hold, mandatory cargo
inspection, the whole thing. I'm doing the math on how bad this is gonna be. And
Kowalski — without a word, without a single word — reaches into his chest pocket
and sets a hard-boiled egg on the man's desk. Just sets it there. Folds his
hands. Looks at him."

Cole stared out the window.

"Inspector looked at the egg. Looked at Kowalski. Stamped our clearance and told
us to have a good day." Ricardo exhaled through his nose. "We asked him about it
on the ride back. You know what he said? He said, 'An egg on a desk confuses any
bureaucrat long enough to reset the situation.' Said it worked every time. Four
stations, never failed."

Something shifted almost imperceptibly at the corner of Cole's mouth. Not a
smile. Just the ghost of one, passing through.

"Dumbest thing I ever saw," Ricardo said quietly. "Worked perfect."

"My energy drink, or whatever's left of it."

Cole glanced to the back seat to catch Bitboy tilting the can upside down above
his gaping maw, which had protruding razor-sharp fangs.

"We have maybe five minutes," Cole buried his face in his hands. "You have to
keep anything he can use for energy on lock, man; you know this: sugar,
caffeine, ethanol."

"Hey, I think we're being followed."

A black SUV similar to their own quickly approached from behind them. It sped up
suddenly, pulling alongside them, then roared ahead and swung in front of them.

Ricardo stared back at Bitboy through the rearview mirror. For no particular
logical reason, Bitboy utilized each of his four arms and two legs to shoot
obscene gestures at Ricardo with a toothy smile. Redirecting the high beams
shining from the car approaching behind them, Ricardo blasted Bitboy with a
concentrated shot of light by cleverly repositioning the mirror.

"What's got you spooked?" Cole asked. "We're going to see another car from time
to time. Bitboy is cracked out; you'll rile him up if you don't chill."

"If they were just looking to pass, why slow down after?" Ricardo said. "Bitboy,
buckle up. Cole, do whatever you'd like, but there's an automatic 9mm under your
seat if shit hits the fan."

The LED brake lights ahead of them shone an intense red as the vehicle dropped
in speed. Cole gripped the overhead handle and braced himself. Ricardo hit the
brakes hard, redirected, then hit the gas. He swerved left around the other
vehicle in a shower of gravel and dirt.

Still looking entirely too calm, Ricardo jested, "Somebody doesn't like you,
Cole. And please handle your monkey."

The SUV was in the rearview again. Bitboy did not buckle up. Instead, he began
to deploy and retract the sleek blades built into his forearms. With each
deployment, Bitboy released a lion's bark. As the car behind them again grew
nearer, it filled the cabin with light, creating a shadow puppet show of the
monkey as he jumped around in the back seat. He shredded the cushions and raked
the ceiling open with his claws.

"All right, Jackpot. Call the cops," Ricardo ordered.

Cole hastily located his phone and dialed.

"9-1-1, please state your name and the location of your emergency."

"This is Cole Nova. I'm located at mile marker, uh, thirty-three on Forest
Service Road 22. An aggressive driver is tailing me. They're trying to run us
off the road."

There was a brief pause before the operator responded. "I'm sorry, sir. We don't
have any units in that area. Can you get away from them?" Her voice was calm,
too calm, with an unsettling smoothness.

"We're trying," Cole said, his voice strained, eyes darting between the phone
and the road. "But they keep ramming us."

"Who else is with you?" the operator asked, her tone oddly clinical.

"Ricardo Corazon and a monkey," Cole replied, the absurdity of the situation
hitting him even in the crisis. There was another pause on the line, longer this
time, enough to make Cole's anxiety spike.

"Is this a prank call?" the operator demanded, her voice now edged with a
strange irritation. "Are there any females in the vehicle with you?"

"This isn't a prank, ma'am!" Cole insisted, frustration seeping into his tone.
"No, just us."

"We have no units in that area. Your report has been logged. Sorry, sir. Good
luck." The line went dead abruptly, leaving Cole staring at his phone in
disbelief.

Bitboy's shrieks filled the cabin as he dismantled the back seat, his fury
palpable. He clambered onto the duffle bag in the trunk, eyes gleaming with a
wild light. The Escalade behind them hit their bumper and the impact went through the seat,
through Cole's spine, through his teeth. The steering wheel jerked in Ricardo's
hands. The tires caught gravel and the whole vehicle slid sideways onto the
rocky shoulder, the undercarriage scraping stone.

Ricardo's voice was terse. "The gun?"

Cole shook his head, his voice barely a whisper. "I've done enough killing.
Bitboy, attention!"

Ricardo sucked his teeth in frustration. Bitboy, illuminated by the dashboard
lights, turned his murderous gaze toward Cole. Ricardo maneuvered the SUV back
onto the road, accelerating to match their aggressors' speed.

"Bitboy, defend," Cole said.

Bitboy's eyes shifted to soft blue light — the glow that meant enhanced mode, the glow that meant the six-limbed test subject from a Federation cage had just become the most dangerous thing on the highway. His metal fingernails extended, clicking against each other with the small precise sound of something calibrating.

"Bad men. Initiate option Sierra-4."

Bitboy turned to face the attacking vehicle, his muscles coiling with tension.
With a savage roar, he launched himself through the shattered rear window, glass
shards glittering in the night. He landed on the hood of the pursuing SUV with a
metallic thud, his blades slicing into the windshield. The vehicle swerved
violently as Bitboy disappeared inside.

Gunfire erupted from within the SUV, each shot a violent burst of chaos. Ricardo
pulled their car to a stop about 40 yards away, grabbing an assault rifle from
under the rear seat. Cole stumbled out, running towards the wreckage, heart
pounding so loud it drowned out the noise. More shots rang out, each one making
him flinch. The shrieking of the chimpanzee drowned out the terrified screams of
the men inside.

Then, silence.

Cole reached the SUV, breath ragged and heart racing. A bloodied, unconscious
man in a sports coat and tee shirt occupied the driver's seat. On the passenger
side floor lay an empty hamburger bag and a pistol. Bitboy squatted in the
passenger seat, sucking on the straw of the man's soda with a satisfied grin.
The rear cabin was a gruesome scene of dismembered body parts, blood splattered
everywhere. Cole couldn't discern how many attackers there were, but their
private attire and weaponry were unmistakable.

"Bitboy. Attention! Threat neutralized."

Cole pulled the driver's wallet from the inside pocket of his sports coat, hands
shaking as he took a photo of his license. He grabbed the gun and then scooped
sand from the roadside. He emptied the remaining bullets from the magazine, then
cocked the gun, pouring sand into the slide until it jammed. He tossed it into
the bushes.

"Let's go. Let's go!"

Cole and Bitboy rushed back to the car, jumping in beside Ricardo. They sped off
into the night, the tension still thick.

"I got a photo of one of their IDs," Cole said, breathing heavily. "We'll file a
report when we get to The Federation."

"Holy shit Cole, what have you been training this monkey to do up there in the
mountains?" Ricardo said.

Cole leaned his seat back, crawling over to inspect Bitboy for wounds. He was
covered in blood and gore, but none of it was his.

The city skyline grew taller as they approached, lights twinkling against the
dark sky.

"We just left a crime scene. And I gave our names to the police," Cole said. "I'm pretty sure we just committed a few dozen
felonies."

"I'd say we just neutralized a threat," Ricardo replied. "Who have you been
pissing off, Nova?"

"Lots of people have reason to hate me," Cole said.

"What's the name on the ID?" Ricardo asked.

Cole pulled up the photo. The license image was clear: the face of the man in
the driver's seat, unconscious. The name beneath it read *Davis, Brandon T.*
Pasadena, California. Age forty-one.

Cole held the phone toward Ricardo without a word.

Ricardo looked at it. The name. The face. He had the ID for maybe three seconds.
His eyes moved from the screen back to the road without hurrying. A moment
passed — the exact length of a moment in which a person is deciding whether
to say something. Then he glanced at Cole. Not the glance of a man who didn't
recognize something. The opposite of that.

The Polaris had carried three hundred and sixty-nine people. They were all
dead. Ricardo had attended every memorial service he could reach. He had met
the families. He knew where a name like Davis fit in that picture, and it was
not as crew.

He said nothing. Cole said nothing. Ricardo drove.

The city lights grew taller around them, the shadows of their actions trailing
closely behind.

MERIDIAN VI — THE CONFIRMATION

What Kate had not put in her seven-sentence relay was what
happened after she hit send.

She sat for eleven minutes.

The ship-day lighting ran at the same level regardless of hour
— the Meridian didn't track a planetary cycle. The station at
0300 looked identical to the station at 1400. Kate had stopped
noticing this. She had started noticing it again after San
Francisco.

Dayo found her there when he came on shift. He read the
confirmation on her screen and stood for a moment — not rushing,
not looking away, locating the feeling precisely, putting it
somewhere it could be carried.

"Her droplet's on the wall," Kate said.

"I know."

"I put it there after we lost her. I didn't ask anyone."

"You didn't need to ask." He sat in the secondary comms chair.
"Petyr asleep?"

"Yes."

"Good." He paused. "A droplet goes to someone who felt the
current before they knew there was a river. That was Sharon.
She was running her own version of this network before the
network knew she existed. She didn't need a rank. She didn't
need a patch. She had already chosen the side before anyone
told her there were sides."

Kate said nothing.

"We're going to get the data out," she said. Not a comfort.
A statement of intent.

"Yes," Dayo said. "We are."

Outside the viewport, Saturn's rings caught refracted sunlight
and held it — a thin, bright edge in the dark. The ship at this
hour made no sound you could name. Only the held absence
of it: the held-breath quiet that had been held so long it had
become the normal texture of the air. Kate had stopped noticing
it. She noticed it now.

"The network needs people who choose it," Kate said.

"Sharon chose it before she knew there was a name for it," Dayo
said. "That's the thing about people like her. The network
doesn't recruit them. They arrive."
