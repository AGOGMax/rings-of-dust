INTERLUDE

The Watch


Wilhelm gave him the pocket watch on his twentieth birthday.

Not as ceremony — nothing Wilhelm did was ceremony. He set it on the kitchen table and pushed it across the oilcloth and said: "Your grandfather carried this to America and lost it in a card game and won it back the next night. Your great-grandfather carried it from a village that doesn't exist anymore. I carried it for forty years. It is now yours."

Klaus picked it up. Brass, worn smooth at the edges. A coat of arms on the back that had been engraved by someone who had understood the weight of what they were putting there. He turned it over. The face: white enamel, black numerals, a second hand that moved in a continuous sweep rather than ticking. He had seen his father hold this watch his entire life. He had never held it himself.

He opened it. The time read 11:17.

He knew it was 11:17 because he had looked at the clock in the hallway before coming to the table.

"You're not going to check it," Wilhelm said. He was watching him.

"I know what time it is."

Wilhelm made a small sound. "You always do."

They sat for a moment. The kitchen was quiet — the house was quiet, the way the house had been quiet since Klaus's mother died, a particular quality of silence that was not empty but had learned to live without filling itself.

"There are things I need to tell you," Wilhelm said.

Klaus waited.

"There is a belief system in this family. It predates me and it predates my father and it predates the name we carry now." He paused. "Von Grutzen was my grandmother's name — the name she carried before the family changed it. The belief came from her line."

Klaus had heard pieces of this. Fragments, oblique references, things said in the presence of certain women at certain times that were not quite explained. He had understood, from an early age, that there was a structure underneath the structure he was being raised inside, and he had been patient about it in the way he was patient about most things.

"My grandmother," Wilhelm said, "was one of the founders. Not of the business — of the belief. She was the one who located the source. She was the one who knew what it was and what to do with it. She organized the expedition. She stood at the entrance of a mine and sent the researchers in."

"She was in Reykjavik."

"She funded Reykjavik. She pointed them at the mine." A pause. "What they found there, I found in the Directorate's archive. The drives. The sequences. I read them and I understood what they were, and I understood what my grandmother had given me, and I made a decision."

Klaus turned the watch over in his hands. The brass was warm from his father's pocket.

"She believed," Wilhelm said, "that what you pour yourself into continues. That the acts of this life shape what the bloodline receives going forward. That there is a mechanic to it — a real mechanic, not metaphor." He said this without embarrassment, which was unusual for a man who did not speak about the things he believed. "I took that belief and directed it toward what I was building. The empire is the expression of it. Everything I have built is poured forward — for you, and for your children, and for theirs. That is what the name Verguld is. It is not ambition. It is the mechanism."

Klaus looked at him.

"There are others who carry the same blood who did not take this path," Wilhelm said. "My grandmother's line branched. Some of them chose sacrifice over accumulation. They walk away from everything we built and give it to the void. This is their right." He paused. "They believe the same thing we believe. They express it differently. I do not quarrel with them. I have never quarreled with them."

Klaus nodded once. He was twenty years old and he already knew, with the specific clarity of someone who had been paying attention for twenty years, that he was at the beginning of something that would take the rest of his life.

"The ritual," he said. "Is it—"

"The ritual is my grandmother's. The Order's. I watched it, as a child. I never performed it. I didn't pass it on." Wilhelm's hand went, briefly, to where the watch had been in his pocket — a long habit reaching for an absent object, corrected. "I am telling you about the ritual so you understand where the belief comes from. The belief is not dependent on the ritual. The belief is the thing. The ritual is how my grandmother's line makes contact with the source. We make contact through building. Through the structure. Through the patience of knowing that what you build does not end when you end." He stood. He put his hand on the table, briefly, and then straightened. "Keep the watch wound. It runs correctly for years if you keep it wound."

He left the room.

Klaus sat at the kitchen table with the watch in his hands for a while. The second hand moved in its continuous sweep — no ticking, no interruption, just the quiet persistence of something that kept time without announcing itself.

He already knew what time it was.

He turned the watch over, felt the engraving under his thumb, and set it in his breast pocket.

He would wear it there for the next fifty years. He would wind it every morning. He would carry it the way you carry something that is both yours and not yours — belonging to the line, held in trust, to be passed forward when the time came.

He never opened it to check the time.

He already knew.
