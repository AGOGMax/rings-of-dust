RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Six

Ray's Discovery

Ray worked at Alpha's materials research division for three years before he understood what they were actually working on.

The official designation was Dark Matter Stabilization. The actual designation, which nobody used in writing, was: they had something they couldn't explain and were trying to make it useful before someone else did.

The something was DarkX. It arrived at the facility in sealed magnetic containers, two kilograms at a time, from a processing site that wasn't on any map Ray had been shown. It was obsidian, and it was never quite still — it threw slow spikes and tendrils against the walls of its container and drew them back, iridescent when the light caught it moving. It behaved like no material he'd worked with — it responded to magnetic field variations the way iron shavings respond to a magnet, except the responses were unpredictable, and the field gradients required were precise to a degree that made the testing equipment protest.

Alpha wanted it solid. Raw DarkX could not be stored, shipped, or burned — the plasma never stopped moving, and pushed too hard it went from volatile to catastrophic. The only way to make it usable was to convert it into Darker Matter crystals, and the conversion was brute and enormous. Industrial magnets the size of cars, calibrated to drag the plasma toward a packed bed of specific mineral salts. The whole reaction dropped into liquid nitrogen cold enough to accelerate the collapse. The magnets pulled the DarkX into the salt; the cold locked it down; the plasma gave up and crystallized — dense, dark, solid.

And it stayed solid for eleven days. After that, a converted crystal reverted — gave the lattice back and became raw DarkX again, plasma again, the thing that would not sit still. Eleven days was the window. You had that long to assemble the crystals into a FuelCell before the batch came undone and turned back into the problem you'd started with. Alpha had been trying for four years to widen the window. They never had.

Ray's job was the stabilization matrix. He ran the salts, the additives, the magnetic curing protocols. He ran them carefully, methodically, the way he'd been trained. He documented everything. He ate lunch at his desk. He was, as his supervisor noted in his last review, thorough.

He was also, by the time the Polaris disaster cleared the news cycle, bored.

The crisis had begun not with a failure of technology but with a coordinated financial assault. The global banking system had moved against cryptocurrency — specifically against the miners and nodes that had been building a decentralized energy grid outside institutional control. Regulatory pressure. Liquidity freezes. Infrastructure seizures. Power plant shutdowns and power grid defunding that official channels called decommissioning. The grid had collapsed along fault lines that had been prepared in advance. What the Federation called an energy shortage, the people who had built the alternative grid called an execution. The Polaris had not made it better. Three hundred and sixty-nine people and one research vessel — the only large-scale non-corporate DarkX extraction attempt in Federation history. What remained was a narrative: that DarkX was volatile, that its handling required institutional oversight, that Alpha Corp's measured approach was the only responsible path forward. The narrative had served the people who needed it to.

His colleagues called him Ray. It had started as a joke — his full name was Max Vision, and somewhere along the way someone had said "Max Ray Vision" because it sounded like X-ray vision, and the middle part stuck. He had never corrected it. He liked the way it sounded more than he liked the way Max sounded.



It started on a Friday afternoon, with a fresh batch of converted crystals on his workbench and the official containment unit across campus. Walking the batch over meant logging the transfer, clearing two checkpoints, and explaining why he had a live batch in his hands at the end of a Friday. He did the lazy thing instead. He sealed the crystals inside a spare FuelCell, flooded it with nitrogen, and drew it down to negative pressure — a sealed vacuum was the most stable holding environment he had on hand — and shoved the cell in his locker. Deal with it Monday.

Negative pressure was the part nobody had ever tried. It wasn't in any protocol. Ray didn't know that yet. He wasn't trying to discover anything. He was trying to go home.

When he came back, the locker was glowing.

He opened it. Inside the sealed cell, the crystals had not simply sat there. They had changed state — not back into DarkX, into something nobody had ever logged: a fine vapor, turning, with nowhere to go inside the sealed casing. As he watched, the vapor began to organize itself into a slow vortex.

That was when he felt it. Not heard — felt. A low resonance below the threshold of hearing, the sensation of a radio scanning through static for a frequency just out of range, of a node reaching toward a network not quite in phase. He stood with his hand near the cell and felt the thing turning inside it.

So he took it home. He didn't log it. He didn't tell anyone. He put the cell in his bag, walked out with it, and set it on his kitchen table where he could watch it.

The vortex kept turning. And it was charging the cell.

Forty-five days later, the mist inside cleared. New crystals had formed on the interior of the cell's base. The cycle had completed itself, and the FuelCell read fully charged. He had felt the whole thing happen — first in a steel locker, then on his kitchen table — without instruments, without authorization, without any preparation except having been too lazy to walk across campus on a Friday.

Forty-five days was too long to be useful and too strange to leave alone. He ran it again, and again, changing one variable at a time. The breakthrough was the cold. When he swapped the nitrogen atmosphere for liquid nitrogen, sealed inside the cell under the same negative pressure, the cycle collapsed from forty-five days to eleven. He sat with that number for a long time before he wrote it down.



He ran it a dozen more times, every way he could think to break it. It held every time. Eleven days, sealed, negative pressure, liquid nitrogen: a FuelCell that charged itself. It was the thing Alpha had spent four years and a fortune failing to do, and he had done it by accident, because he hadn't wanted to walk across campus on a Friday.

He sat with that until the numbers stopped moving.

Then he went into the lab the next morning and told his supervisor he needed time on the main testbed.



The conference room was windowless and over-lit — the flat fluorescent kind that
made everyone look like they hadn't slept. The table was long, the coffee
untouched, the air recycled to the point where it tasted like nothing at all.

He walked them through it in the order he'd discovered it. The spare cell. The negative-pressure seal nobody had ever tried. The vapor, the vortex, the way the cell charged itself. The forty-five-day cycle, and the liquid nitrogen that brought it down to eleven. He had photographs. He had numbers. He showed them the numbers in the clear, careful way he'd always showed them things — the same way he'd presented three years of stabilization data — and watched their faces.

His supervisor's face did something that was, unmistakably, surprise. The program had spent years trying to make Darker Matter crystals do almost anything. They had tried them a hundred ways and gotten inert weight every time. Nobody had ever gotten a cell to charge itself, to generate, to make energy out of crystals under a sealed negative-pressure load — they had not believed the crystals could do it at all. The surprise lasted about four seconds.

Then the man next to his supervisor — introduced as a representative from Alpha's executive division, whose name Ray did not catch — said nothing and showed nothing, which was its own kind of expression.

"Where did you run the initial test?" his supervisor asked.

Ray told him.

The room went quiet in a specific way.

He was fired that afternoon. Not for taking the sample — the sample was not mentioned. He was fired for, as the paperwork stated, a failure to adhere to research protocols and the unauthorized dissemination of proprietary findings. He was told, in language that was careful without being explicit, that the research he had conducted was the property of Alpha Corporation and that any further discussion of his findings — internally, externally, in any medium — would be treated as a breach of his existing non-disclosure agreement and prosecuted accordingly.

He was escorted out. The man from the executive division walked him to the door himself, and on the way he said — quietly, almost pleasantly, the way another man might mention the weather — that people who respected proprietary boundaries tended to live long, uneventful lives, and that he sincerely hoped Ray intended to live one. It was not careful language anymore. It was the other kind, and they both heard it land.

He walked home.

He had been alone with it for three weeks when Red and Squirrel found him.



Ray's apartment had gotten smaller since the firing. The oak table, scarred from
years of restless nights, held the FuelCell under a blanket and not much else. The place smelled like
cold coffee and the ramen packets he'd been living on since the firing — the
foil wrappers stacked in a neat pile next to the sink because he was the kind of
man who let his laundry climb the bedroom chair for a week but couldn't stand
trash on the counter. A single light hung overhead. The faint hum of the lamp was the only sound.

In front of him, the stolen FuelCell gleamed dully under the light. He stared
at it, half-expecting it to give him answers. The thought of selling it tugged at
him — the black market would pay a good price. But the longer he looked at it,
the more something twisted in his gut — the same thing that had twisted when
he'd first seen the charge readout, the thing he had been trying for three weeks
to kill and couldn't.

He hadn't realized how small his apartment had become until he looked up and the
two people were standing in it.

He had not heard a knock. He had not heard the door. Squirrel was just inside it,
heavy and unhurried; Red was already further into the room, her gaze sweeping the
space with a quiet intensity that made Ray's skin prickle. They were not the kind
of people who fidgeted. They had come in the way they did everything — without
announcing it — and now they stopped and waited, and the quality of their waiting
was very different from the waiting of civilians.

They didn't sit. They didn't move toward the couch half-covered in old blankets.
Instead, they positioned themselves near the table — not threatening, exactly,
but settled in the way of people who intend to conclude this conversation before
leaving.

Squirrel didn't waste words. "We have a window of about seventy-two hours before
Alpha sends someone who isn't going to knock. You're already on a list, Ray.
You've been on it since the night you told your department head."

Ray looked at them. "You people have been watching me."

"We've been keeping you alive," Red said. "There's a difference."

"Not from where I'm sitting."

"I understand that." She pulled out a chair and sat down across from him —
unhurried, like she was prepared to be here a while. "What you found in that
locker didn't stop being important because they took it from you. Alpha knows
the discovery exists. They know you made it. And they have built a significant
amount of their current business model on the fact that the rest of the world
does not know what you know."

Ray's hand moved toward the blanket on the table, then stopped. He looked at
Squirrel. "What do you actually want from me?"

"We want you alive and working," Squirrel said. "We have a place for you to
hide and a new identity to protect you."

"And in exchange?"

"You keep doing what you were doing. The research doesn't stop — it just stops
belonging to people who bury it."

Ray was quiet. Outside the window, a car passed and did not slow down and its
headlights swept the ceiling of his apartment. He had been watching headlights
for three weeks, logging which ones slowed down.

"You understand what the cell actually did," Red said. It was not a question.
"Alpha thinks they understand it. They're harvesting the output — the crystals,
the yield, the energy the conversion cycle produces. They built their whole
operation around the output." She leaned forward. "They never figured out what
they're actually dealing with. They only ever saw it as a container."

"They saw what they expected to see," Ray said, in spite of himself.

"Yes."

He looked at the cell under the blanket. "I packed the crystals in there because
it was convenient. The negative pressure, the nitrogen atmosphere — I thought it
was just a stable environment. I wasn't trying to trigger a reaction. I was
trying to avoid paperwork."

"And that's exactly why they can't reproduce it," Squirrel said. "Alpha's
standard containment runs nitrogen. Open cycle — nitrogen atmosphere, no seal
modification. Every lab in the program. Every researcher who ever handled Darker
Matter crystals ran the same protocol." He looked at Red, then back at Ray. "Nobody sealed a cell under negative pressure. It wasn't in any protocol."

"I added it as a shortcut," Ray said.

"You added it because you were trying to avoid a walk across campus. That single
variable — the seal — is why they've been failing for two years trying to
reproduce what you found in a locker on a Tuesday."

Ray pulled the blanket away from the FuelCell. He hadn't meant to do it — his
hands made the decision before his head caught up. The cell sat on the table,
dull and inert in the lamplight.

"I know how the cells work at eleven days," he said. "That's the window Alpha
operates on. Every cycle they crack the cells open to pull the crystals back
out — depleting them, they call it — and they lose about ten percent to vapor
the moment the seal breaks, every single time. Then they reset and run it
again. They never let one sit." He reached into the drawer and pulled out a
battered notebook. Several pages were covered in diagrams, heat maps, rough
models of accumulation behavior. "What I don't know is what happens if you
don't reset. If you don't deplete it at all. If you just leave it sealed and
walk away."

Red looked at the notebook.

"I need proper instruments," Ray said. His voice had changed. He was not frightened anymore. He was precise, and precision was, for him, a kind of territory. "A controlled environment. I need to test what happens if you don't reset. I cannot do that here."

Squirrel looked at Red. Something passed between them — not surprise. Recognition. The kind of look two people exchange when the person across the table has just described exactly the gap they've been trying to fill.

"That's why we're sending you inside," Squirrel said.

Red pulled a card from her pocket and set it on the table — plain, no logo, a
handwritten address. "Tonight. Flight out. New name. New country."

"Tonight."

"The window closes tomorrow morning."

Ray picked up the card. He set it back down. He picked it up again and held it.

He looked at the FuelCell for the last time in his apartment. He put his
notebook on top of it, carefully, like he was tucking something in.

"Seventy-two hours," he said.

"Less, now."

He stood up. He went to his closet and pulled out a bag from the top shelf and
stood holding it for a moment. Then he started packing. He was not fast about it
and he was not slow. He was precise. He took the notebook. He took the fuel
cell, wrapped in the blanket. He left the loan statements on the counter.

At the door, he turned back and looked at the apartment once — the lamp still
burning, the oak table still scarred, the lamp's flicker still going. Then he
followed them out into the dark.



The flight was three hours and forty minutes. Ray slept through one of them, not by choice but because his body had been making decisions without him since the apartment.

He woke to descent. The seatbelt light. The PA announcing something in Spanish, then English, then Spanish again, as if the English had been a courtesy they'd already moved on from.

He did not have luggage. He had a backpack Red had handed him in the car — already packed, his name not on it, things inside he did not remember owning. A change of clothes. A toothbrush, still in its packaging. A paperback in a language he couldn't read. He hadn't opened it.

He came through arrivals into the main hall of Tocumen International Airport and stood still long enough that the people behind him had to move around him, which they did without comment, having seen that before.

The heat hit him outside. Not gradual — immediate, physical, the air swapping itself for something thicker. It was nine in the morning and already hotter than he'd lived in. He was wearing a jacket.

He took the jacket off.

He stood with the jacket over his arm and looked at the street in front of the terminal. Taxis, vans, a man with a handcart full of something green, someone's grandmother in a lawn chair conducting business of some kind. At the end of the pickup zone, a line of pedicabs: three-wheeled, fringed awnings, drivers who had the patient stillness of people who know how to wait and have been doing it.

He had an address on a piece of paper. Red had written it in block letters: CALLE 50, CERCA DE LA CRESTA. Below it: ASK FOR LUCIA. Below that: DON'T TAKE A CAB.

A pedicab driver made eye contact.

Ray held the eye contact too long, which was apparently sufficient.

"You go?" the driver said.

Ray showed him the paper.

The driver glanced at it, nodded once, gestured at the seat with the efficiency of someone who had done this thousands of times and found no reason to expand it. Ray sat. The driver mounted the front and started pedaling.

They entered traffic.

Traffic in Panama City operated on principles Ray did not have access to. There were lanes, but the lanes were advisory. Signals existed but were in negotiation with everything else. There was a sound system — horn, counter-horn, acknowledgment-horn, horn of general principle — that carried specific meaning to everyone who spoke it. He did not speak it.

The driver did not seem concerned. He cut between a bus and a delivery truck at a gap Ray would not have called a gap, banked hard left on a street that appeared to be going right, threaded a roundabout by logic Ray couldn't identify, and came out onto a boulevard lined with glass towers that threw the morning back at everything beneath them.

The city was big. This was the thing he hadn't expected — that it would be this operational, this indifferent to his arrival. He had not thought about Panama as a place. It had been a word on a piece of paper for two days. Now it was a city going about its morning in heat he hadn't lived in and languages he didn't speak and at a pace the pedicab was matching without effort.

A bus pulled alongside and blew its horn for reasons that weren't apparent.

He grabbed the side rail.

The driver looked back. "It's okay," he said. "Bus always does."

They turned off the boulevard into streets that were narrower and lower, the buildings closer together, the sound differently textured — generators, music through open windows, a saw running somewhere up a side street. A woman leaned from a second-floor window and called something down to someone Ray couldn't see. A dog crossed the road with the authority of something that had done it many times and trusted cars to have figured it out by now.

The pedicab slowed on a block of storefronts — a pharmacy, a tienda, something selling fabric, something selling something he couldn't identify — and stopped in front of a door painted green. Not recently green. The green of something that had been green long enough to mean it.

"Calle Cincuenta," the driver said. "Near La Cresta. This one."

Ray got out. He gave the driver the money Red had put in his jacket pocket — he didn't know how much, he just gave him all of it. The driver looked at it, gave some back, and pedaled away.

Ray stood in front of the green door.

He was going to knock on it and ask for Lucia. He was going to do that in a moment. He was standing here first because this was the first time since the apartment that he'd been somewhere without someone telling him where to be, and he needed to use it.

He thought about the FuelCell on his table. About the number. About the look on their faces when he'd shown Alpha what he'd found — the moment they understood what it meant for them, and the speed with which they'd decided to bury it and bury him with it. He thought about Red and Squirrel in his apartment doorway at six in the morning, with the car already running.

He was in Panama. He was alive. He was standing in front of a green door in a city he'd never been to, holding nothing that belonged to him, about to knock and ask for a woman named Lucia.

He knocked.



Aboard the Meridian

What Kate had not put in her seven-sentence relay was what
happened after she hit send.

She sat for eleven minutes.

The ship-day lighting ran at the same level regardless of hour
— the Meridian didn't track a planetary cycle. The station at
0300 looked identical to the station at 1400. Kate had stopped
noticing this. She had started noticing it again after San
Francisco.

Dayo found her there when he came on shift. He read the
confirmation on her screen and stood for a moment — not rushing,
not looking away, locating the feeling precisely, putting it
somewhere it could be carried.

"Her droplet's on the wall," Kate said.

"I know."

"I put it there after we lost her. I didn't ask anyone."

"You didn't need to ask." He sat in the secondary comms chair.
"Petyr asleep?"

"Yes."

"Good." He paused. "A droplet's not a rank. It's the crew saying
we saw you. Sharon never wanted in — ran her own game, for her own
reasons, and turned us down every time we asked. But she knew the
work and she knew what it was for." He looked at the wall. "You put
it up because someone should mark that we knew her."

Kate said nothing.

"We're going to get the data out," she said. Not a comfort.
A statement of intent.

"Yes," Dayo said. "We are."

Outside the viewport, Saturn's rings caught refracted sunlight
and held it — a thin, bright edge in the dark. The ship at this
hour made no sound you could name. Only the held absence
of it: the held-breath quiet that had been held so long it had
become the normal texture of the air. Kate had stopped noticing
it. She noticed it now.

"The network needs people who choose it," Kate said.

"Sharon opened the door," Dayo said. "Whether the sister walks the
rest of the way through is up to the sister. That part was never
ours to assign."
