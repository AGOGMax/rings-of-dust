RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Ten

Behind the Curtain

The badge was laminated. Ray turned it over in his hands, noting the weight, the
specific sheen of the photo beneath the protective coating. The name read MIKOV
FORSINKO. The face in the photo was his.

"The contractor database entry went live six hours ago," Squirrel said. "Backstory
is clean. Eighteen months of consulting work for two Alpha-adjacent firms in São
Paulo, both of which are real and both of which have been — let's say — made
receptive to confirming the relationship if anyone calls."

Red was at the window, watching the street below. The safe house was a two-room
flat above a laundry operation in the Marbella district, which was the kind of
neighborhood that was neither nice enough to attract attention nor rough enough
to generate it. Laundry was going below. Ray could hear the drums through the
floor. The air smelled like detergent and hot cotton — the laundry's heat rising
through the floorboards, making the room warmer than it should have been for the
hour.

Red turned from the window. She reached into her jacket and produced something
small — folded dark fabric, a wrench worked into it in fine silver thread. She
set it on the table beside the Forsinko badge.

Silver. Rendered in outline, small enough to vanish into a seam.

"Senior Technician," Red said. "You're not an informant anymore. You're not
adjacent. You're in."

Ray picked it up. It weighed almost nothing. He turned it over in his fingers —
the stitching was hand-done, careful, the kind of work someone had spent time
on.

"How many ranks," he said.

"Seven," Squirrel said. "Informants at the bottom — they pass along what they
see, some of them don't even know they're doing it. Operators move the
intelligence. Technicians build, hack, steal, redistribute — that's you now.
Agents set up operations and move people. Navigators run missions. Commanders
move the big pieces." He paused. "And above all of it, there's a rank nobody
talks about."

"What rank."

"Grand Admiral." Squirrel's mouth did something that was not quite a smile.
"One person. One rank. Nobody's ever seen the patch. Half the network thinks
it's a myth the Navigators made up to give the organization weight." He
shrugged. "Right. And I'm the Grand Admiral."

Red, from the window: "Squirrel, if you were the Grand Admiral, I would
start making soap at the colony."

Ray looked at the wrench patch in his hand. He folded it and put it in his
breast pocket, the way he'd seen Red carry hers — inside the jacket, against the
lining, invisible unless you knew to look.

He noticed, without quite knowing why, that Red's own patch was the same symbol
— a wrench — but rendered in gold thread instead of silver. Lead Technician. A
tier above the rank she had just handed him.

"Your specialization is fuel-cell stabilization engineering," Red said without
turning around. "That gets you lab access at the mid-tier. Not the vault. But
the vault isn't where you need to be."

Ray set the badge down on the table next to his notebook. "And if someone runs a
technical deep-dive? Asks me to explain something I don't actually know?"

"Then you explain that the methodology you worked with in São Paulo was
proprietary and you're under NDA." Squirrel sat down across from him. "But that
won't happen. You're a consultant, not a hire. They want your output, not your
credentials. The whole point of bringing in outside contractors is that nobody
has to own the gaps."

Ray considered this. The logic was sound. Corporate epistemology worked exactly
this way: consultants existed in a categorical space where their knowledge was
assumed and therefore never verified. He had navigated this structure his entire
career from the other side. Now he was using it.

"Okay," he said.

Squirrel looked at him for a moment, as if waiting for something more.

There wasn't anything more. This was a protocol. Ray understood protocols.

---

Alpha Panama occupied a forty-story tower on the eastern edge of the Casco
district, where the old colonial architecture stopped and the twenty-first
century asserted itself in glass and exposed steel. The building's ground floor
lobby was the size of a small airport terminal, and it had been designed to
produce the same sensations: the ceilings were high enough to make you aware of
your own dimensions. The floor was polished stone that reflected the overhead
lighting in long pale columns. Every surface was clean in a way that suggested
staff had been specifically tasked with the maintenance of cleanliness, which is
different from surfaces that are simply clean. The air was cooled to a
temperature that had been decided in a meeting. It smelled like nothing, which
was its own kind of smell — the absence of everything a city puts in the air,
purchased at the cost of a ventilation system the size of a bus.

Ray stood on the sidewalk and looked up at the building. Forty stories of glass and institutional ambition. The front entrance of the company that had fired him and threatened to kill him. He was wearing the badge of a man who did not exist, holding credentials that had been real for six hours, and he was about to walk through the front door.

"You've gotta be shitting me," he said, to no one.

He scanned in at the exterior gate with the Mikov Forsinko badge. The reader
beeped once, green, and he was through.

He noted: seven badge readers between the exterior gate and the elevator bank.
Cameras at intervals of roughly eight feet along the main corridor, angled to
produce overlapping coverage with no dead zones he could identify from a walking
pass. Staff moved through the lobby with the calibrated efficiency of people who
had been, over time, implicitly and explicitly rewarded for moving efficiently.
They walked at a consistent pace. They did not linger. Nobody was on their
phone. The behavior was not robotic — it was more specific than that. It was
optimized. You could see the optimization in the way a person held their badge
against a reader before the door had fully come into range, anticipating the
beep. You could see it in the way two researchers passed each other in the
corridor without breaking stride, their acknowledgment of each other's existence
compressed into a single nod.

The building was designed to make you feel small. That was one of its functions
and it worked. But Ray noticed something else operating simultaneously: the
materials were expensive, the lines were elegant, the signage used a typeface
that communicated precision and authority. The building also made you feel that
being inside it meant something. That you were part of something with resources.
With ambition. With a certain kind of importance.

Both effects were intentional.

He rode the elevator to the fourteenth floor.

---

Dr. Vince Oren was shorter than Ray had expected from the name, which was not a
rational thing to notice but Ray noticed it anyway. He was perhaps fifty-five,
with close-cut gray hair and the steady quality of attention that marks
scientists who have spent decades actually listening to the problems in front of
them rather than performing the act of listening. He came around his desk to
shake Ray's hand rather than waiting for Ray to come to him.

"Dr. Forsinko. Good to have you." He gestured toward a chair. "Coffee?"

"Please. And it's Mikov." Ray offered it the way you offer a thing you want
taken — easily, already decided. "Nobody's called me Dr. Forsinko since the
paperwork went through."

"Vince, then." He poured from a carafe on his desk without asking for
specifications, handed Ray a cup — black, which was correct — and sat down with
his own. "The doctor part wears off around the second pot in here anyway. São
Paulo firms don't usually send us people who work on the stabilization side.
That's usually handled in-house. I was curious about the request."

"My understanding was you had a throughput bottleneck in the batch processing
stage."

"We do." Vince nodded. "Third shift is running about twelve percent below
projection. The models say it's a crystallization rate issue — the yield windows
are tightening. But the models are based on assumptions about the feedstock
purity that I'm not entirely confident in." He paused. "I'd rather have someone
look at the process fresh. People who have been here five years see what they
expect to see."

"That's usually how it works," Ray said.

Vince smiled. It reached his eyes and then retreated — there and gone, the way
real ones were. "Let me show you around."

The lab was large and climate-controlled, divided into sections by clear
partitions. The air tasted faintly metallic, the specific signature of
plasma-adjacent work. Ray knew this smell from his own lab, from the years
before. There were twelve researchers working across two adjacent sections, and
they moved with focused concentration. The equipment was newer than anything Ray
had worked with. Considerably newer. The centrifuge array alone represented a
capital investment he could not calculate from the outside but could estimate
from its output capacity at somewhere between eight and twelve million dollars.

Vince walked him through the setup with the easy familiarity of someone who had
designed it. He explained the negative-pressure chambers, the nitrogen cycling,
the batch-log interface. He introduced Ray to two junior researchers — a young
woman named Anushka who nodded without looking up from her console, and a man
named Chen who asked Ray two technical questions that were sharp enough to
confirm he was the genuine article, not a greeter. Ray answered them correctly.
Chen seemed satisfied.

"The batch logs are here," Vince said, pointing to a wall-mounted terminal near
the back of the lab. "Pull access is part of your standard contractor clearance.
If you need write access to flag anomalies you'll need to submit a request, but
I can expedite that."

"Pull access is fine to start," Ray said. "I'll want to review three months of
throughput data before I form any opinions."

"Exactly the right instinct." Vince clapped him once on the shoulder — not a
territorial gesture, genuinely collegial — and returned to his own work.

Ray turned to the terminal and began to read.

---

The proprietary stabilization process was not what he had imagined.

He had imagined it the way everyone outside Alpha imagined it: some closely
held technical innovation, years of refinement, a process that would require
significant reverse-engineering to reproduce. He had assumed the secrecy meant
complexity. He had assumed that walls of that height protected something
proportionally valuable.

What he found instead was a crystallization protocol that was competent,
well-optimized, and entirely derivable from first principles by any team of
moderately skilled researchers given six months and a reasonable equipment
budget. The negative-pressure parameters were standard. The nitrogen cycling was
efficient but not novel. The yield windows they were using — eleven days, always
eleven days — were the obvious sweet spot for the extraction economics they had
built their model around.

There was no secret ingredient. There was no breakthrough. There was a standard
process, executed with the precision that comes from doing something exactly
this way ten thousand times in a row.

Ray worked through this understanding methodically. It did not make him angry.
He noted it the way he noted anything: as a data point. The data point was this:
Alpha's hold on the DarkX market was not a product of superior science. It
was a product of superior lawyers. They had acquired, at the right moment, the
patents that covered the obvious implementation paths. They had signed the right
supply agreements. They had structured the right exclusivity contracts with the
Saturn harvesting operations. The wall they had built was not a wall of
brilliance.

It was a wall of paperwork.

The energy crisis, as a technical problem, had been solvable for at least a
decade.

Ray read the sentence twice. He made a small mark in the margin. Kept
reading.

---

The months made him into Mikov Forsinko. Not all at once — the way a name
becomes yours, by being said. He learned the bus that ran along Balboa, the
fruit vendor who set aside the good papayas, the particular green smell of the
city after the afternoon rain that arrived at the same hour like an appointment.
He went home to a small flat with a fan that ticked. He read the batch logs by
day and wrote in the notebook by night, and the notebook got heavier by the
season, not the week. This was a long con measured in years, and Ray, who had
always been patient with data, found he could be patient with this too.

Red and Squirrel were a city away and a phone call closer than that. Red turned
out to be from somewhere cold she wouldn't name and to have opinions about every
meal she was served. Squirrel had a habit of answering a question four minutes
after it was asked, having apparently needed the time. Somewhere in the second
year Ray realized he trusted them — not operationally, which had come fast, but
the other way: the way you trust people you've been bored and scared and
unimpressed with in the same small rooms. They were the only three people on
Earth who knew who he actually was. It made a kind of family out of the secret.

---

The long-cycle data was buried three directories deep under a project
designation he did not recognize: EXTENDED_HORIZON_ANALYSIS. He found it
because he was methodical and because the indexing structure had a gap between
Q2 and Q4 that suggested Q3 had been archived rather than deleted — and
archived data, in his experience, was where the things you weren't supposed
to find actually lived.

Inside were 47 cell-monitoring records from cells that had not been
terminated at the eleven-day window. Some were accidents — containment
failures, scheduling errors, batch records that had fallen through the log.
Some were deliberate: an internal research project, nine months long, that
had been run in a sealed sub-lab and then classified at a level that should
have prevented its appearance in a pull-access terminal.

Someone had made an indexing error. Or someone had wanted it findable. Ray
did not know which and did not have time to determine it.

He read the yield curves.

The data had a structure he recognized immediately — the standard eleven-day
harvest window, the expected depletion curve, the nominal yield per cell. He
had run these numbers himself for three years. He skimmed past them.

What stopped him was the linked-cell column.

A subset of the test batches had been run with the cells in contact —
physically coupled, the housing ports connected so the crystalline
environments communicated. The research team had included this as a secondary
variable. It was not the main study.

It was everything.

At thirty-three days of continuous linkage, the internal crystal formation
rate was not elevated by a small margin. It was ten times the standard. The
Darker Matter structure, when cells were left coupled past the eleven-day
harvest threshold, did not stabilize or deplete — it accelerated. Tenfold.
And the acceleration did not plateau. It kept climbing for as long as the
cells stayed connected — and then Alpha's own data simply stopped, mid-curve,
at the edge of what they had been willing to test.

He read that line twice. The number had nowhere to go but up, and no one had
followed it.

He sat with this for a moment.

Alpha had known about this for at least nine months. They had run the data,
understood what it meant, and classified it.

He scrolled to the end of the file. The last entry was a single line, a
recommendation from the lead researcher: Proceed to Saturn field test.
Recommend continuous-linkage protocol.

The entry had no response logged. No approval. No denial. Just the
recommendation, sitting there, with a timestamp from eleven months ago,
never acted on.

---

The apartment had become a place where the three of them talked at night, after
Ray's shift, with the laundry gone quiet below. Red sat on the floor with her
back to the couch. Squirrel ate something out of a tin.

"Thirty-three days, linked, and the inside yield goes to ten times," Ray said.
"And then it just keeps going. Alpha stopped measuring. They never leave a cell
linked longer than they have to, because every day a cell sits is a day they're
not harvesting it." He turned his cup without drinking from it. "But here's the
part I can't stop thinking about. If time does that to the curve — what does
gravity?"

Squirrel stopped chewing. "Say it the simple way."

"Every test in that building runs in standard gravity, on a planet, under a
ceiling nobody's noticed is a ceiling. The crystal forms against the pull. I
think the pull is the limit." He looked at them. "Take it off-world. Leave it
linked. No reset, no gravity. I think the curve never levels out at all. I think
it breaks."

"You think," Red said.

"I can't prove it. Alpha hasn't done either — they've never left a cell linked
long enough to find the top, and they've never run one in zero gravity. They had
no reason to. The model that pays them is the eleven-day model." He set the cup
down. "Somebody would have to actually leave it alone, in orbit, and watch.
Nobody has ever just watched."

---

The anomaly records were now legible to him in a way they hadn't been twenty
minutes ago.

The batch logs used a status field he had initially skimmed past — not a flag
exactly, more a secondary classification for high-variance yields. He had
assumed it was a quality tier. He looked at it more carefully now.

The field appeared at irregular intervals. Not random — irregular. When he
filtered for it, he got one entry for approximately every seven hundred and
forty standard batches.

Each of these entries documented a single cell that had, on its eleven-day
review, produced something no model predicted: a one-time external crystal
deposit on the housing — dense, structured, extending outward from the base.
Not internal yield. Not a recurring flush. Once, on roughly one cell in seven
hundred and forty, and never on the same cell twice.

One researcher, in a handwritten annotation attached to a 2030 batch record,
had used a different word.

Jackpot.

The yield from a single Jackpot deposit was enormous — the exterior crystal ran
to roughly three hundred and thirty-three times the mass that had gone into
assembling the cell. Alpha tracked these separately. They did not appear in the
public supply figures.

They had been watching for the conditions that produced a Jackpot for years.
They had never understood what those conditions were — the events were rare,
scattered, impossible to force. Ray didn't understand them either, not yet. But
he wrote the number down and underlined it, because a one-in-seven-forty event
that gave back three hundred and thirty-three times its input was not noise. It
was a door, and nobody had found the handle.

Ray cross-referenced the anomaly records against the grant filings he had
found in the public database last week. Alpha had filed, over the previous
seven years, nine separate government research grants for the development of
what their applications described as next-generation fuel-cell stabilization
technology. Combined, the grants totaled several billion dollars, drawn from
energy crisis relief funds allocated by the Federation Council.

The technology they were describing in the grant applications was Ray's
process. His recipe. His parameters — the negative-pressure chambers, the
nitrogen cycling, the eleven-day window. Renamed. Reformatted. Resubmitted
with incremental variations small enough to constitute new filings and large
enough to maintain the appearance of ongoing research.

Alpha had been paid billions of dollars to develop technology that already
existed. Technology that a junior researcher had discovered by accident in a work
locker. Technology that the company had then taken, patented, and presented
to the Federation Council as the product of years of sophisticated R&D.

He added it to the notebook without commentary. The notebook was getting heavy.

---

Vince invited him to a thing at his apartment after the third-quarter numbers
came in — "not optional, Mikov, the third quarter is the only thing worth
celebrating in this business." There was real food, which during the energy
crisis still meant something. Chen argued with him about lattice geometry for an
hour and was right about half of it. Anushka, who never looked up from her
console in the lab, turned out to be funny in the loud, sideways way of people
who spend all day being quiet — she did an impression of the building's
ventilation system that made Ray laugh before he could decide whether Mikov
Forsinko was a man who laughed.

That was the part he hadn't planned for.

He had planned for the lying. He was good at the lying; it was just protocol
with stakes. What he had not planned for was liking them — Chen's sharp
questions, Anushka's ventilation impression, Vince refilling his glass and
asking, with real interest, what São Paulo had been like. These were not the
architects of the machine. They were its hands, and the hands didn't know. He
sat in Vince's apartment with good food in front of him and a false name in his
mouth and felt the first real edge of it: that getting the truth out was going
to cost these specific, decent people something, and they would never understand
why, and he was going to let it happen anyway.

He wrote nothing about the party in the notebook. Some things he kept off the
record even from himself.

---

It took him two and a half shifts to find the batch destruction logs.

He was not looking for them. He was auditing throughput across Q3 and Q4 of the
previous year, following Vince's hypothesis about feedstock purity, when he found
a field in the batch records he didn't recognize. The field was labeled
DISPOSAL_PROTOCOL_11 and it appeared at irregular but recurring intervals across
the dataset. He filtered for it.

Three hundred and twelve entries in an eighteen-month window.

Each entry recorded a quantity of Darker Matter crystals, a timestamp, and a
status code. The status codes were internal Alpha nomenclature he didn't
immediately know, but they were consistent, which meant they were systematic,
which meant he could decode them from context. He spent forty minutes doing
this. When he had the schema, he read the entries again.

DISPOSAL_PROTOCOL_11 was not a contamination flag. It was not a quality-control
rejection. The quantities attached to it were not anomalously small or large.
They tracked, with a consistency that was striking in its precision, at roughly
eleven to thirteen percent of each batch's usable yield.

Alpha was destroying product.

Not spoilage. Not error. Consistent, scheduled, deliberate destruction of viable
Darker Matter crystals representing eleven to thirteen percent of everything this
facility produced.

Ray sat with this for thirty seconds. He thought about the supply charts he had
memorized in the weeks since leaving Alpha. Global DarkX supply had run at
approximately four to eight percent below projected demand for four and a half
years. The energy crisis was, in the language of economists, a
demand-side problem: there was not enough supply to meet demand at any
reasonable price point, which drove prices up, which made DarkX unaffordable for
most of the world's population outside of priority industrial contracts.

Eleven to thirteen percent of production destroyed per batch. Four to eight
percent supply gap.

The math was not subtle.

He thought, briefly and precisely, about Dr. Vince. About whether Vince knew. He
looked across the lab to where Vince was bent over a console, reading something,
and he considered the man's face: the quality of attention, the genuine
curiosity, the enthusiasm with which he had explained the nitrogen cycling this
morning. He thought about what it meant to be a good scientist working inside a
machine without knowing what the machine was for.

It was then that the terminal did something small and wrong — a status line he
hadn't seen before, bottom corner: ACCESS LOGGED — TIER REVIEW PENDING. The
sealed archive had a tripwire on it. Of course it did. He had just stepped on it.

He did not hurry. He opened the drive housing on the terminal's secondary port
and inserted the Collective drive — the size of a thumbnail, the color of the
housing it went into — and pulled everything: the linked-cell file, the grant
filings, the disposal logs. The copy bar crawled. His pulse did not. Then he
took out his phone and sent the one line he and Squirrel had agreed on months
ago, for exactly this and nothing else: Dinner tonight. The usual place. It
meant: I'm burned. I'm coming out. Be at the spot.

He pocketed the drive and the notebook and stood up.

Then he made his own chaos.

On his way toward the corridor he stopped at the nitrogen-cycling bank in
14-C — the unit Vince had walked him through on his first morning, whose manual
pressure-bleed he had logged, the way he logged everything worth keeping — and opened it a
quarter-turn past tolerance. The unit complained, then failed the way it was
built to fail: safely, loudly, with the distinctive three-pulse containment tone
that meant a pressure fault in a cycling chamber. Not catastrophic. Not
dangerous. But by protocol the floor dropped straight into incident mode — all
non-essential personnel to staging, all lab-adjacent doors to lock-and-hold, all
badge readers elevating to tier-two on a countdown.

It would seal the floor in about two minutes. It would also, for those two
minutes, turn the room into exactly the kind of practiced, preoccupied scramble
that nobody watches a contractor walk out of.

Two minutes. He had planned for ninety seconds.

Anushka was moving toward the designated staging area near the north wall. Chen
had already crossed the partition into the adjacent section, presumably to
assist with the containment. Three other researchers were moving in the same
direction as Anushka, unhurried, the way people move through a practiced drill.

Vince was coming toward Ray.

"Nitrogen coupling failed in 14-C," Vince said. "Routine. We'll be in hold for
probably twenty minutes." He gestured toward the staging area. "Come on, I'll
finish walking you through the Q4 anomalies while we wait."

Ray did not go toward the staging area.

"I need to use the restroom," he said. "Before the hold locks me in for twenty
minutes."

Vince smiled. "Corridor B, left at the junction. You have maybe two minutes
before it locks."

"I'll be quick," Ray said.

He walked at the pace of a man who intended to return.

---

Corridor B was clear. He turned left at the junction and kept his pace even,
noting the badge readers on the doors he passed: the first was already showing
amber, cycling toward red. He did not use Mikov Forsinko's card on that one.

The second door, forty feet further, was still green. He scanned through and
kept moving.

He used the card on one more door after that — a maintenance corridor that ran
parallel to the main shaft, which he had mapped on his first walk-through by the
simple expedient of noticing where the maintenance staff went when they
disappeared from view. The door opened. He walked through and descended two
flights of stairs into a service level that smelled of refrigerant and insulated
cable.

The loading bay exit was at the west end. A man in a gray Alpha uniform was
moving a pallet loader toward the freight elevator, his back to the door. Ray
walked past him at a consistent pace, badge in hand though there was no reader
on this door, and pushed through the bay door into the late afternoon.

Panama City in May at four in the afternoon smells of exhaust and something
green and wet under it — the vegetable richness of a city that is
fighting a constant battle against tropical encroachment and losing it at the
margins. The light was golden and horizontal. The noise was immediate and
specific: traffic, a vendor's amplified radio somewhere to the right, the
compressed-air grunt of a delivery truck two blocks up.

Ray walked.

He did not move fast. A man moving fast from a loading bay draws attention. He
moved at the pace of a contractor who has just finished a shift and is thinking
about dinner. He held his badge lanyard loosely. He did not look behind him.

He was three blocks north before he turned into a side street and allowed
himself to stop.

He stood in the doorway of a hardware store. The smell in here was specific and
known: machine oil, rubber gasket material, the dense dustiness of packed
shelving. Through the window, he could see a man behind a counter watching
something on a screen. Normal afternoon. Nothing looking back at him.

His hands were shaking. He noted this without particular interest. It was the
body's delayed accounting for the last fifteen minutes, which was a known
phenomenon. He put his hands in his pockets, feeling the drive in the right one
and the notebook's corner digging into his ribs from where he had it wedged
under his arm.

Six blocks east. That was the extraction point. He had forty minutes before Red and Squirrel's window closed.

He started walking.

He thought about Vince.

He thought about Vince the way you think about a fact you have not yet fully
integrated: turning it over, testing its edges. Vince was not performing
goodness. He was good, in the way that some people are simply good, the way some
scientists are simply curious — not because it is useful but because it is the
thing they are made of. He would go back to his lab after the nitrogen cycling
incident was resolved and he would work on his yield models and he would think,
in whatever part of his mind processed such things, that he was contributing to
something important. To the science of energy production. To the world's power
supply. To the careful, careful enterprise of making the crystals stable and the
yields predictable.

He would never see the DISPOSAL_PROTOCOL_11 entries. Not because they were hidden
from him — Ray did not think they were hidden from Vince. He thought they simply
existed in a part of the system that Vince had never had reason to look at,
because Vince's job was the science and someone else's job was the numbers and
the system was designed so that those two things never needed to be in the same
room.

That was the design. Not malice toward Vince, specifically. Just the ordinary
architecture of a machine that works better when its parts don't know what the
other parts are doing.

Ray thought about the junior researcher at Alpha who had first noticed that
a certain class of feedstock was yielding anomalous results. He thought about
the report that researcher had almost certainly written. He thought about where
that report had gone and who had read it and what decision had been made about
it and how far upstream that decision would have had to travel before it reached
someone who understood not just what the finding meant scientifically but what
it meant commercially.

Someone had understood. Someone had calculated the value of that finding against
the value of the current model and had made a decision, and that decision had
been the right one from the perspective of the current model's beneficiaries.

The energy crisis was not a crisis in the sense of something that had gone
wrong.

It was a product. It had been designed, maintained, and optimized over four and
a half years with the same care and precision that Vince applied to his yield models.

Ray walked east through the Panama City late afternoon. The smell of the city
was around him: exhaust, green, something frying from an open window two floors
up. He was sweating in the heat and his hands had stopped shaking. In his pocket
the drive was the size of his thumbnail and it contained enough data to
demonstrate everything he had just been thinking in terms that a court, a
journalist, or a historian could use.

He thought: nobody in that building thinks they are doing something wrong.

He thought: that is the most frightening thing about all of this.

Not the calculation. Not the destroyed crystals. Not the four and a half years and the
billions of people who had paid for it in energy costs and cold winters and
factories that couldn't run. The most frightening thing was Vince, and Chen
asking sharp questions about crystallization rates, and Anushka not looking up
from her console. The most frightening thing was a building full of careful,
competent, genuinely dedicated people who went home at the end of each shift and
slept well, because the machine they worked inside had been constructed so that
none of them ever had to know what the machine was for.

The extraction point was a café awning two blocks ahead. He could see Squirrel's
jacket through the crowd.

He kept walking.

At the café, Squirrel did not look at him when he sat down. Red had a coffee
in front of her and was watching the street. The café ran onto the sidewalk —
small round tables, metal chairs, an awning that didn't quite reach the sun. The
espresso machine behind the counter hissed at intervals. A radio played
something with horns. The crowd on the sidewalk moved at the pace of a city in
late afternoon, close enough to the tables that you could smell perfume and
cigarettes and the frying oil from the vendor three doors down. Ray put the drive on the table
between them, face down, the way you set down something that has weight.

Squirrel's hand moved over it without a gesture. It was in his jacket before
anyone at the adjacent tables would have had reason to notice an exchange. Ray
caught a brief glimpse of the jacket's inner lining as it fell closed. The patch
had changed. Where the wrench had been — that first night in the Marbella safe
house — there was a different shape now: a pair of sunglasses in gold thread,
sewn flat against the lining. Agents, both of them. The network had moved them
up while he'd been inside, and kept them on him anyway. He didn't say anything
about it.

"What's on it," Red said. She was still watching the street.

"The long-cycle data," Ray said. "What happens if you link the cells together
and don't reset them at eleven days. Thirty-three days of continuous linkage:
ten times the internal yield, and still climbing — the curve never plateaus, and
Alpha's own data just stops, because they never left a cell linked long enough
to find the top." He paused. "They've known about the thirty-three-day number
for nine months. It's buried under an access tier that shouldn't have been
reachable from my clearance level. What they don't have — what nobody has yet —
is what happens if you take the ceiling off the whole thing."

Red looked at him for the first time.

"There's also documentation of the government grants," Ray said. "Seven years
of applications. Billions drawn from the crisis relief funds. They described
my process as their own ongoing research." He looked at the table. "And the
destruction logs. They've been disposing of eleven to thirteen percent of
production per batch for four and a half years to maintain the supply gap."

The table was quiet for a moment. Outside, a motorcycle passed on the street.

Red looked at Squirrel. Something moved between them — not surprise. The
particular stillness of people who have been building toward something for a
long time and have just been handed the piece that makes it load-bearing.

"That theory of yours," Squirrel said. "Zero gravity, no reset, no ceiling.
That's the piece they'd be most afraid of — if it's true."

"If it's true. And I think it is," Ray said. "You can't manufacture a shortage
of something that compounds in everyone's hands."

Squirrel turned the coffee cup once. "We're going to need a name for this.
When we move it through the network, when the parallel release goes out."
He looked at Ray. "Something that says what it is."

Ray thought about the curve that had no top — the one Alpha had stopped
measuring. About what a cell might do off-world, linked and left alone, if
anyone ever bothered to find out. A theory, still. Something that would have to
be proven somewhere there was no gravity to hold it down.

"Antigravity project," he said.

Red looked at him. "That works," she said. She went back to watching the
street. "That's what we'll call it."

Ray didn't get up. Red noticed.

"There's something else," he said. "Vince Oren. The man who runs that lab. He
doesn't know what the building is for — none of them do. Chen, Anushka, any of
them. But Vince especially." He turned the cup. "When this comes out, it's going
to land on him. He'll find out he spent his career as the careful, honest face
of a thing that was rotting people in the dark — and he'll find out the same
week the rest of the world does, with his name on it." He looked at Squirrel.
"I want to tell him first. Offer him a way out."

Red set her coffee down. "You want to walk up to an active Alpha lab director,
burn what's left of your cover, and recruit him."

"I want to give him the choice I got. Somebody knocked on my door once and told
me the truth seventy-two hours before it would have killed me." He didn't look
away. "He's a good man inside a bad machine. That used to be me."

Squirrel and Red traded the look. "Not now," Squirrel said. "Not while you're
still inside the operation. If the work moves somewhere we can't reach you from
here — we'll talk about it then." A beat. "It's not a no, Ray. It's a not-yet."
