RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Seventeen

The Locked Bay

The rings looked the same as they always did. A cold pearl with a knife laid
across its belt. Cole had stopped finding them beautiful around the third week
out from Earth, which he supposed was normal. Beauty required distance. This
close, the rings were just math — ice density, particle distribution, the column
of DarkX plasma threading the B-ring's inner edge like a bruise under skin.

He was at the command console. Sam was at her station three meters to his left.
Heavy stood at the munitions panel, which had nothing to do with munitions
today and everything to do with the drone telemetry coming back from the first
two harvesters. Bitboy moved in the far end of the cargo bay — not
patrol-moving, not assigned-task moving. Just moving. The way he'd been moving
for the last six hours.

The harvesters were deployed. The mission clock showed forty-two minutes to
primary collection. Everything was proceeding.

It was almost ordinary.

Cole watched the drone feeds and thought about nothing in particular and waited.

---

FLASHBACK — SEVEN YEARS EARLIER
Alpha Regional Briefing, Sector Five Annex

The room was the kind of room that didn't have windows. Taylor had been in
dozens of them. Identical white acoustic panels, an oval table, a presenter at
the far end who had the look of a man delivering information he hadn't
originated.

There were nine people in the room. Taylor knew five of them. Vance Black was
across the table, two seats down, with the held stillness of a man who has
already formed his opinions and is waiting to hear whether the room will catch
up.

The presenter was walking through the extraction protocols — timeline adjustments, coverage expansion into territory the Shared Access Compact still listed as a dead zone. Taylor had read all of this. The numbers were good.

Vance raised his hand. Not urgently. Just the way a man raises his hand when he
has a question he has already decided he is going to ask.

"The Compact language on dead zones," Vance said. "Clause 12-C. Are we operating
under an active exemption, or are we anticipating one?"

The presenter paused. The question was not aggressive. That was the thing about
Vance — the questions never sounded aggressive. They just implied that he had
been thinking about the implications of the thing, not just the thing itself.

"An exemption request is in process," the presenter said.

"So anticipating."

"Correct."

Vance nodded once and wrote something on the pad in front of him.

Taylor said, "The exemption is a formality. The underlying access rights predate
the Compact."

Vance looked at him. Not a hostile look. More the look of a man noting a data
point he had not expected.

"Predate it by what authority?" Vance asked.

"The 2027 development charter. Section four."

"Section four was superseded in the 2031 revisions."

"The spirit of it wasn't."

Vance held the look for one beat longer than was comfortable and then returned
to his pad.

At the end of the table, a junior analyst named Greenbaum had his hand
half-raised. He offered something — a number, a correction to one of the
coverage projections, the kind of small contribution that technically made the
analysis more accurate and that no one in the room was going to remember. Taylor
was already talking before Greenbaum finished. Not to Greenbaum. Just forward,
past him, toward the presenter. Vance had already moved to the next item on the
agenda. Greenbaum looked down at his pad and nodded as if he had agreed.

The meeting ran another forty minutes. Taylor felt good about it. The numbers
were good. The plan was sound. The exemption would come through because the
exemption always came through when the right people asked for it.

On the way out, he did not look at Vance Black.

Vance did not look at him either.

---

The intercom clicked on at 14:07.

"Crew, this is Taylor. We've got a pressure integrity flag on the cargo bay
seals before we open the drone housing. Routine pre-harvest check. I need
everyone in the cargo bay for a physical walkthrough. Should take ten minutes."

Cole looked at Sam.

Sam looked at Cole.

The message was routine. The timing was routine. Taylor's voice was the voice he
always used — measured, slightly professorial, the voice of a man who explained
things carefully because he believed that careful explanation was a kind of
virtue.

This was the moment they had agreed would come. In the conversation in Cole's
quarters three weeks ago, when Sam had laid out what she'd found in the iRIS
cache and Heavy had stood in the corner with his arms folded and Cole had
said, okay, so when it comes, we play it through. We go where he asks. We don't
spook him before we know the shape of what he's trying to do.

Sam closed her station. Heavy secured the drone telemetry panel. Bitboy was
already near the cargo bay entrance, his LED eyes a steady amber.

They went.

---

FLASHBACK — FOUR MONTHS BEFORE LAUNCH
Project Mirage, Mojave Desert

The desert spread out in every direction. After an hour on dirt roads with mountains dimly visible through the haze, Vance pulled up to a chain-link fence topped with razor wire. Behind it: a partially-demolished drive-in theater, its screen leaning like a relic. A spotless white guardhouse stood out of place against the decay.

A guard came out. Assault rifle. Lazy smile. "No movies today, sir. Turn around."

Vance flashed his badge. "You should be expecting me. Call whoever you need."

The smile dropped. "Out of the car. Hands where I can see them."

He complied slowly. The guard leveled the rifle and motioned him down.

"Face down. Arms spread."

Vance lowered himself onto the scorching dirt. The heat came through his shirt. Sand and grit stuck to his cheek. He heard the guard's knee hit the ground behind him, felt the cuffs snap.

"How considerate," Vance said, mostly to the dirt.

They kept him there while the call went in. He heard it through the guardhouse window — "We've got a Vance Black, says he's got Federation clearance." A pause. "Looks fake to me." Another pause. "Yes, sir. Understood."

The cuffs came off. "Clear to proceed, Mr. Black. Main building. Don't stray."

Vance stood, brushed the dust off his jacket. "Appreciate the warm welcome. Nothing like eating dirt to feel at home."

The guard did not smile. Vance got back in the car and drove through.

The drive-in ruins were a deliberate costume — broken concession stands, graffiti-covered ticket booths, a screen going nowhere. The modern building at the far end of the lot was the tell. Polished, clean-lined, wrong for the neighborhood. Vance parked and went in.

The lobby kept the theater's trappings: vintage posters, a dead popcorn machine, a soda dispenser with something rancid in the lines. A man in a pressed lab coat greeted him at the door.

"Dr. Leith, director of operations. Welcome to Project Mirage."

Down a narrow stairwell. Through a key-card door. A long corridor with cameras every twenty feet, a live-armed guard at the halfway mark, and a turret above the door marked Storage Room X that adjusted its aim as they walked past. Another reinforced door. A room that opened into a low-lit underground archive.

The clerk behind the desk had the posture of someone hired to be unremarkable, which was itself a kind of remark. Leith turned and walked back the way they'd come without saying anything else.

Vance showed his credentials. He asked for the Conrad files — Dr. Stephen Conrad, cross-referenced under Federation research archives, IRiS development, the Gracie nanoparticle program, years 2031 through 2038.

The clerk typed. She looked at her screen. She typed again.

"That archive was cleared," she said.

"Cleared meaning—"

"Transferred. Physical and digital. About six weeks ago."

"Where was it transferred?"

Another pause. She was reading something, and whatever she was reading required
her to arrange her face carefully. "Alpha Corp. Authorization came through
Federation Representative Rachel Vayes."

Vance stood at the desk. He kept his expression where it was. His face was the first thing people read, and alarm made them stop giving you information.

"Thank you," he said.

He walked to the lobby and sat in one of the chairs. Four minutes. He had come here without telling Marcus — had done the whole drive, the dirt, the cuffs, the corridor, the turret, without logging it, without informing anyone. He had come because he thought Conrad's records on Gracie and the IRiS program were here, and because if they were, he would have had something real. Proof of what Alpha Corp had done with the nanoparticle program. Something to bring to someone.

The archive was empty. Transferred to Alpha Corp six weeks ago. Authorization from Rachel Vayes — who chaired the Federation's resource ethics committee, who had spent two years giving speeches about corporate overreach. That Rachel Vayes.

Vance sat with that for four minutes. Every exit sealed before he arrived. The woman he had believed was at minimum a neutral party had been running something else entirely, and had been running it for longer than he had considered.

He stood up.

Vance had always believed that confrontation was the one thing people like Marcus couldn't plan for. That walking into the room and saying what was true — out loud, in person, looking at the man — was a variable that didn't fit their model, because their model assumed everyone else was calculating the same way they were.

He did not compose a message. He drove to Marcus's building and walked in.

---

The cargo bay was cold. The raw DarkX plasma in the harvester housing required near-vacuum containment temperatures — Taylor had cited this in the mission briefings as a reason to keep crew time in the bay to a minimum, which had seemed reasonable then.

They were all in the bay now. Cole near the aft wall. Sam and Heavy at the
mid-level. Bitboy between Cole and the main harvester housing, still amber-lit,
still moving in that way that was not quite patrol.

The door sealed.

It took four seconds. Cole counted them.

The latch sound came at four.

The intercom was quiet. Not the quiet of a dead line — Cole could hear the
slight electronic breath of an open channel. Someone was on the other end.
Someone was choosing what to say and when to say it, and the calculation of that
choosing was the quiet, and the quiet was the thing that made the back of Cole's
neck go cold.

Taylor's voice came through flat and even.

"I told you to stop looking for someone to blame, Jackpot." A pause. "Same captain. Second accident. You were never supposed to come home."

Not a shout. Not even an accusation, really. More the voice of a man delivering
a conclusion he had already reached at some earlier point and was only now
choosing to share. The cover story laid bare in one sentence — Cole's death was
always the plan, the tragic pattern that would close the file on the harvest
theft and seal the Rapture window behind it.

The Golden Dawn was never going home. Neither was the name Taylor Glenn. What came next was already waiting on the vessel two hundred meters off the port side — and the crew that was supposed to be dead in the cargo bay would never know the difference.

Cole said nothing. He looked at Sam. Sam was already moving toward the manual
override panel — she had found it three days ago and said nothing.

The intercom clicked again.

"Samantha."

Same flat register as the first time. Sam went by Sam. Had gone by Sam since the first day of mission briefings. "Samantha" was not an accident. She stopped moving for exactly one second — not frozen, just registering it — and then kept going.

The temperature in the bay dropped one degree. Cole watched the atmospheric
gauge in his peripheral display. The reading fell by one percent. Then another,
over the course of perhaps thirty seconds. Not explosive decompression — not the
sudden catastrophic Hollywood version. This was slow. Careful. A man who knew
what he was doing.

Taylor was venting the bay.

He had run the math. No power to the door's locking circuit — Taylor held the isolated bay systems — meant the only exit stayed shut. And the one mechanical variable left, the harvester housing, contained raw DarkX plasma. Nobody opened that in a sealed room. Nobody deliberately bridged a live electrical arc through their own body to restore circuit power. These were not things people did. Taylor's model of the situation did not include things people did not do.

He was betting on two things: that no one in their right mind would open the harvester, and that without power to the door locking circuit, the only exit stayed shut. Both reasonable bets. Both correct. For anyone else.

---

"Sam." Cole kept his voice level.

Sam was already at the override panel housing. She had the cover off. She looked
at the configuration for two seconds and then she looked at Cole.

"Manual override is behind the harvester. If we open it, the plasma is in this
room."

She said it the way she said most things. As information.

The atmospheric gauge was at ninety-four percent. Falling slowly. The
temperature had dropped another degree. Heavy was near the storage rack — he
had grabbed a thermal wrap from the emergency supply without being told, was
calculating the same math Cole was calculating.

Cole looked at the harvester housing. It was a standard extraction unit — the
same model he had reviewed in the mission briefings, the same model that three
different safety protocols required be kept sealed whenever crew was present in
the bay. The plasma inside was raw. Not containerized. Raw DarkX, harvested from the ring column.

The gauge hit ninety-two.

Bitboy made a sound.

Cole had heard Bitboy produce a wide range of sounds over the course of the
mission — the rapid serial clicks of threat assessment, the lower frequency
tones of a task acknowledgment, the specific modulated sequence that meant he
was detecting something in the EM spectrum the rest of the crew couldn't read.
This was none of those.

It was older than those. It was the sound, Cole thought, that whatever Bitboy
was before he was Bitboy had made when it understood something irreversible was
happening. His LED eyes had gone full blue — not the amber of monitoring, not
the red that Cole had only seen twice and hoped not to see again. Blue.

Bitboy was already moving toward the harvester housing before Cole said
anything.

Cole watched him for the length of two breaths.

Cole thought the word he had never said. Kamikaze. The command that meant suicidal charge — the command the crew had agreed, one night in the galley without Bitboy present, they would only use if there was no other way back. He looked at Bitboy. Bitboy looked back at him with the flat amber attention that meant he was ready for whatever came next.

Cole did not say it. There was still another way.

Die in the bay with the atmosphere bleeding out around them, the cold coming in
increments, the slow clinical certainty of it. Or open the harvester and put raw
DarkX plasma in a sealed room with three humans and one biomechanical
chimpanzee.

He chose.

"Get anchored," Cole said. "Both of you. Now."

Heavy was already moving. Sam dropped to the floor anchor point near the panel
and locked her suit clip without looking. Cole crossed to the harvester housing
in four steps and worked the manual release — the mechanical one, not the
electronic seal, because the electronic seal was routed through the bay control
systems and Taylor had those. His hands were steady. He had noticed that before,
in situations like this: his hands did what he told them to do and they did not
shake until after.

The housing opened.

There was no sound. That was the first thing. No burst, no hiss, no dramatic release. The DarkX plasma was simply there, the way Cole had known it would be, from the moment the Polaris had torn open and put him in contact with it the first time. It reached. That was the only word for it. Not outward — not in any direction that corresponded to the physics of pressurized release. It reached the way attention reaches, the way you can feel someone looking at you from across a room.

Cole's hand was four inches from the edge of the housing.

He could feel it through the suit glove. Not heat and not cold. Something that was not temperature at all.

He felt it the way you feel something that was taken from you before you finished understanding it — the recognition arriving ahead of the memory.

He did not move his hand.

The DarkX was looking at him. He understood this the way you understand things that you cannot explain and do not need to. It was not a metaphor. It was looking at him, and he was looking at it, and whatever the nature of the contact was it had already begun.

It had been patient. The patience of something that has been waiting, in the dark, at the center of a sealed cell, for the conditions that would allow it to be something other than contained. It was not merely responsive. It had been waiting for this exact moment, and now it had it, and it was pulling.

He kept his eyes on Bitboy.

Bitboy had found the junction box.

It was recessed behind the harvester housing — the splice point where the bay's isolated power circuit fed the door locking mechanism. Taylor had the electronic seal. What Taylor did not have was a biomechanical chimpanzee with metal forearms willing to bridge the gap himself.

Cole watched him position both mechanical upper arms across the open terminals. The metal was already conducting — Bitboy's forearms were live junctions, always had been, and what he was about to do was run the full circuit through his own body to restore power to the door seal long enough to reverse it.

Cole looked at him. Bitboy looked back with the flat amber attention of something that had already made its decision.

Cole did not say the word. He didn't have to.

Bitboy closed the circuit.

The sound he made was not a sound Cole had heard before and hoped not to hear again. The arc went through both arms simultaneously — a crack, a brief blue-white flash, the smell of scorched metal. His LED eyes spiked to white and then went dark.

Cole was already going.

Sam said his name.

He heard it from a long distance.
