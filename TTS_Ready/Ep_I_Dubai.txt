EPILOGUE I

Marcus Verguld had been sitting at the same table for ninety minutes.

The table was in the corner of a restaurant so quiet it felt like a library. Everything here cost money in a way that didn't announce itself — the kind of place where nothing was said about price because nothing here was bought. It was arranged. He had been coming since before the magazine profiles. The staff knew to leave the chair across from him empty.

He had ordered the orange juice. Four ice cubes, nearly perfect, the way they always were. He had drunk half of it and was no longer drinking.

The Panama files were out to two hundred thousand people. In forty-eight hours that number would be closer to two million. He had done the math in the car on the way from the airport. The math was fine. The math was the wrong thing to focus on. The Panama files were noise. Noise became signal only when it had somewhere to land, and the structure that would make it land — the infrastructure of institutions, the legitimate levers — had always been his. That was the play. The play still held.

He was reviewing the mistakes. He did not call them that out loud. He called them positioning errors. Things that had been true an hour ago and were no longer true. Taylor: positioning error. The road attack had been Taylor's initiative, unauthorized, sloppy, and it had cost him Taylor, who had been — whatever else — a functioning instrument for twenty years. That was the ledger. You kept it. You moved on.

He had not noticed the man sit down until the glass was already on the table.

A second glass. Orange juice. Four ice cubes, nearly perfect. Placed without a sound.

Marcus looked at the glass for a moment before he looked up.

Klaus was seventy-one. He had the face of a man who had been patient for a very long time and had found the patience correct. He had come to America on a freighter, settled in Culver City, driven a trash route as cover — Verde Sanitation, a name nobody remembered — while he spent a decade quietly building the structure that a subordinate he never met eventually named Alpha Corp. He had raised Marcus in deliberate ignorance, the only luxury he ever permitted himself, so that when Marcus was ready the wound of a small life would make him want the larger frame. He set nothing down. He had already set down what needed setting. He folded his hands on the table. The restaurant had not changed in any way that Marcus could identify, and yet it felt smaller.

"You built the cover," Klaus said. "The structure continues."

Marcus said nothing. He was not certain, in this moment, that he was expected to.

A pause. Long enough to be a deliberate thing.

"The empire outlasted Wilhelm," Klaus said. "It will need to outlast you. You have no heir. How can we reward the bloodline if there is none?"

He said it once, quietly, and in a way that could not be unsaid.

Klaus had liked the Nova boy. He had written in cipher, years ago, in a notebook Marcus had never been permitted to read: *The Nova boy is good. He may be useful or he may be in the way. Either is permissible.* It turned out he was useful. Klaus did not say this aloud. He did not need to. Marcus already knew which one Cole had been.

Rachel Vayes. She was never his. Klaus did not say this aloud either. Some conclusions were better left for the person who needed to arrive at them.

The ice in the second glass was still perfect. The ice in Marcus's glass had begun to shrink.

The restaurant was very quiet. The staff did not approach the table.

Marcus lifted his glass. He drank the remaining orange juice. He set it down without a sound, the way he had been taught.
