RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Sixteen

The Void and The Crater

The ceremony was at 0300 on the seventh day. It was November 22.

Nobody announced it. Nobody sent a notification or posted a time or made any visible preparation that Jill could track. At 0245 she woke up in her bunk aboard the Hound's Tooth — not because anything had disturbed her, but because she had been sleeping lightly for two days and something in the quality of the station's ambient sound had shifted. A settling. A stillness that wasn't absence but attention.

She dressed. She went to the main tunnel.

They were already there.

All thirty-three of the Void-Walkers, and eleven of the Loyalists, including Petra, who stood at the far end of the group with the expression of someone who disapproved of the form while accepting the substance. Tyler was at the east end of the tunnel, near the preparation chamber door. The four cells were on a cart beside him — the same ones she had seen two days ago, their crystalline exteriors catching the amber light.

Nobody spoke.

Jill found a place at the edge of the group and stood there.

---

Tyler began without preamble.

He was not loud. He did not need to be — the tunnel was quiet enough that a normal voice carried the full length of it. He spoke the way someone speaks when they have said something many times and the repetition hasn't dulled it; when they have been waiting for the moment to arrive and now that it's here the words come without effort.

He spoke about Elsa Klein.

He spoke about the eleven years she had spent building this colony out of rock and belief and the four notebooks she had left behind. He spoke about the day she had arrived with three hundred and sixty-nine people and one resolution: that what she had found deserved to be handled with care rather than stripped for profit. He spoke about the eleven who had survived the first decade, and what had been lost to the asteroid and the years and the things an outpost this far from everything required of a person. He spoke about the last entry in the last notebook, written three days before she died, in handwriting that Petra had described to him as shaky but precise.

He did not read from the notebook. He had it memorized.

The cells are not ours. We assembled them. We stabilized them. We gave them conditions in which to become what they are. But the process they run — the sync, the lock, the Jackpot, the void alignment — this did not come from us. We found it. We observed it. We learned to work with it the way a farmer learns to work with rain: not by controlling it but by understanding when to plant.

I believe the void receives what we give it because the void is not empty. I believe the energy that moves through these cells moves through everything — through us, through the line of who we are and who we came from and who we will be. I believe this because I have watched the math long enough to understand that the math is describing something larger than the math.

I am going to die in this asteroid. I am not afraid of that. I am going to die and the energy I carried through this life is going to move — forward, into the cells I helped stabilize, into the notebooks my friends will keep, into the people who come after and find this place and understand what it is. I am going to be received by the same void that receives everything I give it.

It is only after you have lost everything that you are free to do anything. I have tested this.

The gold will keep coming. I want my colleagues to keep sending.

He paused. What came next was different in register — not doctrine, not elegy. Instructions.

"The first rule of the colony," he said.

Jill heard the words and felt, with a completely unreasonable clarity, the finish of that sentence arrive in her head from somewhere else entirely: *don't talk about the colony.* She nearly laughed. She did not laugh. The tunnel was very quiet and twenty-two people were watching Tyler Durden and this was not that moment and she knew it.

"You carry it. Not push it. Not hand it off at the threshold. You carry it, in both hands, for the full length of the tunnel, until the carrying is done for you."

He waited.

"The second rule: you walk until it lifts. Not until you're close to where it has lifted before. Not until you believe you've gone far enough. Until it lifts."

Another pause. The amber lights ran the full length of the tunnel and then stopped at the dark.

"The third rule: when it lifts — you let it go."

---

The silence after lasted long enough that Jill stopped counting.

Then Tyler lifted the first cell from the cart. He carried it himself — no ceremony, no procession, just a man carrying something heavy and handling it with the care you handle irreplaceable things.

He looked back at the group.

"She came back for a reason," he said. "We're sending her the best we have."

He did not say who "she" was. Nobody asked.

He turned and walked into the east tunnel.

What happened after the light ended was not visible from where they stood. Jill understood the mechanics the way you understand things you have been told rather than things you have felt. The tunnel ran through the asteroid toward the void. As you walked deeper, gravity gradually let go — not as a threshold but as a slow release, the asteroid's claim loosening increment by increment until it was no longer a claim at all. A stabilized FuelCell carried in both hands would begin, at some point, to lift. Not because you threw it. Not because you pushed it. Because it had arrived at the place where the void wanted it more than the rock did. You carried it until it left you.

Tyler had never described what this felt like. Petra had said, once, that he came back lighter every time. Not metaphorically — that the walk out was physically different from the walk in, as if something in the carrying had altered his relationship to weight.

He came back. He lifted the second cell.

He carried the second into the tunnel. Then the third. Then the fourth.

When the last one was gone he stood at the edge of the light with his hands open at his sides. Not in prayer. In the specific posture of someone who has finished something that required everything they had and is letting the empty feeling that comes after simply be there.

He turned around.

He looked at Jill.

"You don't have to believe in the ancestors," he said. "You just have to watch the math."

"What math," she said.

"Eleven days," he said.

---

The Golden Dawn had been running a twenty-six-day supply loop between Pell Station and the outer ring when Heavy discovered Fight Club.

He had not looked for it. He had cycled through the library in a bored moment on the fourteenth day and it had appeared in the recommendations, and he had watched it at 0200 with the volume low and the monitor angled away from Bitboy's alcove. The next morning Bitboy had watched it at 0600, alone, without mentioning it to anyone.

By the eighteenth day they were on their fourth watch.

Cole came in for coffee. He could hear the monitor from the galley corridor — a specific scene he hadn't placed yet, Brad Pitt delivering something in the register of a person who has burned everything down on purpose and found the air on the other side clean.

*It's only after we've lost everything that we're free to do anything.*

Cole stood in the doorway with his mug.

Heavy was in the big chair. Bitboy was on the equipment bench to his left — all six limbs folded in the compact configuration he used when he was paying close attention to something and didn't want anyone to know how much. His LED eyes were dim. That was the tell.

"He talking to himself again?" Cole said.

"He does that," Heavy said, without looking away from the screen.

Cole poured his coffee. He thought about the cabin. He had been in it for fourteen months before he found Bitboy — in the equipment locker of a decommissioned research vessel, not because anyone had left him there, but because Bitboy had decided it was a good place to be and no one had been paying close enough attention to notice. Before the cabin there had been the university. Before the university there had been a life that had made sense in the way lives make sense when you haven't tested them yet.

He had not chosen to lose any of it. The losing had happened to him, incrementally, by a series of events that hadn't asked. He'd arrived at the other side — at the bourbon, the snow, the CyberMonkey, and eventually Sharon — not because he'd understood what the losing was doing but because at a certain point the absence of a choice becomes the choice.

He watched Brad Pitt talk to himself on the monitor.

"Good movie," he said.

"Best one," Heavy said.

Bitboy's upper left arm twitched. Cole had learned, over the years, to read this as agreement.

Cole went back to bed.

---

She stayed.

She didn't decide to. She just didn't leave. The morning after the ceremony she ran the full diagnostic on the Hound's Tooth — actually ran it this time, every system, every readout — and found nothing wrong, which she had expected, but the process took three hours and by the time it was done it was midday and she was hungry and the kitchen had food.

She told herself she was leaving tomorrow.

The kitchen had better food than she had expected. Real protein — from an actual animal, from somewhere with atmosphere. She asked Yun about it on the second day.

"Pell," he said, without looking up. "Tyler runs a standing order. Best grade the station stocks."

"The colony budgets for that."

"Gold receipts," he said. "The sacrifice is the big show. But the returns come back in a form that trades. Pell has access to markets we don't." He glanced over. "Tyler wants to sacrifice more than we keep. I think that's easier to believe when you've had a good meal."

She thought about this for the rest of lunch and did not argue with it.

On the third day she found the memorial wall.

It was at the junction between the main tunnel and the east branch — not marked, not framed, just carved directly into the asteroid rock in handwriting that had changed over the years as different people had added to it. Three hundred and fifty-eight names. She stood there for a while. She read what she could read and stood with the rest.

Nobody came by. Nobody explained it. Nobody needed to.

On the fourth day Yun took her into the tunnel — not to the sacrifice point, but to the section about two hundred meters in where the gradient started. He was carrying two mugs. He handed her one and they walked.

She noticed it after a while. The weight of the mug hadn't changed, exactly — but the claim gravity made on it had. As if the asteroid had stopped insisting and was only suggesting.

"Here," Yun said.

She tilted her mug. The liquid lifted toward the rim. Not a slosh. A rise. Measured. Like something had quietly revised its opinion about where it should be.

"It's a metaphor," Yun said. He had said it on purpose and he knew it.

"You walk in with a full cup," he said, "and somewhere in the middle it becomes something you're holding differently than you held it at the entrance." He took a slow sip, completely unbothered. "Doesn't spill if you know what you're doing."

She had run this kind of differential for years — the numbers, the tolerances, the launch windows a gravity gradient opened and closed. She had delivered cargo through tighter corridors at higher differential than this.

She still stood there for a while and watched her coffee lift toward the rim.

She told herself this for ten days.

---

The eighteenth day. Eleven days after the ceremony.

She was in the tracking room at 0347. She had set an alarm, which she had not told anyone about and which she was not entirely sure why she had set. Tyler was already there. Yun was there. Four other Void-Walkers she knew by face and not yet by name. And Petra, at the back of the room, standing where she always stood — slightly apart, slightly sideways, as though the data on the screen was something she was being asked to testify about rather than watch.

Nobody had told Jill to come. Nobody had needed to.

At 0402 the proximity sensor lit.

The asteroid resolved on the tracking display from the same vector as the previous one — she had reviewed the logs, all six years of them, the same approach corridor each time, the same impossible deceleration as it entered their field, the same final holding position just outside docking range. It was always this way.

This one was larger.

Not by a little. Twice the mass of the previous one. Maybe more.

"He sent more than last time," Yun said quietly. "He sent four. We've never sent four."

The asteroid rotated slowly on the display. At 0419 it reached the angle that put its largest face toward their sensors, and the spectrometric reading came in, and the display showed what the reading showed.

The face had an impact crater. Thirty meters across. Dense, uniform, reflective.

Gold.

Not gold-bearing rock. Not gold-flecked mineral. A crater of solid gold on the face of the asteroid, as though something had struck it from the other side with an instrument that left gold behind instead of taking material away.

Petra looked at the display for a long time.

"Jackpot," she said.

Quietly. The way she always said it. The way it always meant the same thing — here is the number, here is what it is, here is what it does to every model I have ever run. She said it and did not look at Tyler, and Tyler did not look at her, and the room was quiet in the way rooms are quiet when the argument has been running for six years and one side has just produced another piece of evidence that neither side entirely knows what to do with.

---

Jill left the tracking room.

She went to the docking bay. She sat in the Hound's Tooth in the dark with the hatch closed and the interior lights off and she sat there for a long time.

She was not a person who believed in ancestors receiving FuelCells through a void. She was not a person who believed in reincarnation into bloodlines or the energy of a life moving forward into the people who came after. She was a person who ran a trade circuit in the outer belt and checked her instruments and made her runs and did not spend a lot of time on questions that didn't have answers she could work with.

She had watched a man carry four FuelCells down the east tunnel until the gravity let go of them, and send them into the dark.

Eleven days later, a gold asteroid — twice the size of any previous one — had arrived from the same impossible vector it always arrived from, and on its face there was a crater of solid gold that nobody could explain, and Petra had said the same word she always said, and the tracking room had gone quiet in the way it always went quiet.

She could not explain the gold.

She had tried. She had spent ten days running the orbital mechanics in her head — inbound trajectories, gravity wells, asteroid composition distributions, the probability of a gold-mass object entering a stable approach vector from the same corridor six times in six years. The numbers didn't work. The numbers hadn't worked the first time she ran them and they didn't work now.

She sat in the dark and thought about Elsa Klein, buried forty meters away in the asteroid wall, who had mapped the void's gravitational signature and left four notebooks and written, three days before she died, that the energy of a life moved forward.

She thought about the math she had been running since the tracking room. Six returns over seventeen years, each arrival documented in Elsa's logs. Whatever the colony spent on supply orders was a fraction of what those asteroids represented in raw value. She had walked past the east tunnel's secondary branch twice without being shown inside it. Nobody had mentioned it. Nobody had blocked it either.

She thought about the part of the notebooks that Petra had refused to show her.

She got out of the ship and went back inside.

---

She found Petra at the bench. Petra looked up when Jill came in. She looked at her for a moment and then looked back at her work.

"No," she said.

"I watched it arrive," Jill said.

"I know. I was standing next to you."

"I can't explain the gold."

"I know that too." Petra was quiet. "That is the problem with this place. The gold keeps arriving and nobody can explain it, and Tyler is very good at providing an explanation, and his explanation requires a great deal of belief in things that are not measurable." She set down her pen. "Elsa's explanation also required a great deal of belief. The difference is that Elsa was a scientist first and a believer second. She believed because the math pointed somewhere and she was honest enough to follow it even when she couldn't verify where it led."

"And Tyler?"

"Tyler believes because he needs to," Petra said. It wasn't unkind. "That doesn't make him wrong. It just means his path there was different from hers."

Jill was quiet.

"The notebooks," she said.

Petra looked at her for a long moment. Then she stood up and went to the supply room. She came back with a sealed case — old, worn at the edges, the kind of case you keep because what's inside is irreplaceable. She set it on the bench.

She did not open it.

"Elsa believed," she said, "that the cells were a medium. The way water is a medium for sound. The Darker Matter conducts something she couldn't name — not energy in the conventional sense, not a field she could measure — but something that moved through it the way meaning moves through language. She believed this because of the sync period." Petra looked at the case. "Not the charge levels. The sync period. The eleven days when you can feel it in your sternum and can't hear it with your ears. She wrote: 'I have felt this before. Every human being who has ever held a newborn child in the first days of their life has felt this. The feeling of a new node reaching toward the network. The feeling of something that has just arrived learning where it is.'"

Jill said nothing.

"She believed that the cells were doing what human beings do," Petra said. "Finding their way back into the circuit. Moving forward through the line." She put her hand on the case without opening it. "She also wrote this — in the section of the records that predates the cell science, three years before she died: 'One day, a descendant of this bloodline will come to the place where the sacrifice is made. The circle will close.' She wrote it as certainty, not hope." She was quiet for a moment. "I have been waiting for that sentence to mean something."

Jill looked at the case.

"I'm not her granddaughter," she said.

"No," Petra said. "You're not."

She picked up the case and took it back to the supply room.

She was gone for a moment. When she came back, she was not carrying the case. She was carrying a single sheet — a piece of paper, old, the edges soft from handling, a copy made from something older still. She set it on the bench between them.

"The notebooks contain four things," Petra said. "The first is the spiritual practices — the ancestor gifting, the belief system, the conviction that what you give to the void moves forward through your bloodline. The second is what Elsa called the DarkX directions — the science of conversion. Three hundred and sixty-nine documented test runs, the locking mechanics, the eleven-day cycle, the void sacrifice data, the final zero-gravity experiments she called the zero condition. The instructions for making FuelCells from raw DarkX, step by step. The third is the timing and process of sacrifice to the void — the Liber Samekh ritual, the three-day fast, the invocation in the dark. How the DarkX moved through the crack in the wall toward the sound of her voice. What it chose to give. And what she understood afterward about the structure of the material — the cold and the containment and the geometry of the lock. The science came from the encounter. Not the other way around. The fourth is the preserved records of the old European mystic order Elsa studied before she came here — the foundational documents she transcribed during her years as a disciple. The woman who founded that order appears throughout. Her name will mean something at the proper time." She smoothed the edge of the paper without looking at it. "I am going to read you one thing from the last set — the Order records. Then I am not going to ask you anything."

She read it.

What she read was not about cells. It was not about the void or the sacrifice or the eleven-day clock. It was a record of a woman's life — the founder of the order, in her own hand. Where she had come from. What she had walked away from. Her conviction that the bloodline she carried would produce, eventually, someone who came back to the sacrifice path and chose it freely — not from duty, not from inheritance. From recognition.

Jill went very still.

Petra looked at her. She did not ask. She did not need to.

The founder's name, at the top of the first page and at the bottom of the last entry, was Von Grutzen.

She folded the paper. She returned to the supply room. She came back with the sealed case and set it on the bench in front of Jill. "Read them tonight," she said. "Return them in the morning. I have copies."

Jill stood at the bench alone for a while. She put the notebooks back in the case. She went to the main hall and sat with a cup of something hot and did not drink it. She sat there for close to an hour. Yun came in at some point — said nothing, sat beside her for twenty minutes, then got up and went back to the cable run without asking. That helped. The not asking helped. Then she went to find Tyler Durden.



---

She went back to the Hound's Tooth at noon.

Not to leave — she told herself — just to look. To sit in the pilot's seat and run the pre-launch checks she hadn't run in eighteen days. To remind herself what the ship smelled like. There was a reason you did maintenance checks even when you weren't going anywhere, and she had a contract with a routing broker that had been sitting unanswered for twelve days, and she was going to answer it.

She ran the checks. The ship smelled like herself — metal and recycled air and the staleness of a small enclosed space that had been habitable for a long time. She had spent more hours in this cockpit than in any building, any room, any place she could name. It had always been enough.

The contract notice was still in the queue. Titan to Callisto. Pickup window closing in thirty-six hours. Standard pay. She had run this route five times. She knew the math.

She wrote the acceptance and did not send it.

She sat with the unsigned message for a while. Outside, through the small forward viewport, she could see the colony's main tunnel entrance. The cargo lights above it were on, their amber glow steady in the dark of the asteroid's unlit face. Someone was moving near the entrance, carrying something in both hands. She didn't recognize the figure at this distance. She didn't need to.

She sent the acceptance.

Then she went back in to say goodbye.

Tyler was in the tracking room, which surprised her. He looked at her face and then at the bag over her shoulder and said nothing. That surprised her too — she had expected a speech. A recruitment pitch. Another version of the race-to-control-the-grid argument.

"Thank you," she said, "for letting me watch."

He nodded once.

"The math is good," she said.

"The math is good," he agreed.

She looked at the tracking display. She thought about the notebooks. She thought about the zero condition.

"The open condition," she said. "The zero-gravity locking. Do you know if anyone outside the colony has been working on it?"

Tyler was quiet for a moment. Then: "The Collective has been asking us this question. For months. Two of them — they called themselves Red and Squirrel — reached out through our trade channels." A pause. "I told them Elsa documented exactly that. I told them it was in the notebooks."

Jill was quiet.

"They knew more than I expected," he said.

He turned back to the screen. He was done with her. He had what he needed from her presence — he had given her the watch and the proof, and now she was leaving, and that was as it should be. Tyler Durden moved on the way that people move on when they have somewhere specific to be inside themselves.

Petra was at the far end of the main tunnel.

She was sitting on a low bench set into the asteroid wall, the kind of thing that looked improvised and had clearly been there for decades. She did not look up when Jill approached. She was reading from one of the smaller notebooks — not Elsa's original, but a copy she had made, in her own hand, years ago. She had made copies so the originals could be kept sealed. Jill had returned the originals to her that morning.

Jill stopped a few feet away. She didn't know what she was going to say. She had been good at not knowing what to say her whole life and she had always gotten through it.

Petra closed the notebook. She looked at Jill with the expression of someone who had arrived at a great number of correct conclusions and had long since stopped needing to be congratulated for them.

"You're going," she said.

"I have a contract."

"I know."

They stood with that for a moment. Down at the far end of the tunnel, where gravity let go, the dark was very complete.

"The notebooks," Jill said. "The last entries. The open condition." She paused. "Do you understand what she was describing?"

"I understand what she documented," Petra said. "I don't understand the implications. I'm not a physicist."

"I might know someone who is." She paused. "Or someone who knows someone."

"The notebooks are yours," Petra said. "Elsa made sure of that."

Jill started to say something.

"The colony is yours as well," Petra said. "Everything we hold. Everything the void has returned to us. Elsa's instructions were precise — legal documents in the archive room, filed with the independent registry on Pell Station, authenticated and irrevocable. I am the executor. You are the heir." She said it the way she said everything — without ceremony, without needing a response. "There is one more thing you should see before you go."

She took Jill to the east tunnel. Not to the dark at the end — to the secondary branch, the door Jill had walked past twice and never been shown inside. Petra opened it.

Jill stood in the doorway.

The chamber was carved wide over decades, hollowed out with the patience of people who had somewhere specific to put what the void returned. The walls were ordinary asteroid rock. The floor was not.

Six asteroids over seventeen years. Each one broken down in parts and consolidated here, stored here, accumulated with the slow certainty of a practice that had not missed a cycle. It was not bars. It was not formatted for transport or display. It was just gold — dense and dull in the amber work lights, the accumulated value of seventeen years of sacrifice filling the secondary branch from the floor to chest height, the way water fills a vessel over time without anyone watching it rise.

"How much," Jill said.

"I stopped calculating three asteroids ago," Petra said. "The number became very large and I found large numbers stopped telling me anything useful." She looked at the chamber. "Tyler has opinions about what to do with it. The Void-Walkers have different opinions. The Loyalists have a third set." She turned. "You'll need to decide."

Jill stood there for a moment. Then she took the originals.

She went to the ship. She sealed the transit case with the notebooks in the cargo locker. She ran the full launch sequence. She launched.
