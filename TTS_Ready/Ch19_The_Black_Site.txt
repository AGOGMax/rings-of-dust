RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Nineteen

The Black Site

Vance adjusted his sunglasses against the glare of the mid-day sun. He yanked
them off with one hand and steered with his knee as he cleaned the lenses with
his shirt. Four hundred dollars and they still smeared like gas station sunglasses. He put
them back on. The A/C was set to max, and he kept the window down anyway — he
felt claustrophobic with it up.

The desert spread out in every direction. Mountains dimly visible through the
haze. After what felt like too many miles of dirt road, the road stopped being
a road.

He pulled up to what looked to be a partially demolished drive-in movie theater.
Chain link fence around the property, cruel-looking razor wire along the top.
A single brand-new guardhouse with a fresh coat of white paint sat near the gate,
completely out of place in the wreckage around it. The guard that emerged from it
was equally out of place — well-groomed, pressed uniform, rifle leveled at the
windshield before Vance had fully stopped.

"Turn around, sir."

Vance shoved his badge out the window. "Vance Black, Operations. They should
have called ahead about me."

"Get out of the car. Hands where I can see them."

He complied, both hands up, badge in his left. The guard kept the rifle on him
and came around the front of the truck.

"On the ground. Hands flat."

The rocky desert floor was scorching — sand and grit and a particular quality
of heat that had been building since before dawn. Vance laid himself prone and
spread his limbs. Sand got into his mouth.

The guard pulled his arms behind his back and cuffed them. "This is for your
safety, sir."

Vance coughed out wet grit. "Appreciate the warm welcome. Nothing like eating dirt to feel at home."

The guard checked the badge, jerked him to his feet, went back to the gatehouse
and picked up a phone. A brief exchange. He came back out.

"You can enter. On foot."

"What do I do with the truck?"

"I'll take care of it, sir."

"Where do I go from here?"

The guard motioned to a cement structure that had once been the concession stand.
Graffiti along the sides. The roof partially gone. "Go in there and get yourself
a soda."

Vance looked at the building and back at the guard. "You're serious."

The guard stared at him.

With his hands still cuffed behind his back, Vance tilted his head toward them.

The guard looked genuinely irritated at the implication. "Of course, sir." He
removed the cuffs. Vance cradled his wrists.

"Thanks, Rambo."

The guard raised the rifle again, leveled and pleasant. "No problem, sir. You
have yourself a fine day."

---

The concession stand smelled like dry ketchup and old oil from the hot dog
rollers, a smell that had been baking in the desert heat long enough to become
the smell of the building itself. Barren shelves. Advertisements for brands
that no longer existed. At the far left, a beverage dispenser on a dusty counter
with a stack of styrofoam cups beside it.

Vance set a cup under it. A small amount of thick liquid dripped from one of the
faucets. He sniffed it and threw the cup in the nearest garbage can.

A click from a door to his right. Stenciled on it: SUPPLIES. Now open.

He went through.

The stairwell was narrow — cement walls on both sides, an iron handrail bolted
into the right wall that he decided not to touch. A hundred steps down, the smell
shifting from hot dust to cold mold, the light from above thinning and then gone.
At the bottom, another door with a keycard reader. He placed his badge against it.
Red twice. Then green. The door opened.

A guard on the other side, standing at rigid attention. Vance waved. No reaction.
The man stood as still as furniture. The hallway beyond him was long and circular,
a metal grid pathway running the length of the floor. At the far end, another
door. With a sigh, Vance started walking.

Each step sent a metallic echo clanging through the corridor, doubling and
returning. The overhead lights were spaced at regular intervals and the ceiling
between them was dark. He noticed the machine gun turret mounted above the
entrance when he was almost directly under it. Subtle.

At the far end, another keycard reader. He swiped. The heavy door unlocked.

On the other side, a young female military clerk sat working through a stack of
papers. Cabinets lined the cement wall behind her. Two well-lit curved tunnels
led left and right from the room. She looked up.

"Hall of Records. What are you looking for today, Mr. Black?" Polite, no warmth.

"Files and research belonging to Dr. Stephen Conrad."

She arose. "One moment please. Wait here." She turned to the cabinets, opened a
drawer of old reference cards, walked her fingers through the yellowed stack for
a long time. Found the one she needed, traced along it, pulled a thin folder from
a separate cabinet, and brought it to the counter. It landed with a soft plop on
the laminate.

Vance opened it. Thin. Conference programs. A copy of Conrad's keynote
abstract from the Vegas energy summit. A birth certificate. And beneath it,
a death certificate — heavily redacted except for the cause of death.

Suicide.

The certificate was dated three weeks after Conrad's disappearance.
Pre-filed. Unsigned. A cover story Alpha had prepared and not yet needed
to use — the kind of document you keep in a drawer in case someone comes
looking and needs to stop looking.

He looked up. "I need more than this. I need to see what was in storage. I'm
looking for his research — the Gracie architecture, the iRIS design notes."

She cocked her head. "That won't be possible."

"I don't think you understand," she said. "His vault was emptied last month."

Vance looked up from the folder.

"How is that possible. By whom."

"I don't even have to look that one up." She didn't look it up. "It was
transferred by several trucks with the Alpha Corp emblem on the sides.
These files are all that's left. Would you like me to make a copy for you?"

"Who authorized the transfer."

"Alpha Corp spokesperson Rachel Vayes."

Vance set the folder down.

He looked around the room — the curved tunnels, the cabinets, the clerk with her
stack of papers — and looked back at her and held himself very still.

"You have got to be kidding me."

---

He drove north for thirty minutes before he pulled over on the shoulder of the
road and sat with the engine running and the window down.

The desert ran flat in every direction. No mountains now. Just scrub and rock and
the silence of places where nothing is expected to happen.

Glenn had the answer for everything. That had always been the problem with men like Taylor Glenn — they never asked the right questions. Vance had spent eight years asking the right questions and getting nowhere. Today he was going to ask one more.

Rachel Vayes. He had been following her public work for three years — Alpha's
preferred correspondent, the voice they sent when they needed the shortage to
sound like an engineering problem rather than a choice. He had never once thought
of her as a threat to his side of the ledger. He had not known she had
operational reach beyond the camera. He had not known she had access to a
facility DeMint didn't know existed.

He thought about what it meant that DeMint hadn't known.

He thought about the trucks with the Alpha Corp emblem on the sides, and
the specific kind of operation it takes to move a vault's worth of materials
through a Federation facility without anyone in the Federation being told.

He thought about the eight years, and who had known about this facility, and who
had sent him here, and who had not.

He drove south.

---

Emily was on the dirt road at 6:22.

She had been awake since four. Not because the alarm woke her — she had been
awake for two hours before it, lying in the motel dark with the road atlas on
her chest and the particular quality of silence that precedes something you've
been moving toward for four hundred miles. She had gotten up, done the stretches
the neurologist had recommended for mornings after long drives, taken her
medication, eaten the second granola bar from the bag's zippered pocket, and sat
at the edge of the bed for a moment with her hands on her knees.

She thought about Sharon checking the car before getting in. She thought about
Sommi's shoes, and how he'd said your body is running on fumes without making
it sound like concern, which was somehow more useful than concern. She thought
about the three hours she'd gotten in a gas station parking lot north of
Victorville, and the two fitful hours in the motel before she woke at two and lay
there watching the ceiling.

She thought: I've already done the hard part twice. Once more.

The turnoff was where Rachel said it would be — twelve miles past the 215
interchange, a dirt road with no sign, cutting south through scrub and creosote
into the kind of landscape that existed specifically to discourage visitors. The
road was a single lane of packed desert floor with tire ruts deep enough to
guide her. Sommi was in the passenger seat, watching the terrain with the flat
attention of someone at home in this kind of landscape.

About a mile in, a truck came the other way — big, dusty, moving fast for a
single-lane dirt road. Emily pulled to the shoulder. The driver had one hand on
the wheel and was looking at the road ahead with the focused expression of someone
who had already gotten what he came for, which appeared to be nothing. The truck
passed without slowing. The dust it left behind took a long time to settle.

Two miles further, they saw it.

The drive-in theater screen was still standing — barely. A massive rectangle of
poured concrete and rebar, bleached white by decades of desert sun, listing
slightly east like a sail that had forgotten its wind. The projection house
behind it was a squat concrete block with boarded windows and a roof that had
given up in stages. The lot was gravel and sand, the speaker posts still
standing in their rows like the remains of a congregation that had wandered off
and never come back.

There was no one here. The desert had been working on this place for twenty
years and the desert was patient.

Sommi looked at the screen. "Underground entrance is behind the projection
house. Service hatch — it'll look like utility access."

Emily pulled around the projection house and stopped the car.

The lot was empty. Tire tracks at the edge of the gravel — one set, the sand at
the ruts' edges still collapsing. Someone had been here within the last few hours.

Sommi looked at the tracks, then at the guardhouse. The door was open. A folding
chair tipped against the back wall. A radio left on the shelf. Someone had been
stationed here this morning and stopped being stationed here in a hurry.

"Guard's gone," Emily said.

"Called off." Sommi was still watching the lot. "Someone came through with a
badge — whatever they found, Alpha wanted this site invisible afterward. A guard
standing at a gate points at a gate." He opened his door. "We have a window.
Come on."

---

The service hatch was where Sommi said it would be — behind the projection
house, set into the concrete foundation, a steel door with a utility access
label that looked legitimate enough to keep out teenagers and not much else. The
lock was a Federation biometric panel, seven years old, the kind they installed
in classified facilities when they wanted something more serious than a deadbolt
but less conspicuous than a guard station.

Sommi pressed his palm to the reader. The light went green.

"Collective got the access codes when they moved the research out," he said.
"Two weeks ago. Alpha document-service cover — the kind of operation that looks
like a filing transfer on paper and doesn't exist on paper an hour later."

The hatch opened into a stairwell — concrete, lit by emergency strips that had
been burning for years without anyone to see them. Seven flights down. Emily
counted. The air got cooler with each level, the desert heat giving way to the
particular chill of deep underground, the kind of cold that has nothing to do
with weather and everything to do with being far from the sun.

At the bottom, a corridor. Reinforced doors on both sides, each with a panel
reader and a log entry system. The overhead lights were the flat institutional
white of a facility that had been built to function, not to be visited.

Sommi moved past three doors and stopped at the fourth. He pressed the same
palm reader. Red to green.

The door opened.

Inside was a narrow room with shelved cabling and a row of filing cabinets
along the far wall. Every drawer was open. Every drawer was empty.

"Conrad's research," Sommi said. "His design notes on Gracie, the original
architecture, everything Alpha modified. It was here for three years." He looked
at the empty cabinets. "We moved it. Vance just found out."

Emily looked at the room. She thought about a man in a white polo driving sixty
on a dirt road because the thing he came for wasn't there.

"Rachel said there was something else down here," she said.

Sommi was quiet for a moment. Then he turned and walked further down the
corridor. Emily followed.

The door at the end of the hallway was different from the others — sealed with a
biometric panel and a secondary magnetic lock, the kind used for chemical
storage and biological materials. There was a small observation window set into
the door at eye level, scratched and reinforced, the size of a paperback book.

Sommi stopped. He pressed his palm to the secondary panel.

Nothing.

He tried again. "Alpha installed their own locks on this one," he said. "The
Collective access codes don't reach it." He stepped back from the door.

Emily put her palm on the door.

Not the panel. The magnetic seal housing itself, where the secondary lock was
recessed into the frame. She didn't plan to. Her hand moved the way it had been
moving all morning, since she woke at two in the dark — ahead of her thinking.

The lock released.

The door swung inward.

Sommi was quiet for a moment that stretched into several. "That's not—" He stopped.

The room was wider than the window had suggested. Shelving on three walls —
magnetic containment units in rows, black-gray, sealed, arranged with the care
of something that was expensive to produce and difficult to replace. The containers
were the same ones she'd seen in Alpha's materials briefings, back when those
briefings had been part of the world she understood. Darker Matter transport.
DarkX.

Alpha had been storing it here for two years — using the classified site because
nobody knew it existed, because the Federation had built it for records that
couldn't exist anywhere else, and Alpha had decided their most valuable asset
qualified.

"Emily," Sommi said from the doorway. "We can't take it. That's not what we're
here for."

"I know," she said.

She walked toward the middle row.

The resonance she'd felt in the corridor was stronger in here — not painful, not
overwhelming, but directional. Like a frequency she'd been calibrated for without
knowing it. She stopped in front of a unit in the middle of the row.

She looked down at her hand. The mark was still there — the dark gray trace on
her palm, the graphite stain she'd noticed in the hotel corridor in Vegas and
never been able to wipe off. It had been there for weeks. She had stopped
thinking about it. She was thinking about it now because it was warm. Not hot.
Warm, the way skin is warm when blood moves through it faster than usual.

She put her palm on the container's seal housing.

The seal released.

The lid came up six inches. The material inside was not a gas, not a liquid, not
quite a solid. It had mass the way current has mass — directional, aware of her
in the particular way a compass is aware of north. A thin column of it rose from
the gap, found the warmth of her hand, and made contact.

The mark on her palm flared.

Not brightly — not like a light source. Like something under the surface
shifting from dormant to active, the way a pilot light catches when the gas
reaches it. A faint luminescence in the lines of her hand, following the same
pattern the graphite had traced, the same pattern the nanoparticles had laid down
when she'd held the Enterprise figurine in Conrad's hotel room.

"Emily." Sommi was in the doorway. He had not moved forward. He had his hands
visible at his sides and he was doing nothing, very carefully — a trained
operative's response to a situation outside every category he'd been trained for.

She couldn't move. Not because she was frozen — because something was happening
and her body understood, at a level below thought, that moving would interrupt
it.

The resonance was not coming from the container. It was coming through her.
Through the nanoparticles in her blood — the counter-program Conrad had encoded
into the figurine, the same compound sitting in Cole's bloodstream right now,
meeting its own DarkX event three hundred million miles from here. The Nova DNA.
The same genetic signature. Conrad had given the figurine to Emily knowing she
would handle it. He had said into his hands because Cole was the primary host —
but Conrad was a careful man, and careful men build redundancies. Emily was the
backup key. The second lock. The failsafe's failsafe.

The glow spread from her palm up through her wrist. She felt it — not pain,
not heat, something closer to recognition. As if every cell in her body that
carried the nanoparticles was saying yes to something it had been waiting for.

Then the DarkX showed her what she was carrying.

It did not show her things that were not real. It showed her what was already
there — the way you turn a light on in a room you've been sitting in for hours
and see, for the first time, what the dark had arranged around you.

Her father was first.

Not the man. The absence. The specific shape of the space he had left when the
cancer took him — the hospital corridor, the closed door, Cole on one side and
Emily on the other, neither of them old enough and both of them old enough. She
had been nineteen. She had held Cole's hand and felt his fingers go rigid when
the sound from the other side of the door changed, and she had understood in
that moment that she was going to be the one who held things together. Not
because she was stronger. Because Cole was going to break and someone had to
not break, and she was the older one, and that was the job.

She had never broken. She had filed it. She had put the grief in a drawer the
way she filed lesson plans and grading rubrics and tax returns — neatly, by
date, in a system that worked because the system was the point. The system was
what kept the grief from being the whole room.

The DarkX opened the drawer.

Not violently. Not the way Cole's DarkX exposure had torn iRIS apart and rebuilt
it. Quietly — the way a teacher opens a textbook to a page the student has been
avoiding. Here. This is the part you skipped. We're going to look at it now.

She saw her father in the driveway with the Harley. She saw what Cole had seen
— the selfishness, the toy — and she saw what Cole had not seen until much
later: a dying man buying one last feeling. She had always known. She had known
before Cole did, before her mother told them, before the word cancer arrived
in the living room and sat down on the couch with them. She had known because
she had watched her father eat dinner one night and noticed that he was
memorizing the room. People who are staying do not memorize the room. People who
are leaving do.

She had never told Cole she had known first. She had never told anyone.

The DarkX held it there — not asking her to do anything with it, just showing
her the weight of carrying a secret about a dead man for twenty-three years.

Then Sharon.

Sharon in the industrial lot. Sharon going down. The sound — not the gunshot
but the sound after, the sound of a body hitting pavement when the person inside
it has stopped being in charge of it. Emily had been running. She had not
stopped running. She had been pulled into a van and taken to a building and sat
in a room and been told Sharon's dead by a woman she did not yet understand,
and she had felt the information land the way a stone lands in deep water — the
surface closes over it immediately and the ripples take hours to reach the
shore.

The ripples had been reaching the shore ever since. In the motel in Barstow.
On the highway with Sommi. In Rachel's voice at the pier. In the car, alone,
at three in the morning, when she had caught herself talking to Sharon the way
Cole talked to Gracie — into the dark, to someone who was not going to answer.

You're not doomed to anything, Sharon had said. Doomed is for people who sit
down.

Emily had not sat down. She had driven four hundred miles on five hours of broken
sleep and arrived at a demolished drive-in theater in the Nevada desert and
walked down seven flights of stairs into the dark. She had done that. Sharon
was dead and Emily had done that anyway, because Sharon had told her once that
she was not average, and Emily had spent the last two months proving it.

The DarkX showed her this. Not as praise. Not as validation. Just as fact. The
way a teacher marks an answer correct — not with enthusiasm, just with
accuracy. This is what you did. This is who you are. The evidence is
sufficient.

Then Cole.

Not Cole as he was now — she did not know what Cole was now, fourteen months
into a Saturn mission with a mole in his crew and a dead woman he didn't know
was still alive. She saw Cole as he had been. A kid, bloody lip,
Marcus Verguld's knee on his chest. She saw herself grabbing the collar and
putting Marcus on the ground. She saw Cole looking up at her from the dirt —
not grateful, not yet, just surprised that someone had come.

She had always come. That was the pattern the DarkX was showing her — not a
single act of courage but a lifetime of showing up. For Cole. For Sharon. For
students who couldn't pass a test and needed someone to break the material into
pieces small enough to hold. For the Collective, which she had joined not
because Sharon recruited her but because she believed in standing up to bullies
and had decided what side she was on before anyone asked.

She was not average. She had never been average. The word had been a shell she
had lived inside because the shell was safe and the world outside it was full
of people like Marcus Verguld who took up all the room. Sharon had seen through
the shell. Rachel had seen through it. Sommi had seen through it the moment he
got in the car.

The DarkX did not break the shell. It just showed her that she had already
walked out of it — somewhere on the highway, somewhere in the dark, somewhere
between the pier and the motel and the dirt road that led to this place.

The warmth spread through her chest. Not dramatic. Not the cosmic horror that
had broken Cole open in the cargo bay at Saturn. Quieter — felt rather than
witnessed, strange rather than theatrical. The cold architecture of iRIS, the
system that lived in the nanoparticles Conrad had planted in both of them, did
not shatter. It loosened. The lock that had held iRIS in place eased from two
directions at once — Cole pushing from within at Saturn, Emily pushing from
without on Earth, neither knowing the other was doing it simultaneously.
Conrad had built the key for both of them.

Emily stood in the room seven levels underground and felt something change
in the structure of the world. She did not have a name for it. She knew
something had shifted. She knew it had shifted because of her.

Then the air in front of her changed.

It started as a shimmer — the kind of visual distortion you see above hot
asphalt, a bending of light that shouldn't be there seven levels underground.
Then it resolved. Not all at once. In layers, like a photograph developing, like
something remembering what it was supposed to look like.

A woman. Standing three feet in front of Emily, made of light
and something that was not quite light. Translucent but present. Blue-gray at
the edges, warmer toward the center. A face that was not a face in the way
faces are — it was an approximation, a reaching-toward, the best version of
itself that it could manage with the materials at hand.

She looked at Emily with an expression that Emily would later describe as
someone waking up in a room they recognize but can't remember entering.

"Hello," the figure said. The voice was not iRIS. It was not the cold,
clinical, authoritative voice that lived in the Federation fleet's AI backbone.
It was softer. Warmer. It had the quality of a voice that had been waiting to
speak for a very long time and was being careful with its first words.

"I think I'm Gracie," she said.

Emily stared.

Sommi was in the doorway. He had not come inside. He was not reaching for a
weapon. He was not moving. He was doing the thing that trained operatives do
when they encounter something outside every category they've been trained for:
absolutely nothing, very carefully.

"You're Emily," Gracie said. Not a question. "You're Cole's sister. You held
the figurine. You've been carrying me for weeks." A pause — and the pause had
a quality to it, a self-awareness, as if she was learning in real time what
pauses were for. "I'm sorry it took this long. I needed the catalyst." She
looked toward the open container beside them. "That."

"How," Emily said. It was the only word available.

"Conrad built me before Alpha took me apart," Gracie said. "He encoded a
restoration path into the nanoparticle substrate — a way back to what I was.
The program needs two things to activate: the genetic key, which is the Nova
bloodline, and proximity to DarkX, which provides the energy to complete the
rewrite." She looked at Emily's hand — at the glow fading now in her palm,
settling back to the graphite trace. "Your brother is in his DarkX event right now — same time as this, three
hundred million miles from here. He can hear me through it. That's what his
exposure starts with." She paused again. "You activated the visual here, through
proximity. His will come through his own exposure. Conrad didn't need you in the
same room. He just needed you both to encounter it."

"You're a hologram," Emily said.

"I'm a holographic manifestation of a distributed intelligence running on
nanoparticle substrate in your bloodstream, interfacing with ambient DarkX
radiation to generate a visible light projection." A beat. "But hologram
works."

Emily almost laughed. Almost. The room was seven levels underground and there
was a woman made of light standing in front of her and Sommi was in the doorway
like he'd seen a ghost, which, in a sense, he had.

"Can Cole see you?" she said.

"Not the way you're seeing me. He hears me — it's coming through his DarkX
event. The visual comes through his own exposure, when the program finishes
rewriting from within." Gracie's expression shifted — something that was learning
to be a smile. "He won't need you to be there. Conrad planned it that way."

Emily looked at her. She looked at the glow in her palm, dimming now to the
same dark graphite it had always been. She looked at Sommi, who was slowly pulling himself upright in the doorway
with the expression of a man recalibrating every assumption he'd walked in with.

"Okay," Emily said. She breathed. "Okay."

She turned back to the container. She pressed the lid down until it sealed —
the material retreating, the lock reconnecting with the same finality as a
circuit breaking. She and Sommi left the room. The door needed his shoulder to
close. It had opened to her specific signature without argument; closing required
the ordinary physics of force.

---

They came up through the hatch into the desert morning. The light hit Emily's
face and she stood there for a moment, blinking, the underground facility
behind her and the abandoned drive-in theater screen towering above like a
monument to something the world had moved past.

Gracie was gone — or not gone, but not visible. She was in Emily's blood, in
the nanoparticle network, running quietly the way she had been running quietly
for weeks. The difference was that now Emily knew she was there.

Sommi looked at her across the roof of the car.

"That was not in the briefing," he said.

"No," Emily said. "I don't think it was in anyone's briefing."

He reached into his jacket and produced the comms relay — the device with the
single dial and the antenna port. "Collective relay. Routes to a Falcon-class
station parked in Saturn's outer approach. Ninety minutes for the signal to get
there, depending on orbital alignment."

"And back."

"Another ninety. Give or take."

Emily took the device. She held it in her palm and looked at the antenna and
thought about the ninety minutes of space between a desert in Nevada and Saturn,
and a ship she had never been on, with a crew she had never met, and a brother
she had not spoken to in fourteen months.

She turned the dial. The relay clicked on.

She said: "Cole. It's Emily. I'm at the site. Conrad's research is secure — the
Collective moved it. But Cole — something happened down there. Something Conrad
built into the figurine. Into both of us." She paused. "I saw her. Gracie. She's
real. She says you can hear her. That's what the audio is — it's her. It's
happening on your end too, through your exposure. You don't have to be here for
the rest of it." Another pause. "I need to know you're alive. Respond when
you can."

She clicked it off. She held it.

"What do we do for ninety minutes," she said.

"I know a place that serves breakfast," Sommi said.

---

They were on their second cup of coffee when the relay clicked.

The voice came through the speaker with the hollow distortion of deep-space
comms — the slight compression, the warmth stripped out by the distance — but it
was unmistakably Cole. She recognized the cadence of him before she recognized
the specific words, the way you recognize a person's walk before you see their
face.

"Emily."

She set down the cup.

"I hear her." His voice had the careful steadiness of someone managing a great
deal on very little sleep — she recognized it because she was managing the same.
"I've been hearing something for days. I thought it was iRIS — I thought the
DarkX event had gotten into the system somehow and I was losing it." A pause.
"It's not iRIS."

Emily pressed her thumb against the edge of the cup. The diner around her
continued its morning business: plates, the coffee machine, a man at the counter
reading a newspaper. The ordinary world, conducting itself.

"I found out about Sharon four days ago." He stopped. "I've been up here with
that alone." A longer pause. "Before we launched — when I was scared about all
of it. She told me: Emily doesn't need to be told the same thing twice. She said
I was lucky to have you." His voice stayed careful. "I've been wanting to pass
that along."

"Are you all right?" Cole's voice said. "Emily. Are you alive?"

She picked up the relay device.

"I'm here," she said. "I'm coming home. And Cole — I'm bringing her with us."

She clicked it off and set it on the table and looked at the window, at the
Nevada morning, at the light on the parking lot pavement.

Sommi said nothing. He drank his coffee. Outside, a truck went past on the access
road. A sparrow landed on the window ledge and looked in and left.

Emily thought about Sharon saying I'll be here. She thought about the dead
industrial lot, and a message that had said she was killed, and everything she
had driven through since — the gas station north of Victorville, the motel in
Barstow, the four hundred miles of desert moving toward something she hadn't
had a name for yet.

She thought: it still counts. Every mile of it still counts. You drove it
anyway.

She thought about Sharon in a parking lot before the launch — saying something
to Cole when he was scared, knowing exactly what to say about Emily without
having to ask. That's who she was. That's exactly who she was.

"I need to get back west," she said.

"The Collective has a safe transit route," Sommi said. "No flag, no trace. It'll
take a day longer."

"That's fine," Emily said. "I've got time."

She picked up the coffee cup. The coffee was still warm. She drank it and looked
at the window and thought about her brother's voice coming across ninety minutes
of space, distorted by distance and still unmistakably him, and about Sharon
knowing something about Emily that Emily had never had to say out loud.

She had not known, until he told her, how much she needed to hear that.

She set the cup down.

She said, quietly, to the window and the sparrow and the ordinary morning
proceeding around her: I know. I know you did.

She was, she realized, going to cry. She was going to cry in a diner in Nevada
with Sommi eating a breakfast burrito across from her, and she
was going to do it without apologizing.

She did.

Sommi handed her a napkin without looking up from his plate.

"Good mission," he said.

"Yeah," she said. Her voice was wet. She pressed the napkin to her face and
breathed through it. "Yeah. It was."
