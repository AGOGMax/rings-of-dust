RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Twenty-Six

The Broadcast

The signal went out from Station Libre at 6:02 AM, Pacific Standard Time. By the time the Federation-affiliated channels were aware of it, two hundred thousand people had already seen the opening segment.

FED-HEX-5555 picked it up twelve seconds after it went live.

The twelve-second lag was the physics of the distance — ship to Earth, Earth to ship, the same mathematics that made everything here twelve seconds old before it arrived. Kate had spent the better part of a year not noticing it. Today she noticed it.

---

Petyr had been in the galley since five-thirty.

He had found the coffee machine on the first day aboard and had been thinking about it ever since — the real kind, a steam wand, actual pressure, not the compressed-tablet situation they had been running on the Meridian for three years. He had asked Gracie three times whether it was actually real and she had confirmed it three times with what he chose to interpret as patience.

He made enough for everyone. He was still making it when the others started arriving.

Cole came in first, then Heavy, then Sam, then Dayo. Loa took a mug without looking at it. Kate came last and stood at the back of the room near the relay panel, which she had authorized for full broadcast without asking anyone, because nobody would have said no. Sharon was in the doorway — not at the table, not standing, just present, leaning against the frame with her arms folded. The augmented side caught the light differently than the other. Bitboy was in the corridor behind her, watching the room from a safe distance.

The table was larger than the Meridian's entire galley. Nobody mentioned this. It had been mentioned enough.

Gracie materialized at the end of the table in the seat nobody had sat in. In front of her, rendered in careful holographic detail, was a bowl of popcorn. The texture was correct. The steam was correct. She reached into it, took a handful, and brought it to her face.

Heavy looked at the bowl.

No, Gracie said. But it's the right thing to have.

She took another handful.

Nobody commented.

---

They watched Ray's opening segment with the coffee in hand. Kate stood at the relay panel and said nothing. Dayo made a note in his log — routine calibration, full-spectrum civilian relay — and Petyr read it over his shoulder and said nothing. Cole sat at the end of the table and watched the FuelCells on the bench behind Ray's left shoulder and thought about the locker. The lazy Friday afternoon. The glow.

When DeMint read Conrad's statement, the room was very quiet.

Sharon shifted in the doorway.

When Marcus's recording played, Petyr made a sound that was not quite a word.

"He's going to walk," Petyr said.

"He's already walking," Kate said. "He walked before we started watching. That's not what today is about."

"What is today about."

Kate looked at the display. "Today is about the three-hundred-and-sixty-ninth test run. Today is about Conrad's notebooks being in the public record. Today is about DeMint reading the thing out loud." She paused. "He can walk. The thing he was managing is out. That's the work. The rest is logistics."

Petyr was quiet. On the display, the Nevada segment was beginning.

When Rachel Vayes closed the broadcast, Petyr made the sound again.

"My name is Rachel Vayes," she said. She looked at the camera — level, unhurried, present. "The Collective is not a political organization. It is not a movement. It is a group of people who decided to spend a decade building the world they wanted inside the walls of the world that existed. Today the walls came down a little. The work has a different shape now." She paused. "That's all I wanted to say."

The broadcast ended.

Petyr sat for a long time after it ended. He had the droplet patch in his hand — the matte black circle he'd been issued when Kate had brought him into the network, the one he'd received in an unremarkable ceremony that felt, looking back, entirely insufficient for what it was confirming. "I thought it was a formality," he said, eventually. "I thought it was a membership card. The patch doesn't make you a member." He turned it over. "You're already a member. The patch is just — it's just confirmation of something that was already there."

"Yes," Dayo said.

"Sharon had it without the patch."

A beat. Nobody looked at the doorway. It was that kind of room.

"Yes."

"Rachel had it for eleven years without anyone being able to see it."

"That," Kate said, "is what a star looks like from the outside."

Petyr looked at her.

She didn't explain further. She watched him sit with it — the shape of a commitment so long-sustained it became structural — and thought that for someone who had been in the network for less than two years, he was learning fast.

"The network needs people who choose it," she said. "Not as assets. As people who chose it." She paused. "Rachel's been choosing it since before we had a name for what she was doing."

On the display, the relay was running the Nevada segment on loop — someone in the network had flagged it as the sequence most likely to reach people who were still deciding whether to believe the rest.

---

Cole had been watching the third shot since it first played. He had watched it twice more when the network ran the loop.

Gracie, he said.

I see her, Gracie said.

Can you route something through the Meridian?

Already composing, Gracie said. Take your time.

He thought about what to say. He had been thinking about what to say for six weeks — since the relay from the cabin had come through, three words in Gracie's handwriting that were also Sharon's handwriting, which meant the same thing: Hi. I'm here.

He thought about the fourth row. He thought about the gray jacket. He thought about eleven hundred miles and an empty lot and a road atlas, and what it meant to be seen by someone who had been paying attention.

He typed: I saw you in the background of the Nevada segment. You looked like yourself.

He paused. He added: I'll see you in eight weeks.

He set the relay device down and watched the popcorn bowl.

Gracie took another handful. The steam rose and dispersed and rose again, the way it does in a closed system that has nowhere else to go.
