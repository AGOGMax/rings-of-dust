RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Twenty-Four

Station Libre

Red left with the car fifteen minutes after they walked out of the park.

The city was still in its early hour, the market district not yet at full voice.
Ray had been here long enough to know the timing of it — which corners filled
first, how the light came down between the towers, which routes looped back on
themselves and which ones didn't. He was done knowing that. He watched it through
the window and did not feel like saying goodbye to it, and did not need to.

They were in a car within ten minutes. Gray sedan, local plates. A driver Ray
hadn't been introduced to and didn't need to be.

Jill had the transit case. She had set it on the seat between them and rested her
hand on it with the ease of something carried a long way already — not nervous,
not ceremonial. Her notebook was on her lap. She was looking out the window at
Panama City as it passed.

Ray looked at it too.

He had learned this city the way you learn a place you are careful in — not as a
resident, not exactly as a stranger, but as someone who had learned which corners
were visible from which windows and which routes were efficient for a contractor
who belonged here versus a man who was watching the place. He had walked these
streets long enough that it had changed his gait. He would need to unlearn that.

The glass towers went by. The park went by — a green shape between buildings that
might have been the heron fountain, already behind them. The city thinned to
highway. Highway to road. Road to nothing.

Red was in the passenger seat on her phone, saying numbers in a low voice. When
she hung up she turned.

"Two hours to pickup." She looked at Ray. "The station has new equipment. Jill's
shipment came in three weeks ago."

Jill was still looking out the window.

"They've been waiting for you specifically," Red said.

"Then they'll be ready when I arrive," she said.

Red looked at Ray. Ray had no addition to make.

He looked at the transit case. "Where does it come from. The source material."

Jill looked at the case too, in the way you look at something you have been
carrying long enough that it has become unremarkable. Then she looked back out
the window.

"An asteroid about three kilometers across," she said. "Spent thirty years as a
soap manufacturing outpost. Industrial scale — they were supplying cleaning product
supply chains for the inner stations. Then it became something else." She was
quiet for a moment. "It makes fuel cells now. It sends them into a gravity void
and gets gold back."

Ray sat with this for a moment.

"A soap factory," he said.

"Not anymore," she said.

He looked at the transit case. Then at her. "And it sends cells into a gravity void and gets gold back."

"Every eleven days," she said. "Since the eighties."

Ray looked out the window.

The highway opened up. The last of Panama City fell behind them, and ahead was
nothing but distance and a Collective transport running on a schedule that did
not appear on any manifest.



Ray had been told: you'll feel it. He hadn't been told what "it" meant. He found
out at T-plus-four-minutes when the orbital transfer burn pushed him sideways
into the harness at 2.5 Gs and held him there for forty seconds while his vision
tunneled and the contents of his stomach expressed strong opinions about the
situation.

Then it stopped.

The ship coasted.

He opened his eyes on a window that showed him the curve of the Earth from low
orbit — lit white and blue, the terminator line bisecting the Pacific in a slow
diagonal — and felt the unmistakable feeling of being somewhere he had absolutely
no business being.

Jill was in the harness two seats forward. She had her notebook open in her lap.
She was looking at the window with the expression of someone running new data
through existing models.

"First time?" the woman next to him said.

Her name was Yael. She'd been doing this run for two years. She handed him a bag
without being asked.

He didn't need it. He thought about needing it. He took the bag anyway.

"Gets easier," she said. "Third time you don't even brace."

"What's the second time like?"

"Worse," she said. "Because you know it's coming."



The station had a name that the Collective used internally — Station Libre, though
Ray never heard anyone say the full name. They said "the station" or, after a few
weeks, nothing at all, because there was only one place you could be saying.

It was built in three modules connected by pressurized corridors that were wide
enough for one person, which meant that two people passing each other had to do
a sideways shuffle that everyone had a version of and nobody liked. The common
module was the chow hall. The science module had been converted to labs. The
residential module had twelve berths and a shared bathroom arrangement that had
required, over the course of several years, the development of a detailed social
contract.

Jill came through the docking tube, set a crate of equipment down in the
corridor, and took in the residential module in about thirty seconds.

"One bathroom," she said.

"Yes," Ray said.

"For thirteen people." She looked at the door. Looked at the crate. Looked at the door again. "Some of this gold is going toward plumbing. That's the first investment." She picked the crate back up. "Let's see the lab."

Ray had been given a berth and a lab bench and told that his access to the dark
matter cache was conditional on a two-week orientation period. He spent the two
weeks learning the station's rhythms — the shift schedule, the hot water queue,
the specific drawer in the kitchen where the good coffee was kept and the
separate drawer where the bad coffee was kept in case of emergencies — and
observing the people who had been here longer.

There were eleven of them. Scientists, mostly. One communications technician. One
person whose role Ray was never able to clearly identify but who seemed to fix
things when they broke, which they did with the unhurried regularity of things
that lived at pressure differential.

Jill was not on the two-week restriction. She had come up with a crate of
upgraded equipment — the colony's gold windfall surplus converted into resonance
scanners and precision instruments and three replacement pressure seals that the
station had needed for a year and a half. Before Ray had finished his second day
of orientation, she had reorganized the lab bench layout, taught the
communications technician how to calibrate the new scanner, and established an
understanding with the person Ray couldn't classify about the pressure seal
installation sequence. The eleven people who had been here longer were, by the
end of the first week, deferential to her in ways they were not deferential to
Ray.

He did not find this surprising.

Jill brought the colony's hospitality to Station Libre the same way the colony had given it to her: without announcing it, without making it about herself, just putting something on the table and letting people eat. The first morning she cooked for the station crew — actual food, grown and prepared, in a facility that had been running on ration blocks for three years — the silence at the table was the silence of people remembering what meals used to be. She just set the plates down.

Ray noticed. You could tell a lot about a person by what they fixed first when they had the resources to fix anything. Jill had fixed the food before she fixed the lab.

But she fixed the lab too. And then the corridor seals. And then the water recycler, which had been running at sixty percent for months because nobody had the parts. Jill had the parts. She had brought them from a colony that had spent years learning how to make do, and the making-do had taught her exactly which things broke first and what you needed to un-break them. She spent the colony's gold the way someone spends money who has never had it and knows exactly what it's for — not carefully, not recklessly, but with the specific joy of a person putting right the things that should never have been wrong.

By week two the station had actual coffee in both drawers. Better pressure readings in all three modules. A resonance scanner that didn't need to be recalibrated every six hours. The emergency ration blocks were still there, but nobody was eating them anymore.

Ray watched her work and felt something he did not have a name for settling into the space between them. It was not romantic in the way stories made romance — no dramatic recognition, no single moment of clarity. It was more like: she was competent in ways that made him want to be in the room, and he was competent in ways that kept her coming back to his bench, and the distance between their stations got shorter every week without either of them noticing.

The introduction in Panama had not been comfortable — two strangers in a transit house with Alpha's people in the streets outside, each carrying years of work they had never been able to share with anyone. What established quickly: they both understood what the notebooks meant, they both knew what Station Libre required, and neither of them was waiting for permission. The attraction developed during the station mission — shared craziness, shared work, the charged chemistry of two people who have both spent years carrying something and can finally set it down with someone else in the room.

The colony had been one of the Collective's primary financial backers for seven years. Relay infrastructure. Safe houses on three transit routes. Two Meridian resupply runs. And now Station Libre — the equipment Jill had just carried up in a crate. Jill had not known this when she first landed on the asteroid. Two visits, less than a month total — she had come in low on fuel, stayed to watch a void ceremony, and left with notebooks. Nobody had told her during either visit that she was standing inside one of the Collective's primary funding sources. The Collective had been operating, in part, on money the colony had generated before she arrived. Before she was part of it. Before she knew there was an it to be part of.

They were, without exception, at ease in a way he hadn't seen people at ease
before. The station produced it — the enforced smallness of the space, the
distance from everything else, the shared project. They had arguments about small
things and agreements about large ones. They ate dinner together because the table only sat twelve — technically eleven plus the folding chair someone had found in the third week, which made twelve, which was now one short — and it was efficient.

In the third week, someone brought out the bottle.



The game was called Whiskey Slap, which was a straightforward name for a
straightforward game. In zero gravity, you couldn't spin a bottle on a table, so
you spun it in the air — a proper glass bottle, transported up at some point for
precisely this purpose, now empty and serving its second career as sporting
equipment. Someone launched it from the center of the module; whoever it drifted
toward when it stabilized took a pull of whiskey from a second bottle and got
slapped by the spinner.

Not hard. Hard enough.

"Why the slap?" Ray asked, the first time they played.

"Because in zero-g," Yael said, "the burn takes longer to hit. Slap helps. Also,
you need something to hold you in place."

The slap hit at roughly the same time as the whiskey.

Ray lost twice his first round. He took the first shot from Yael and received the slap — open palm, measured, professional. She'd done this before.

The bottle spun again. It pointed at Ray.

Jill was the spinner.

She took the bottle from Yael and handed it to him. Their fingers touched on the bottle. She watched him drink it — not looking away, not performing anything, just watching with the steady attention she brought to everything she did.

Then she slapped him.

Open palm, left side, precise and not gentle. His head turned with it. The sound was louder than it needed to be in the small module. When he turned his head back she was looking at him with an expression he hadn't seen before. Not apologetic. Not playful. Something closer to: now you know what my hand feels like.

The moment held for a beat longer than the game required.

Someone at the table said something. Neither of them heard it.

"Welcome to the station," Jill said. Her voice was steady but her eyes hadn't moved from his.

"Thank you," Ray said, meaning it — and not because of the whiskey.

The game continued. Ray's face burned on the left side for twenty minutes. He did not mind.



He found it on Day one hundred and eighty.

Not a dramatic notation in the lab book. More like: he had been running the standard protocol, and it was on day eleven — the morning the resonance in the casings went quiet and the sync completed — that he made the first discovery the station made possible.

He had been scanning all 777 casings. Routine post-sync check. He was at Cell
304 when he stopped.

Cell 304 had something on the outside.

Not internal growth. Exterior — a mass of darker matter crystals on the outer
surface of the casing, larger than anything he had pulled from a single unit at
Alpha. He measured it. He measured it again. He finished scanning the rest of
the Grid.

The other 776 cells had nothing on the outside.

He went and got Jill.

She was in the common module replacing the air filtration gaskets — another item from the crate, another thing the station had needed and learned to live without. She had grease on her hands and one of the colony's work scarves tied back over her hair. She looked at his face and put the gasket down.

"Show me," she said.

Jill looked at Cell 304. She looked at the other 739. She opened Elsa's notebook — the third volume, the one with the cloth cover worn thin at the spine — and turned to a page she had marked with a strip of pressure tape. She held the notebook next to the scanner readout.

The numbers matched.

"One out of seven seventy-seven," she said. "Elsa called it the Jackpot yield. Page two-twelve." She tapped the notebook entry — Elsa's handwriting, small and precise, forty years old. "She predicted the ratio. She predicted the magnitude. She just never had seven hundred and forty cells to test it on."

"Random distribution," Ray said. "You won't know which cell until you scan all of them. And it only happens once — after the initial sync. Not again. You get one Jackpot per new Grid. You just don't know where it's going to land."

"And the magnitude—"

"Larger than anything Alpha has on record from a single unit. By a significant
margin."

Jill sat down. Not because she didn't understand it. Because she did. Because a woman she had never met — whose notebooks she had carried across the solar system in a transit case, whose handwriting she had learned to read the way you learn to read a friend's — had written this down in a room by herself on an asteroid, and nobody had tested it. Until now.

"She saw this," Jill said quietly. She was looking at the notebook, not at Ray. "She wrote it down forty years ago and nobody tested it."

She was quiet for a moment. Then:

"At the colony, on my second visit — Day 18. Eleven days after the ceremony." She
looked at the scanner readout, not at him. "The asteroid that came back was twice
the usual size. A gold crater on the face of it, solid gold all the way across.
Petra said Jackpot. Just that word. Like she'd been waiting to say it."

Ray didn't speak.

"That was the day I went back to my ship," she said. "And then came back. And
asked about the notebooks." She paused. "I didn't understand why at the time. I
just knew I wasn't done." She looked at Cell 304. "I think that was the sign."

She said it the way a person says something that resists a better word — not as
superstition, not as theology. Just: the most accurate available description of
what had happened to her on a day she hadn't known was going to matter.

It became a thing they did — at the end of every new Grid's first sync cycle,
whoever walked the casings first said the same word when they found it. Quietly.
The way you say something that is true and large and you are not yet sure what
to do with.

Jackpot.

Nobody had decided this would happen. It had simply started happening and
continued. And every time someone said it, Jill would open the notebook to page two-twelve and add a tally mark in the margin next to Elsa's prediction. A small ritual. A confirmation.

He did not know that the exact same word had been shouted in Alpha's laboratories
— years earlier, by people in better-lit rooms with better instruments, who had
found the same thing and understood immediately what it meant for everyone who
was not in the room.

They kept the Grid locked.

Day 33, Ray ran the scan.

The notebooks said 10×. Interior acceleration across all locked casings — ten
times the unlocked rate. Elsa had documented it in the first third of her runs.
The intermediate signal, the first confirmation the lock was working.

The reading came back at 10×.

He checked the calibration. He ran it again. He pulled Elsa's volume two and
matched the protocol step by step. He ran it a third time.

10×. Exact.

He logged it: Day 33. Grade 1 confirmed. Interior: 10×.

He also logged the exterior — trace deposits on twelve cells, small, within
calibration variance. He labeled them SCATTER / CELL 304 SECONDARY SHED and
moved on. The interior was what the notebooks were tracking. The interior was
the story.

---

Day 66: interior at 25×. Grade 2. He logged it, matched it against Elsa's table,
confirmed. The exterior traces had grown from the twelve cells at Day 33 — more
cells now, slightly larger deposits — but the logs read the same: scatter,
consistent with Cell 304 shedding, insufficient for conclusion. He labeled them
and moved on.

Day 132: 60×. Grade 3. Jill read the result over his shoulder and went back to
the bench without comment. The exterior was now measurable across a significant
fraction of the Grid — not large, not alarming, the way static sounds before
you know what you're listening for. He noted them in the log with the same
label he'd been using since Day 1.

Around day 150, between grades, he ran out of useful work and started doing the
kind that was really just waiting with his hands occupied. Jill caught him
recalibrating an instrument that didn't need it and made him stop.

The supply shuttle docked on day 161. By now the docking alert drew most of the
station — not for the water or the parts, but because Jill had put in a standing
food order and nobody knew until the crates opened what she'd decided to send up
next. The crew gathered at the airlock — they had been eating well for months and had strong opinions about what came next.

Ray was at the lab bench when the airlock cycled, and he didn't look up until a
voice he knew said, in the careful tone of a man who'd spent the whole flight
rehearsing being casual about it:

"So this is where the science is. I wanted to see the equipment for myself."

Vince. In a borrowed flight suit, gripping the rail with both hands the way
everyone does their first day in zero gravity, faintly green and entirely
delighted.

Ray crossed the module in two pushes and they collided into the clumsy floating
version of an embrace, and for a second neither of them said anything, because
there was nothing that covered it.

"You took the number," Ray said.

"I took the number. I read everything you left. Then I read it again. Then I
called." He let go of the rail to gesture and immediately had to grab it again.
"You were right, Ray. About all of it. I just needed to be somewhere I could see
it with my own instruments."

Vince joined the team for the last stretch the way he joined everything — by
asking sharp questions and being right about half of them. He understood the
cells better than anyone aboard except Ray and Jill, and within a week the three
of them had fallen into the old rhythm of people who'd argued lattice geometry
before — except now there was no badge to scan and no column he wasn't allowed
to read. He slept badly in zero gravity, complained that the corridor had been designed
by someone who had never met another person traveling in the opposite direction,
and stood at the data wall for hours, and the station was better for having him
in it.

He found the Whiskey Slap game on his third evening aboard. He had not understood
what "hard enough" meant until Yael demonstrated it. He understood afterward. He
came back the following week with a more realistic mental model and only marginally
better results.

---

On Day 231, Ray ran the count.

He didn't know why he ran it. He had been at this long enough to recognize the
difference between purposeful work and the kind his hands did when his mind was
circling something it hadn't named yet. He walked the Grid — all 777 casings —
and counted. He came out at 777. He walked it again. Still 777. He did it a
third time with his finger on the casings, touching each one, because the number
had started looking wrong and he needed to prove it wasn't.

Seven hundred and seventy-seven.

The interior reading that morning was 150×. Grade 4, Elsa's notebook, page
ninety-three, labeled HIGH CHARGE, underlined once. He had confirmed it on the
evening scan and noted it and set the notebook aside.

After the count he stood at the wall where he'd been taping the exterior scan
printouts since Day 1.

He had started the log as a record of Cell 304's Jackpot deposits — tracking
the fade to rule out contamination. It was supposed to close out by Day 22. But
the trace readings on other cells had persisted, and he had kept logging, and
the wall log had grown into something eight months long.

He pulled the printouts and spread them on the bench in order.

Grade 0, cycles 1 and 2: trace. One cell. Then three.

Grade 1, Day 33: twelve cells. Slightly larger — he had noted it and moved on.

Grade 2, Day 66: more cells, measurably larger. He had labeled it scatter.

Grade 3, Day 132: a significant fraction of the Grid. He had noted the spatial
distribution at the time — if Cell 304 were shedding, the deposits would
concentrate near it. They didn't. He had written "uniform distribution —
possible contamination event" and filed it.

Grade 4, Day 231: all 777 cells. Exterior deposits cycling on the eleven-day
interval. And they were not small.

He had labeled this scatter eleven times. It was not scatter.

He had been looking for a cliff. He had been walking past a slope.

He went and got Jill.

She was already awake — he found her in the corridor outside the lab, holding
two cups of the actual coffee she'd stocked, as if she'd known. She handed him
one. She followed him in.

He spread the printouts on the bench without explaining. He pointed.

Jill looked at them for a long time. She had the notebook open on the bench
beside the scanner — volume four, the last volume, the one Elsa had written in
the months before she left the colony for the last time. The page was open to
a section labeled THEORETICAL PROJECTION: EXTENDED LOCK SCENARIO. Elsa's
handwriting was smaller here, tighter, as if she'd been writing fast and trying
to fit everything in.

Jill held the notebook next to the wall log. Grade by grade, interior and
exterior both. One curve.

"It's been climbing since the first cycle," Ray said. "I read it as noise."

Jill looked at the projection. She looked at the printouts. She looked at the
projection again.

"She was right," Jill said. Her voice was not loud. "She was right about
everything."

She looked at the Grade 4 reading. Then at the projection table, which did not
stop at Grade 4.

Grade 5: 375×. Day 363.
Grade 6: 900×. Day 495.
Grade 7: 2,000×. Day 627. ZERO CONDITION.

"It doesn't stop at 150," she said.

"No," he said.

Jill closed the notebook gently — the way you close something that belongs to
someone who trusted you with it. She pressed her palm flat on the cover for a
moment.

"The shortage," she said.

"Was a choice," Ray said. "They knew that much. Knew it before they fired me."

He was thinking about Alpha's face in the lab. The specific expression when he'd
shown them the flywheel connection — the room going quiet in the way rooms go
quiet when people understand the implications of something they are about to
suppress. They had seen what it meant before he'd fully finished the sentence.
They'd fired him before he could take it further.

They had never taken it further. They had never worked in zero gravity. They had
never locked long enough to find a grade — and even if they had, they had never
stayed long enough to find out it was a ladder, not a ledge.

The zero-gravity lock — the grade that climbed in eleven-day steps without
ceiling, that Elsa had mapped all the way to 627 days and 2,000 times and
labeled Zero Condition and underlined twice — belonged to Elsa Klein and to Ray
and to this station and to nobody else.

They'd been wrong about that.

Jill was quiet beside him. She was looking at the display with the same
expression she'd had when she slapped him — not gentle, not cruel, just present
and exact.

"Elsa knew," she said. "And the colony knew. And they kept her notebooks for
whoever came next." She looked at Ray. "That was you."

"That was us," he said.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. The display cycled through its
readings. The Earth rotated in the window behind them.

Vince had come in a step behind Jill that morning — he'd taken to shadowing the
morning readings since the shuttle dropped him off. He was in the room for all
of it. He'd watched the printouts get laid out on the bench, grade by grade,
and understood what it meant before Ray finished. He was standing with one hand
on the rail and the other over his mouth, eyes wet — a man watching a thing he
spent his whole career being lied about turn out, in the end, to be true and
good and bigger than the lie. When Ray finally looked up from the readout, Vince
was gripping the rail with both hands.

"You were right," Vince said. Like it still surprised him. Like it would keep
surprising him.

"We were right," Ray said — and meant the we to take him in: the man who had
spent four and a half years as the honest face of the lie, standing here now in
the room where the truth finally paid out.



He ran the unlock experiment three times. Jill was beside him for every run, the
notebook open, tracking each result against Elsa's documented predictions.

The first time, he broke the lock on a unit at Day 22. Interior: dropped to
unlocked baseline. Exterior: the trace deposits vanished within hours. He
re-locked it immediately. Thirty-three days later it was back at Grade 1. The
grade was recoverable. What you lost was the time to rebuild it.

"She called it grade loss," Jill said, turning to the relevant page. "Lock
removed, the grade dissolves. Re-lock from Grade 0. The cell is undamaged — but
the accumulation is gone. You earn it back or you don't." She looked at the
readout. "She documented it in zero gravity. She knew."

Second run: unlocked at Grade 1, Day 33. Interior dropped from 10× to baseline.
Thirty-three days of grade: gone. He re-locked immediately. The cell started over.

Third run: unlocked at Grade 3, Day 132. Interior at 60×. He broke the lock. The
cell returned to baseline within hours. One hundred and thirty-two days of
accumulation, three grades: gone. Not slowly — at once, the way a held breath
leaves.

Consistent across all three runs: unlock before Zero Condition and you lose your
grade. You cannot harvest and maintain the lock. You cannot take the return and
keep what built it. You choose one or the other, and in zero gravity, you are
choosing inside something you cannot see until you understand what the lock is
building.

The cells themselves were undamaged. Lock again from Day 0. The loss was not just
time — it was the grade you had built, and you had to build it again from the
beginning. Every grade. Every cycle.

That was the entire sacrifice. Lock the cell and hold it — give up what it would
have yielded day by day, give up the early return, give up the ability to open
what you've built and know how much is inside. The colony had been doing a version
of this for decades without knowing the full mechanic: walk the cell down the east
tunnel, release it into the void, receive nothing for eleven days, trust the
return. They gave something real and waited. Alpha had always done the opposite —
harvest early, take the yield, reset, never sit with the lock long enough to find
the grade, or the grade above it, or the one after that. Two approaches to the
same mechanism. One had built a manufactured shortage. The other had been quietly
filling a moon with gold.

Alpha had always chosen harvest. They had never found out what they were giving up.

He put this in the notebook.

Three hundred and sixty-nine documented runs. Every mechanic Elsa had mapped,
every threshold she had logged. The 11-day cycle. The grade ladder. The cost of
unlock. The slope with no visible top.

All of it confirmed. All of it exact.

Jill had been there for every confirmation. She had tracked each one in the margins of Elsa's notebooks with the same small, precise marks — a check next to each prediction, a date, the station's coordinates. She was building a chain of evidence that connected a woman working alone on an asteroid forty years ago to a lab in low orbit where everything she'd written was proving true.

The colony and the Collective were fighting the same war for entirely different reasons. The Collective wanted to break the scarcity model — to prove the grid was a choice, the shortage manufactured, the crisis engineered. Civilization stakes. The colony was not fighting for civilization. The colony was fighting for the right to walk down a tunnel in the dark and let go. For the void. For the sacrifice. They did not care about the grid. They cared about what the cell became at the end of the cycle. They had been here for seventeen years protecting something that Ray had discovered in a storage locker on a Tuesday, and they had never needed him to know why.

She had been right. Working alone on an asteroid with limited support, she had
documented the complete theoretical framework for a mechanism that changed the
energy calculus of an entire civilization.

He thought about the notebooks. Worn at the corners from years of daily handling.
Left in four cloth-covered volumes for whoever came next.

He sat with the notebook in his lap for a while. Jill sat beside him. She had her own notes — pages of cross-references, Elsa's predictions matched against station data, every number confirmed. She didn't say anything. They had both carried something a long way and they were both, for the first time, able to set it down.

After a while she leaned her shoulder against his. Not much. Enough.

He did not move away.

---

Jill wrote to Tyler that night.

She didn't explain the full mechanics in the relay — Tyler understood what the
cells did, not what they were doing at a molecular level, and that had never
mattered to him. She told him what mattered: instead of walking a single cell down the east tunnel
every eleven days, lock a grid. Fill the void with as many cells as the prep room
could hold. Leave them locked — and leave them as long as you can hold them. The
return starts from the first cycle. The grade builds in steps, every eleven days,
compounding. The longer the lock holds, the larger it gets.

Not one asteroid every eleven days. All of them. And more than that — more with
every grade the lock holds.

She wrote: The void doesn't just receive. It compounds. Elsa knew. It's all in
the notebooks.

She did not know how long the relay would take to reach him. She knew he would
understand it when it did. Tyler had spent six years watching the math. He would
see what the numbers meant for the east tunnel, for the ceremony, for everything
he had been doing one cell at a time without knowing what he was leaving on the
table.

She sent the relay and went back to the lab.

---

Rachel arrived on a Tuesday, four months before the Grand Hall hearing. She came
on the same transport Ray had taken — same burn, same two and a half g, same
window with the curve of the Earth. She didn't use the bag. She had not slept
during the transit — Ray would learn, over the next two days, that she simply
did not. She operated at the same frequency at three in the morning as she did
at three in the afternoon, and the crew stopped waiting for her to flag.

She had her press credentials and a recorder and three charged batteries for the
recorder, which she'd been told to bring because the station ran on a different
voltage standard than anywhere else she'd worked.

Ray met her at the airlock. Jill was beside him — she had been reviewing the day's scan data when the docking alert came, and she'd set the notebook down and come without being asked.

He'd been told she was coming. He'd been told who she was — not her Collective
role, just her public one — and he'd recognized her face from the Alpha Corp
broadcast announcements he'd spent years watching. The one they used when they
needed someone credible to explain why the shortage wasn't their fault, why the
timeline kept moving, why patience was the reasonable position.

He'd found that interesting, before he knew anything else about her.

"Ray Vision," she said, stepping through the airlock.

"Ray," he said. "Here I'm just Ray."

She looked at him for a moment — the look of someone recalibrating, not the name
but the person attached to it. "Ray," she said. "Okay."

Then she looked at Jill.

There was a beat — the brief beat that happens when two people who are both accustomed to being the most competent person in the room meet each other for the first time. Rachel took Jill in with a single look: the work scarf, the grease under her fingernails, the way she stood in the corridor like someone who had already memorized every pressure seal and emergency exit. Jill took Rachel in the same way: the press credentials worn like armor, the steady posture of someone who had spent years standing in rooms where she was not supposed to be and never once flinching.

"Jill," Jill said. She did not extend her hand. She nodded — a nod that was also an assessment and also a welcome.

"Rachel Vayes," Rachel said. Her warmth flashed — not performed, not diplomatic, just the specific warmth of a person recognizing something in someone else. "I've been wanting to meet the person who's been keeping the Collective's lights on."

"The colony keeps the lights on," Jill said. "I just carry the crates."

Rachel smiled. It was the smile of someone who recognized deflection because she used it herself. "I've carried a few crates."

"I know," Jill said. And the way she said it made it clear she did know — not the details, not the role, but the weight. The particular weight of a woman who has been running something larger than herself for a long time.

They liked each other immediately. Ray saw it: Rachel's warmth, Jill's punk rock directness.

At dinner that evening, Vince was on the floor.

He had been on the floor since he arrived — the table sat twelve, and with Vince
the station was running two short, and someone had to go somewhere. Vince had
found a spot against the wall with his tray on his knees and declared it
acceptable. He was eating a meal Jill had made with ingredients from the last
shuttle drop and not complaining about it.

Ray had given up his chair to Rachel when she came through the airlock and had
been standing against the far wall with his tray for several minutes, holding a
conversation with Yael about pressure readings that neither of them was fully
attending to. He was doing a sort of standing wall-sit that he had not intended
to look like a wall-sit but increasingly did. Jill glanced over. She patted her
knee once without looking up from her food.

Ray sat.

Rachel watched this with a small smile she didn't bother hiding. She went back
to her dinner.

Then Vince finished, picked up his tray, and navigated toward the corridor door —
sideways, the way everyone moved through the corridor, his shoulder angled to
clear the frame. They all watched him go.

Jill said, without looking up: "Looks like the bathroom isn't the only thing that
needed expanding."

Rachel looked at her. "First thing you said when you arrived?"

"First thing," Jill said.

He showed Rachel the lab. Jill came with them — not because she was asked, but because the lab was as much hers as his now, and the data they were showing Rachel was data they had built together. He showed her the grade ladder —
the exterior log, the wall of printouts in order, the curve visible from grade 0
all the way through grade 4 and projected beyond. The crystal growth. The numbers
he had now run in triplicate across seven different FuelCell units with six
different operators to make sure that what he was looking at was not specific to
his method, his instruments, or his hope.

Jill had Elsa's notebooks open on the bench. When Rachel asked about the theoretical basis, Jill walked her through Elsa's framework — the predictions, the margins of error, the confirmation chain. She did it the way she did everything: directly, without ceremony, with the quiet authority of someone who had earned her understanding the hard way.

Rachel asked good questions. Clear, specific, the kind that showed she'd read the
background material before she arrived. She did not pretend to understand the
chemistry when she didn't, and she didn't pretend not to understand it when she
did.

"Alpha suppressed this," she said.

"Yes."

"And you're the only independent source who can verify it."

"The station can verify it. Every person here can verify it. I'm just the one
who found it first." He looked at Jill. "And she's the one who carried the proof that it had been found before."

Rachel looked at Jill. Jill looked back — steady, unperforming.

"Elsa Klein," Rachel said.

"Elsa Klein," Jill confirmed.

Rachel looked at the crystal samples on the bench. "When this goes public—"

"The shortage ends," Ray said. "Not in five years. In months. The infrastructure's
already half-built. They stopped it from working on purpose."

She was quiet for a moment.

"I know," she said.

He hadn't expected that. He looked at her.

"I've known for a long time," she said. "I needed to be able to prove it." She
gestured at the lab — at the displays, at the notebooks, at Jill's cross-reference margins. "Now I can."

Outside the module window, the Earth made its slow rotation. The terminator line
moved across the Atlantic.

"What happens next?" Ray asked.

"The Grand Hall," she said. "And then everything changes."

He looked at the curve on the display. Seven separate test runs. Seven identical
results. Jill's hand was resting on the notebook. Rachel was already composing
the story in her head — he could see it in the way her eyes moved across the data.

"Yeah," he said. "It does change everything."
