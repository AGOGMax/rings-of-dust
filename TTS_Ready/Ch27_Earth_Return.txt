RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Twenty-Seven

Earth Return

The Golden Dawn broke atmosphere over the Pacific at 0617.

The entry was textbook. Gracie ran the sequencing with the clean economy she'd built over six months. The ship's systems answered her. The heat shield protocols held. Cole had his hands on the controls and barely used them. Gracie had it. He let her have it.

Sharon was belted in the medical bay, which they had converted to a secondary
crew station for the descent. Sam had arranged it — the positioning, the harness
configuration that accommodated the augmentation. Sharon had looked at the setup
and said: thoughtful, which was her way of acknowledging that Sam had done
something considerate without making it a thing.

Heavy was at the co-pilot station, arms folded, watching the atmosphere go
orange through the viewport with the expression he wore when he was feeling
something and keeping it quiet. Bitboy was in his harness beside
Cole, both sets of arms crossed over his chest, watching the orange through the
forward glass with enormous interested eyes.

The G-forces built. The ship pressed them into their seats. The orange outside
the viewport went white and then went the blue of high altitude and then went
the blue of actual sky — the real sky, the one with weather in it, the one that
had birds in it, the one they had not been inside for almost a year.

Cole looked at the blue.

He had thought, coming back from his first deep-space mission, that the re-entry
was the wrong way to feel it — the violence of the descent, the heat and the
noise and the press of gravity reclaiming you, was not a dignified way to return
to the world. He had spent years thinking the right way to come home would be
slower, quieter, more deliberate.

He understood now that the right way to come home is any way that works.

The Golden Dawn settled through the cloud layer. The Pacific appeared below
them, gray and vast and running endlessly to the horizon. Cole could see the
California coast — the dark line of land becoming distinct, the fog sitting in
the valleys, the mountains catching the early light.

"Federation Space Command," Gracie said, in the calm register she always used
for the things that mattered most, "in range for final approach. Runway seven is
clear."

"Copy," Cole said.

"Welcome home," she said.

He kept his hands on the controls.

---

The return had been eight weeks. Faster than the outbound trip — FED-HEX-5555 was built for speed in a way the Golden Dawn was not, and Sam had run the burn calculation twice before she believed it. They had docked the Golden Dawn to FED-HEX's starboard lock and the Meridian to port, running all three drive systems in tandem: FED-HEX's main engines, the Golden Dawn's boosters, the Meridian's auxiliary thrust burning whatever was left in her tanks. The Meridian had not objected to being used as a thruster. Kate had said she thought the ship would find it fitting.

In the cargo hold, secured and logged and sealed in the transit containers Sam had designed for the trip out, was everything they had pulled from the ring plane before the Rapture closed. Every canister of DarkX. Every harvested cell. The full manifest, transferred from the Golden Dawn before departure and verified twice. They were bringing it home.

Sam spent the first of the eight weeks at her station, the middle ones arguing with
Heavy about routes they were not driving, and the last of them trying not
to think about the fact that they were getting closer to Earth — which meant
getting closer to the end of this particular closed container of
circumstances, and she was not ready for that.

Heavy had shown up at her station on day nine with two cups of coffee — the real kind, the one Petyr had been cataloguing since day one. He set one down beside her without asking. Sat in the second chair. Looked at the star field. He did not explain why he was there. Sam did not ask. He came back the next day. And the day after that.

He was not what she would have predicted. She had categorized him early in
the mission: capable, loyal, loud in the way that covered something quieter
underneath. She had been right about all of it and wrong about the
proportions. The quieter thing was larger. Ricardo was a man who felt things
at full volume and had spent a significant portion of his life pretending
otherwise, and in the thin-walled atmosphere of a ship running
DarkX at cruise velocity he had stopped pretending. It suited him better.

She was not a person who filled silences with noise. Neither, it turned out,
was he — not when it mattered. They had sat through enough of them together
to know.

On the twenty-second day he said: "I'm going to be honest with you about
something." She had looked at him. He had said it — plainly, without
qualification, the way Ricardo did things once he was doing them.

She had not said anything for a long time. Then she said: "I know."

The last three weeks of the journey, Sam had her own bunk and another option
and chose the other option most nights, which was the clearest data she had
collected in some time. She slept better in those eight weeks than she had in
the three years before them, which she filed without analysis. Some things are
better observed than explained. She thought: this is a thing I'd like to
keep. She thought about that, with the ship humming around them and the stars
in the wrong configuration outside the porthole and Heavy's arm where it was.

She kept the thought. She would figure out the rest later.

---

On the last night of the return, Cole stood at the forward viewport and looked at Earth. White and blue and enormous — the kind of enormous that didn't mean anything until you'd been away from it long enough.

Eight weeks wasn't very long at all, he thought.

And then: it was long enough.

---

Taylor Glenn was removed from the Golden Dawn at the runway.

He walked off under his own power, which was consistent with Taylor. He had been under quarters-restriction for the eight weeks back. Cole had checked on him once. Taylor was in his bunk, not performing anything, running the math on his situation in his careful deadpan way. The most efficient path forward was compliance. He would have arrived at that quickly.

He came down the ramp flanked by two Federation task force agents.

Sharon was already at the bottom.

She had been walking on her own for three weeks — the augmented side integrating in ways Hexo described as faster than expected and slower than she wanted. She had the balance now. She stood at the foot of the ramp with the morning light on both sides of her face, the augmented side catching it differently, and she watched Taylor come down.

When he reached the bottom, she touched his arm. The augmented hand, just above the elbow. A grip that looked like nothing. Taylor missed a step — one step — and caught himself without expression and kept walking. The agents looked at her. Sharon looked back at them. They looked away.

Cole watched this from the top of the ramp and said nothing.

---

Emily was on the tarmac.

She had driven through the night from Henderson — Sommi at the wheel for the last four hours because she had stopped being able to keep her eyes open somewhere past Bakersfield and had finally, for the first time in months, let someone else drive. She was standing at the edge of the runway in the morning light with her arms crossed and her hair unwashed and the road atlas in the car behind her and four hundred miles of desert highway in her legs.

Cole came down the ramp and saw her and stopped walking.

They looked at each other across thirty feet of runway.

She crossed it. He met her halfway.

She put her arms around him. He put his arms around her. Neither of them said anything for a while.

"You look different," she said, into his shoulder. "Like something got lighter."

"You drove," he said.

"I drove."

Emily looked up and Sharon was there — not in a chair, not at the top of the ramp. Walking. Hexo a step behind her, not guiding her, just there. Emily's face did something she had not allowed it to do since that night. She pressed it into Cole's shoulder and let it happen.

"Yeah," Cole said, holding her. "I know."

---

DeMint was on the apron. He had flown in the night before and he stood away from the reunion, the way he always stood — present, patient, reading the room. He let the ramp clear before he walked over.

"Glenn," Cole said.

"Federation custody as of this morning." DeMint was quiet for a moment. "He'll cooperate. He has information on Alpha's operational network. People, locations, timelines. He'll trade it."

Cole said nothing.

"He knew about Teez," Cole said. "He knew she was alive and where she was."

"Yes."

"Keep me informed," he said.

---

The last of the light was on the eastern ridge when Cole pulled up.

He had been giving his statement since seven that morning — everything that happened from departure to landing, in order, in the flat procedural register that the debrief required. What he left out was the part where he visited himself at three different points in his life inside a ring of DarkX vapor while the ship tried to kill him. He left that out. Thirteen hours. He drove up the mountain with the windows down.

He sat on the cabin steps.

The light was leaving the ridge. He was tired. He
reached into his breast pocket and took out the copper-wire ring — the one Hexo
had given him on the Golden Dawn, the one he had been carrying since. He sat
with it in his hand.

Headlights came up the road twenty minutes later. She parked on the gravel and got out. Then the passenger door opened, and Sommi got out. Both of them were holding bottles of orange juice.

Cole looked at them.

Emily walked toward him. He stood. She put her arms around him and he put his arms around her.

When they separated she looked at his face — the careful inventory she had
always done, the steady watchfulness of a person who had spent a long time
caring about someone from a distance and needed to confirm the actual evidence.

"You look different," she said.

"Yeah."

"Older."

"Not like that." She studied him. "Like something got lighter." She paused. "I
don't have a word for it."

"I know what it is," he said.

She looked at his hand. At the ring.

"Hexo," she said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

Behind her, Sommi stood at the edge of the gravel finishing his orange juice, giving them the space and not making a production of it.

They sat on the steps as the light left the ridge. The three of them. The pines
went from gold to gray and the air came down off the high country, cold and
clean and certain.

Cole looked at the first star.

He thought about the love and the fury and the raw sensation of both at
the same time. He felt something that was not quite fury but was adjacent to it
— directed at Marcus, at Alpha, at the machine that had taken what it had taken.
And underneath it, a thing that was quieter and larger: the recognition that
they were here. That he was sitting on these steps in the dark with the people
he needed around him, and they were here.

He thought about his father. The Harley in the driveway. The word cancer. And what the old man had given him in the cargo bay: the cell door's been open the whole time.

He sat on the steps with that for a while.

The stars were coming out over the ridge.

"She's inside," Emily said.

Cole went inside.

---

The lamp in the corner was on. Sharon was standing at the window — coffee on the sill, augmented side toward the room — looking at the dark ridge.

She heard him come in before she turned. The brain smooths the gap between the eyes automatically — she'd been told that, and it was true. She didn't feel the augmented eye leading. She only noticed it at the edges: something very fast, something in bad light, something she caught a half-beat before she was supposed to.

She turned.

Cole crossed the room and sat. She stayed standing for a moment, then came and sat across from him.

For a moment neither of them said anything.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," he said.

She looked at his face. Emily had been checking him for damage. This was something else.

"You look like yourself," she said. "More than before, actually."

"People keep saying that."

"It's true. You're all here." She turned her left hand on the armrest, palm up —
not a gesture, just a position. "There were days, before, where I could tell
that parts of you had gone somewhere else for the day and you were operating on
what was left."

"I know."

"I used to find it very annoying."

"I know that too."

Something shifted at the corner of her mouth. He knew that shift. He had been
carrying it with him the whole time she was gone, and it looked the same. There was no way to counterfeit her warmth.

"Hey," Sharon said.

He looked at her.

"Where did you go," she said.

"Somewhere I needed to be," he said.

She looked at him with the left eye and the augmented eye, both steady.

"Good place or bad place?"

"Good place," he said. "I'm back now."

She nodded. She turned her hand on the armrest again, palm up, and he moved his
chair forward and took it.

She squeezed his hand once. The augmented arm on the other armrest did not move.
The hand he was holding did.

They sat for a while in the lamplight with the pines moving in the wind outside.

Then there was a sound from the doorway.

Cole did not turn immediately. He knew the sound. He had known it for years — six mechanical limbs on a wood floor trying to be quiet and failing because they were six mechanical limbs on a wood floor.

Sharon looked at the doorway.

Bitboy was in it.

He was standing at the threshold with the cautious posture of an animal that
is trying to assess whether it's welcome in a room — body weight slightly back,
mechanical arms folded, the amber-brown eyes doing their careful scan. One of the arms was still the replacement brace from the cargo bay incident. Heavy had offered to have it properly replaced. Bitboy had refused the sedation twice. They had agreed to revisit it later.

He looked at Sharon.

Sharon looked at him.

And Sharon said, with the left eye and the augmented eye both entirely clear, in
the exact voice she had always used for it:

"Hi, you little bastard."

Bitboy made a sound — a whimper, barely held, the kind that gets out before you decide to let it.

He crossed the room in four long bounds and climbed into her lap and pressed his
face against her shoulder.

Sharon put her arms around him — both of them, the left arm and the augmented
right, both — and held on.

Cole watched them for a moment.

He reached into his jacket pocket. He took out the copper-wire ring — the one Hexo had pressed into his palm outside the recovery facility, the one he had been carrying since. He set it on the armrest beside her hand. Not a presentation. Just where she could see it.

She looked at it. She looked at him. Bitboy was still against her shoulder.

She picked it up. She put it on.

---

Emily came in twenty minutes later with two more coffees and a jar of blackberry
preserves, Sommi behind her with a second jar and the expression of
a man who has been told to bring the preserves and has done so without asking
questions. She set them on the table.

"I told him about the route," Emily said, pointing at Cole. "She's going to tell
you about the route."

"He went through Prescott," Sharon said, from the chair. Bitboy had migrated to
the window ledge but was still within arm's reach and keeping track. "He does
this every time. He always adds forty minutes to avoid the freeway, and then he
complains about the drive."

"I don't complain," Cole said.

"You say 'long drive' in a tone that means complaining."

"That's just what the drive is."

Emily sat on the edge of the window bench and watched the two of them and drank her coffee with the expression of a person exactly glad to be where she was.

Outside, the pines were going dark, the light already gone behind the western ridge and taking the warmth with it the way it always did this high up. The Sierras did not do gradual evenings. The air was cold at the edges and the cabin would need the woodstove soon and that was fine. That was what the woodstove was for. The coffee was warm.

Cole looked at the jar of blackberry preserves and thought about evenings in this
same cabin a long time ago, the woodstove going and the snow on the ridge and
everyone there at once. He thought about a kitchen table full of books and diagrams and a mother who had been a chemical engineer before she decided she was a mother. He thought about a summer delivering pizzas. Sixteen years old, windows down, the city going by.

He thought: I'm going to be okay.

He had thought it before, in the cargo bay, with Sam's hand on his shoulder and
Heavy's weight against his side and Bitboy crying. He had thought it and meant it. The months between that and this were the proof.

It was true.

He was still carrying things. He would always be carrying things — the crew of
the Polaris, the cabin in the Sierras, the county road in the dark with the
hazards clicking. But the carrying was different now. The warden had put down
the keys. The cell door had been open the whole time.

He was the one doing the carrying.

That, the substance had told him, was a great deal.

He looked at Sharon — the left eye and the augmented eye, the plating where her
shoulder used to be, the arm that was hers and the arm that was something else —
and he felt it. The thing the teenage Cole in the Tercel had foreshadowed.
Love and fury in the same breath. His father fighting with less and less of
himself, loving with all of it anyway, and the thing Cole had called letting go
was just the last of what the man had. Sharon coming back in pieces was the
mirror. He forgave his father in the same moment he accepted Sharon as she was
now — not despite the fury but through it, the way you walk through a door you
didn't know was open.

He did not say any of this aloud.

She was telling Emily something about the gaps — about a
memory that had come back that morning, specific and unexpected, the smell of
the Sequoia pines at six in the morning when the dew was still on them. She was describing it plainly — just the fact, the smell, what it meant that it had come back. Not making it larger than it was.

He thought: I know who you are. I keep losing the where. I'm working on it.

He thought: all we have is time.

Sharon looked over at him and said: "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing."

"The going somewhere else while staying in the room thing."

"I'm right here."

"You're mostly right here." She looked at him with the full precision of both
eyes. "Which is better than before."

"It is better than before," he agreed.

She held out her hand.

He took it.

Outside, the stars were out and the woodstove was going and in the cabin the five of them — Cole, Sharon, Emily, Sommi, Bitboy on the window ledge making sure everyone was accounted for — sat in a shared moment.

---

Sam and Ricardo arrived the next morning.

Ricardo was through the door before Sam had finished parking. He crossed the room to Sharon and put his arms around her — not a careful version of himself, just Ricardo, at full weight — and held on.

Sharon held on too. Both arms.

When they separated Ricardo looked at the augmentation the way a man looks at something he has heard about secondhand and is now confirming is exactly as impressive as reported.

"Yeah," he said. "That's badass."

"I know," Sharon said.

Sam came in behind him with coffee and the expression of a person who had watched Ricardo contain himself for the last two hours of driving and was glad it was over.

---

Later, Ricardo stood at the porch railing with a beer he wasn't drinking and looked at the ridge.

He had been a Federation man down to the bone — that was his whole frame, his people, his sense of what was worth protecting. Everyone outside it got a simple slot. The Colony people: true believers who'd signed the deed to a rock in space. The Collective: earnest, a little much, the kind of people who made their own bread and meant it as a statement. Not enemies. Just not his world. Then Taylor Glenn turned out to be the machine. Alpha Corp built the suppression. Sharon came back in pieces. And the woman he was in love with had been Collective since before she boarded the Golden Dawn.

Sam had said it plainly, sitting across from him the night she explained: "The Collective has been doing what the Federation was supposed to do for eleven years. From inside the thing that captured your institution. We weren't your enemy, Ricardo. We were the reason the enemy didn't win all the way."

He had sat with that for a while.

His institution was captured. The people he had written off as cookie idealists were the ones who had been holding the line.

"The Dude abides, Coley," he said. After a moment: "That's the whole thing. You can't control what the institution turned out to be. You can only control whether you abide." He looked at the beer, drank it, and set the bottle on the railing without sound.

"Yeah." A long pause. "Cole."

"I'm here."

"I'm glad you came home."

"Me too," Cole said.

"I told her," Ricardo said. "About the road. What I saw, that year." A pause. "I'd been sitting on it."

"How'd she take it."

"She cried." He was quiet a moment. "How do you think Sharon's gonna do with the bionic arm?"

Cole thought about Sharon's determination. The specific set of her shoulders when she decided she was going to do something.

"I guess we'll find out," he said. "God help the bowling alley if she can't."

Ricardo laughed. The sound of it filled the valley.
