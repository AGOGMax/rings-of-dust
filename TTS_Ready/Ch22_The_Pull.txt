RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Twenty-Two

The Pull

The twenty-third day.

She had been gone for five of them.

She caught herself counting on the approach back in, the same way she had counted
on the approach out — but out had felt like release and in felt like something
else. Not return. Not obligation. Something she didn't have a name for yet, which
was becoming a pattern with this place.

She had been three hours out when it happened.

Not a voice. She would have said there was no voice, if anyone had asked. It was
the rhythm — the specific cadence of her grandmother's Friday evening recitation,
the way she had said the names of the ancestors before lighting the candles, and
the phrase that came at the end of the names, the one Jill had heard so many
times as a child that it had stopped meaning anything and become wallpaper.
Background sound in a childhood kitchen.

If you truly believe in something and pour yourself into it, it will continue in
your children.

She had been running the post-launch checks, which she did on autopilot, her
hands doing what hands do when the mind is somewhere else. She was somewhere
else. She had been somewhere else since the asteroid had disappeared from the
rear scope.

The phrase arrived the way things arrive when you have heard them five hundred
times and never listened: all at once, and with the specific weight of something
that had been waiting for the right conditions to land.

It will continue in your children.

She didn't have children. She had never thought about children — not seriously,
not in any way that meant something. She had spent her adult life moving around
the edges of the empire her bloodline built, staying lean and unencumbered and
deliberately unreachable, because the one thing she was certain of was that she
did not want to become the thing the Vergluds had become. She had made herself
small to keep herself clean.

But that wasn't the whole thought her grandmother had been saying, was it. The
thought was: if you believe in something and pour yourself into it.

She had just spent twenty-three days watching a community of people pour
themselves into something. She had watched Tyler walk down a tunnel until gravity
let go of him. She had watched Petra sit with those notebooks for decades,
keeping them alive, keeping the record. She had watched the gold crater appear in
the side of an asteroid eleven days after a sacrifice, and felt something in her
chest that she had spent three hours trying to name and had eventually set aside
because she didn't have a category for it.

She also had a routing contract she had just accepted, a 36-hour pickup window,
and a career built on the simple principle that you kept your word with brokers
because the job was not glamorous and consistency was the only thing that made it
viable.

She sat with all of this for a while.

Then she put the ship in a holding pattern, opened the comm panel, and sent a
short message to the routing broker. Forfeiting. Citing equipment concerns. She
would lose the contract and probably the broker. She had lost brokers before.

She turned the ship around.

The asteroid appeared again in the forward scope. Tiny, then growing. The
colony's cargo lights were still on — amber, steady, the same as when she'd left.
Petra had said when you come back with the calm certainty of someone who knew the
difference between someone leaving and someone being done.

Jill had thought she was done.

She hadn't been.

---

Tyler was at the east end of the tunnel when she docked. Not blocking the way —
standing where the gradient started, the place about two hundred meters in where
the asteroid's claim on things began to loosen, one hand at his side and the
other holding a cell in both palms, the way you hold something you are still
deciding what to do with.

He did not look surprised to see her.

She walked up to him. She looked at the cell. He looked at it too.

"The gold," she said. "I can't explain it."

"No," he said. "I know."

"Petra's explanation is physics. Your explanation is something else."

"Both explanations produce the same prediction," he said. "The asteroid arrives
on the eleventh day. It's always gold. The more we send, the more comes back."
He was quiet. "The question of why has two possible answers. The physics answer
says: the void redirects inbound asteroid traffic along a specific vector and
gold-bearing asteroids happen to cluster in that approach corridor. The other
answer says: the void receives what we give it and sends back something of
equivalent value in a different form." He looked toward the dark at the end of
the tunnel. "Elsa spent the last years of her life trying to determine which
answer was true. She died before she decided."

"What do you think?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"I think the physics answer is correct," he said. "I think the gold is exactly
what Elsa's orbital calculations predict. I think there is a mechanism and it is
real and it does not require anything supernatural to function." He paused. "I
also think that mechanism is not an accident. I think the void has the specific
gravitational signature it has because something put it there. I think the cells
interact with it the way they interact with it because the same something built
that interaction into them." He looked at her. "I think there is a difference
between understanding the mechanism and understanding the intention. Physics
explains the mechanism. The other answer explains the intention."

"You don't know that."

"No," he said. "I believe it."

She looked toward the end of the tunnel.

"I came back," she said. Which was not a question and not an explanation.

He nodded once. He had already known that.

He carried the cell the rest of the way into the east tunnel until gravity let go
of it, and then he released it, and then he walked back out with his hands open
at his sides, and he looked at her for a long moment.

"Ask Petra," he said.

---

Petra showed her the second tunnel that night.

It was a sealed passage running deeper into the moon's interior — away from the
east tunnel, away from the docking bay, away from everything Jill had been shown
as a guest. The door had been there the whole time. She had walked past it twice
a day for eleven days and never asked about it because it looked like a utility
access and nobody had given her a reason to ask.

What was inside took her breath for a full minute.

Gold. Decades of gold returns, accumulated and stored, filling chamber after
chamber in the moon's interior. The asteroid the Federation had registered as
commercially worthless and classified as a desolate religious outpost was not
desolate. It had been quietly filling up since Elsa Klein's first Jackpot — the
returns stacking year after year, generation after generation, more wealth than
every fortune Jill had ever heard of combined. The colony had been sitting on it
the whole time, living bare-minimum, dressing in work clothes, eating real food
and calling it enough.

Jill stood in the passage for a long time before she could form a sentence.

"Tyler knows," Petra said, behind her.

"Tyler knows and he lives like this."

"Tyler lives like this because it isn't his. It's the colony's. And the colony
gives it to the void." Petra paused. "What's left over goes to the Collective."

"How much," Jill said.

"I stopped calculating three asteroids ago," Petra said. "Past a certain size a
number stops being information." She looked at the chamber. "Tyler has opinions
about what to do with it. The Void-Walkers have different opinions. The Loyalists
have a third set." She turned. "You'll need to decide."

The sacrifice branch of the family now had more than the accumulation branch had
ever dreamed of. And they had arrived at it by giving everything away.

Petra reached into the case she had been carrying and set four cloth-covered
volumes on the rock shelf at the entrance to the tunnel. Elsa's notebooks. The
originals.

"I told you I had copies," Petra said. "These are yours. Elsa's instructions
were precise — legal documents in the archive room, filed with the independent
registry on Pell Station, authenticated and irrevocable. I am the executor. You
are the heir." She said it the way she said everything — without ceremony,
without needing a response. "Read them tonight. You already know your name is in
there. You know what that means."

Jill looked at the notebooks. She looked at the chamber. She looked at the tunnel
that ran toward the void at the end of the asteroid.

She picked up the notebooks.

She read them through the night — the science first, the 369 test runs, the
mechanics of lock and sync and the eleven-day cycle; then the spiritual records,
the ancestor gifting, the belief that energy moved forward through lines. The
last volume she read twice. The zero condition. Elsa's final handwriting — tighter
than the earlier volumes, as if she'd been writing fast and trying to fit
everything in. The ceiling is gravity. The zero-gravity entries she read a third
time.

By morning she understood what she was carrying.

---

She contacted the Collective through the colony's existing channel that morning.
She told them about the notebooks — the zero-gravity data, Elsa's test runs, the
zero condition. She didn't fully understand how it fit in the larger picture. They
did. The intelligence moved through the informant network to Red and Squirrel,
who were simultaneously receiving Ray's Panama findings. The two pieces converged.
Squirrel told Ray at the heron fountain park that someone was already working on
the same theory. Red told Jill where to find Ray.

---

She left the next morning.

She ran the departure checklist the way she always ran it — methodical, unhurried,
the way you do things when you've been doing them alone for long enough that the
ritual of the list is its own kind of company. She filed the departure notice with
the colony's docking registry. She sealed the transit case with the notebooks in
the cargo locker. She disengaged the clamp.

Tyler was not at the docking bay. She had not expected him to be.

Yun was there. She stood at the bay entrance with her hands in her pockets and
watched Jill run the pre-launch sequence.

"Will you come back," Yun said.

"Yes," Jill said. Which was honest in a way that I don't know never had been.

She launched.

She thought about Tyler Durden, in the way you think about someone you met at a
very strange time in your life — when you were in the middle of something you
didn't yet have a name for, and they named it anyway, and you left before you
could decide what to do about that.
