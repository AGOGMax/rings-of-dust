RINGS OF DUST

Chapter One

The Polaris

Cole Nova checked the seal on his suit, pulled his gloves tight, and stepped into the storage bay of the starship Polaris. The ship felt emptied out. Three hundred and seventy crew confined to their quarters by his order — the call he'd made after the second fight broke out in the mess hall and Hendricks was found holding a fork over the same plate for eleven minutes, eyes fixed on nothing. Whatever DarkX was doing to the crew, it was getting worse. The medic had checked Cole's vitals twice before clearing him. A coffee cup sat abandoned in the corridor outside the bay, still warm, left by someone who hadn't come back for it.

Under his hastily adorned space suit, the inner stitching on his "Jackpot" patch rubbed a valley of irritation into his chest. The patch — a gift from his crew — was not Federation-issued. Unlike the official patches and ribbons he wore at ceremonies, this one Cole treasured. The itch of the rough stitch filled him with pride that was beginning to twist into fear.

He crept along the rows, verifying each of the fourteen harvesters was operational. Luminescent bands traced the contours of each slender monolith, softly pulsing in unison. Green is good. This was the last task of the mission before returning home — obtaining a sample of DarkX. Only Gracie and him on this one. He wouldn't ask anyone else to walk in here.

As he moved deeper, the green glow gave way to shadows. The rhythmic hum surrounding him grew fainter. His steps slowed. The air grew colder, and a faint thought of his sister tickled his spine. What on earth, literally, was she doing right now? His pulse brought his attention back to the target — a gentle, yet ominous, red glow.

Much like crab pots in Earth's ocean, harvesters were dropped into Saturn's rings to capture gas, dust, and ice. During these runs, crews had encountered something new. DarkX was not a liquid or a gas. It was more of a plasma — obsidian, shifting, forming spikes and tendrils like it had opinions about being observed. The First Spark logs from a generation earlier had carried the same language: every crew, different words, same conclusion — it knew they were there.

Cole had pushed back when the assignment came through comms. Why do this aboard a crewed vessel? The Rapture window doesn't close for another five years. There's time to build a proper isolation chamber on one of Alpha's unmanned stations, time to study this thing without putting three hundred and seventy people between it and the vacuum. The Federation agreed to send two escort ships — the Falcon and the Horus — but Cole knew both were headed for the wreck yard on skeleton crews. He agreed when he saw the crew manifests. He had spent over fifteen years with most of them and knew they were damn good Astros.

The red harvester intensified its glow as he drew nearer. The substance inside changed shape — tendrils stretching toward the edge of the container, then pulling back, then reaching again. DarkX did not want to be separated. It did not want to be handled. It had secrets it was unwilling to share.

Cole opened the harvesting container, a narrow glass collection tube in hand. He watched light bend and fracture across the plasma's surface.

"Hey, Gracie," he said. "You seeing this?"

"Seeing it, logging it, and quietly losing my mind over it," Gracie said. "There's no match in any database I can reach. And Cole — the crew's stress levels are through the roof. Every single deck."

"Yeah. It's pretty wild in here. I think suiting up was a good idea after all." He brought the tube closer. "Let's see what this space goo is made of."

"Be careful. In its raw state, this stuff is extraordinarily volatile. One wrong nudge and we're not having this conversation anymore."

Cole led the collection tube toward the plasma. The hum of engines and the soft whir of air systems drew silent, as if the ship itself was holding its breath. A wave of memories hit him — his entire past distilled into this moment, consumed all at once. Using every ounce of focus he fought the intoxicating pull and stayed on the task. The plasma inspected the inside of the tube, curled against the glass, then recoiled. Cole scraped it with the rubber-lined lid and felt the euphoric rush break, as though he had offended it by separating it. He sealed the harvester, still throbbing with red light.

"Cole. Come back to the corridor. Please."

He had never heard her say please.

He secured the tube to the hazardous materials compartment on the waist of his suit, tugged it tight, and turned for the doors. He walked. The bay felt like it was stalking him.

"Cole, I'm detecting an intermittent yet increasing energy surge from the harvester you sampled."

"Shit. Hail the Falcon and Horus. Alert the crew — more emergency precautions." His voice tightened. "And keep trying the hooligans."

Gracie's broadcast cut through every speaker on the ship: "Attention all crew: Emergency protocols 11, 22, and 33 are now in effect. Please proceed to your designated emergency—"

The signal cut. Halfway through the word, she was gone. The bay doors blew.

A brilliant incandescent light erupted from the sampled harvester, engulfing the bay in a tidal wave of radiance. Time slowed. The shockwave tore through the last remaining atmosphere within the crumbling ship — a cacophony of force that fractured the corridor into a vortex of light and sound. Cole, caught in the ferocious heat and pressure, was flung toward the sealed bay doors.

On the Falcon, Ricardo sat frozen as he watched the Polaris transform into a wasteland devoured by a sudden, blinding flash. The Falcon rocked violently as the dead and debris impacted the outer hull. Heavy grabbed the nearest support beam. Bitboy's limbs anchored him to the floor.

"What the hell is this?" Heavy yelled over what sounded like an oversized hailstorm.

Bitboy was already at the controls, plotting a course. The Falcon's engines roared as they veered toward the Polaris, dodging debris.

"Get the rescue gear ready," Heavy said. "We need to find survivors."

The ship's skeleton warped and fractured under the strain. Metal alloys engineered to endure the harshness of space folded and twisted like paper caught in a gale. Fuel cells ruptured, blooming into fiery orbs that consumed everything in their path. Cole was jettisoned from the ship. He floated untethered in the violent disarray, a lone figure amid the chaos. His ship — his crew — torn apart and fed to the void. Flames and metal shards danced a silent ballet around him. A lattice of cracks formed across his helmet's visor, and the faint whistle of his oxygen supply escaping was the only sound left in the universe.

"All crew report," he said into the void. Nothing. "Triage team report." The words dissipated into the vacuum.

"Gracie. Anyone."

Silence.

He activated the recorder on his left wrist.

"Mission Log — Final Entry, 2028. This is Captain Cole Nova. Our ship has sustained critical damage following an unexpected explosion in the storage bay. I've managed to secure a sample of the anomaly. It should be on my body. The crew executed emergency protocols and attempted to stabilize the situation to the best of their abilities. It appears I'm the only survivor."

He paused.

"Ricardo 'Heavy Hitter' Corazon was more than our second officer. He was one of the best Astros I've had the honor of serving with. His humor and strength held a good crew together, even in the darkest hours. Bitboy — whose contributions were as invaluable as they were unusual. An agility and problem-solving ability that surpassed all expectations. His presence was a constant reminder of the extraordinary lengths this crew went through to explore the unknown."

His voice softened. "Emily. The guiding light in my life. Distance and time mean nothing against the bond we share. Look to the stars — I'll always watch over you. Keep our dreams alive. I love you."

He sent the message into the darkness.

Against the backdrop of the starship's fractured body, a sliver of movement broke the stillness — one object that defied the slow twisting of the debris. A shuttle, its form barely discernible, arching and weaving with purpose through the floating carnage. Inside, Ricardo worked alongside Bitboy — a chimpanzee operating six biomechanical limbs in a symphony of coordinated motion. Under their control, the shuttle danced through the wreckage and the drifting bodies of the crew. As they closed the distance, the debris thinned, revealing the drifting figure of their comrade.

No tether. No rescue. No hope. Cole's oxygen whistled through the cracks in his faceplate. Then the shuttle's side hatch swung open. Gloved hands stretched into the emptiness.

Contact. A rescuer's glove brushed against Cole's suit — unsure at first, fingers sliding across the slick fabric. Another hand joined, substantial and confident. They drew him in.

Air, rich and sweet, flooded his lungs. Ricardo's and Bitboy's faces, seen through a blur of tears, shone with relief and warmth. Behind them in the cargo compartment were stacked and strapped-down harvesters of different substances bound for Alpha's space station.

"Are any of these filled with DarkX?" he demanded.

"Most of them are top priority on the manifest," Ricardo said.

"It's not safe!" He tried to rip the sample tube from his pocket like a stick of lit dynamite.

"What are you talking about? The ore and sludge we're packing are sealed tight, even wearing a seatbelt." Heavy gestured toward the tie-down straps.

"I took this sample from a harvester right before it destroyed our entire ship."

"Why the hell would you open a harvester and play with that creepy pudding?"

"I was following orders."

"Cole, I know this isn't the time, but sometimes you gotta play dumb and pretend your radio is broken."

Cole did not say anything else for a long time. He stood at the shuttle's viewport and watched the remnants of the Polaris twist and turn amid the cosmic whirl of debris orbiting Saturn. Three hundred and sixty-nine people. The wreckage that used to be their ship was already becoming part of the rings — indistinguishable, in a few orbits, from everything else that Saturn had ever swallowed.

His powder-blue eyes stared back at him from the viewport. They were older than they had this morning.
