EPILOGUE II

The town was called Eureka. It did not appear on any map he had been given, which was the point. Population four hundred and something. Situated at the end of a two-lane road that gave out at the foot of a range of hills nobody had named anything useful.

He had been given the name Dale Custer, which was not a name he would have chosen for himself.

The bus had dropped him at a filling station and the filling station attendant had looked at him the way a person looks at something that has arrived already wrong. Taylor had not slept on the bus. He had the pocket watch in the inside breast pocket of his jacket, where he always kept it, and he had held it through the jacket fabric for most of the drive the way you hold a thing when there is nothing else to hold.

He had known, walking into Eureka from the filling station, that it would not take long. A town this size knew what it had lost and knew what a new face meant. The hospital had been a single building on the northeast edge of town, and it was a single building on the northeast edge of town now — which was to say it was a shell. The sign was still there. The sign would probably outlast the town. Eleven people had died when the supply chain collapsed — not all at once, drawn out over two years, the kind of dying that a small town does not recover from. He knew the number. He had been briefed on the region as a logistics parameter, years ago, in a room full of men who called it acceptable attrition in the transition corridor. He remembered the phrase because it was precise and he had always respected precision.

He did not have long. He could feel the geometry of the town organizing itself around his presence.

A man came to him before the others. He was not young. He wore a denim jacket with nothing on it. He stopped at a distance that was not quite conversational.

"The watch," the man said.

Taylor did not speak.

"He wants it back."

Taylor reached into his jacket. The brass was warm from being against his chest for the better part of three days. He had carried it for longer than he had carried anything else in his life. He looked at it for a moment — the case, the weight of it, the engraving he had never had explained to him — and he understood, in the act of handing it over, the full shape of who had sent him here and why. Not Marcus. Never Marcus. Marcus was the noise.

The man took the watch without looking at it and walked back the way he had come.

The town closed in from the edges, the way towns do.

Taylor stood in the road and did not move. He had been precise his entire career. He had not been wrong about that. He had simply been precise in service of something that had always been larger than he was, and he had never been told the shape of it, and now he was standing in the center of it with nothing left in his jacket pocket.

The machine disciplines its own errors.
