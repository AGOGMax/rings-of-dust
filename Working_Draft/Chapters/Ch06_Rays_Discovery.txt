RINGS OF DUST

Chapter Six

Ray's Discovery

It had started as laziness.

The protocol was clear: submit for containment within twenty-four hours of
crystallization. That window had closed ten days ago.

Max had converted the batch on a Tuesday — standard process, salt compounds and
liquid nitrogen in a negative-pressure chamber until the DarkX plasma locked
into solid form. Beautiful crystals. Dense, dark, faintly luminous at the edges.
The kind of thing that took three hours to make and thirty seconds to ruin. The
kind of thing a junior researcher had no business holding onto without
authorization.

The official containment unit was across campus. Getting there meant logging the
transfer, signing through two security checkpoints, and explaining why a junior
researcher had an unauthorized batch of Darker Matter crystals at all. Max
looked at the crystals. He looked at the clock. He looked at the spare fuel cell
sitting on the shelf behind him.

He packed the crystals inside it, sealed the cell under negative pressure,
topped it off with nitrogen to keep things stable, and shoved it in his locker.
*I'll deal with it tomorrow.*

Tomorrow became the next day. The next day became a week. A week became ten
days, the mandatory turnover window long gone, the crystals now approaching
reversion on their own schedule whether he was ready or not. Each morning he
stood at the locker for a full minute before he could make himself open it.

Max slumped over his workstation, eyes strained from hours of staring at data
screens. The fluorescent lights above cast a sterile, cold glare on the polished
floor. His mind was a storm of thoughts, still reeling from what he'd seen. The
fuel cell—his impulsive shortcut—hadn't just held; it had transformed.

Sitting hadn't worked. Relaxation is a distant bitch. A double-edged sword. His
feet wanted to run in any direction other than the one he forced his mutinous
legs to continue. He rounded the corner, his pulse quickening. The locker loomed
ahead, a secret buried beneath anxiety and cautious hope. His fingers fumbled
with the lock, the combination momentarily escaping him as nerves tangled his
thoughts. When the door finally swung open, his breath caught in his throat.

The fuel cell was glowing. A soft, eerie light pulsed from the panel, a
heartbeat in the silence. He lifted it carefully, hands trembling as he peered
through the viewport. The vapor inside was dense, swirling lazily like a storm
caught in a jar.

"No way," he muttered, almost dropping the cell in shock. The crystals he'd
sealed there were gone, replaced by something entirely different. His mind
raced, a dozen explanations swirling like the mist in the cell, none making
sense. He should have reported it immediately, but the fear of repercussions—of
losing everything—held him back.

Instead, he tucked the cell back into the locker, slammed the door, and forced
himself to breathe. He needed time, needed to think. The lab was a maze of risks
and rewards, and Max knew better than most how quickly those scales could tip.

The following weeks were a blur of late nights and whispered conversations, the
fuel cell hidden away like contraband. Max monitored it obsessively, his heart
pounding every time the lab door creaked open. Each day, the cell's charge grew,
the vapor inside thickening, alive with a strange, magnetic energy. He was on
the edge of something huge that could change everything.

But when the 11th day came, and the mist cleared, revealing a cluster of
delicate crystals clinging to the inside of the cell's base, one word escaped
him before he could stop it.

"Jackpot."

He said it the way a man says something he didn't know he was going to
say—quietly, to no one, to the empty lab, to the crystals themselves. Then the
dread moved in behind the word and filled all the space the triumph had just
vacated. He knew what this meant. This wasn't just a fluke; it was a
breakthrough—a dangerous one. He scribbled notes furiously, documenting every
detail, his thoughts a chaotic jumble of triumph and fear.

Then came the decision he couldn't put off any longer. He had to tell someone,
had to share what he'd found. He chose his department head, hoping that his
discovery's potential would outweigh the protocol breach.

It didn't.

Security was on him within minutes, their hands rough as they stripped him of
his badge and lab coat. Max barely registered the words—the accusations, the
threats. He was too stunned, his mind still fixated on the image of those
crystals, their potential.

"You'll keep quiet about this, or we'll make sure you never work again.
Anywhere," one of the guards snapped, his voice a low growl. Max blinked, the
weight of the threat slowly sinking in. They weren't just talking about his
career. This was bigger, more dangerous than he'd realized.

Outside, the night air was sharp against his skin, the chill cutting through the
shock. He stood in the parking lot, staring at the looming silhouette of the
research complex. It felt like he'd been expelled from some exclusive club, and
the doors slammed shut behind him.

At that moment, he knew that Alpha would bury his discovery and lock it
away behind layers of corporate secrecy. But he couldn't forget what he'd seen.
Couldn't let it go.

Back inside, Alpha's upper echelons were already dissecting his research.
The data was clear: the combination of nitrogen, negative pressure, and the fuel
cell circuits had triggered a reaction that turned Dark Matter crystals back
into its vapor state, generating massive energy. They added liquid nitrogen,
accelerating the formation of the crystals and cutting the cycle down to 11
days.

The process was erratic. Some cells bloomed with crystallites, others failed
spectacularly. Researchers started betting on which cells would produce, a
twisted game of chance in a world where control was everything. The "gardens,"
as they called the fuel cells, became a grim parody of hope—a hope that could
only thrive in secret, behind locked doors.

Every 11 days, they harvested what they could, the crystals growing more
reliable but still volatile. The cells themselves deteriorated under the
constant strain, leaking energy and sometimes exploding. Any remnants were
transferred to fresh cells, and the losses""tailings," they called them—were
painfully significant.

They knew what they had, though. The data was buried deep, accessible only to a
select few. Stock prices soared on speculation and rumor, the true nature of
their discovery hidden beneath layers of corporate bureaucracy.

"  "- "

The crisis had begun not with a failure of technology but with a coordinated financial assault. The global banking system had moved against cryptocurrency — specifically against the miners and nodes that had been building a decentralized energy grid outside institutional control. Regulatory pressure. Liquidity freezes. Infrastructure seizures. Power plant shutdowns and power grid defunding that official channels called decommissioning. The grid had collapsed along fault lines that had been prepared in advance. What the Federation called an energy shortage, the people who had built the alternative grid called an execution.

Meanwhile, at Federation Headquarters, the crisis deepened. Chancellor DeMint
stared at the holographic display, the red zones creeping across the globe like
a spreading disease. The energy crisis was a noose tightening around their
necks, and the room buzzed with the grim knowledge that time was running out.

"We're looking at six months, maybe less," Dr. Salazar said, her voice cutting
through the tension. "Without the new fuel cells, we're facing catastrophic
blackouts."

DeMint's jaw clenched. "What about production? Can we ramp it up?"

"Not without more DarkX," Taylor interjected, his tone clipped. "The
stabilization period is still 90 days, and our reserves are almost gone. We're
scraping the bottom of the barrel."

Sam, a data technician who had been silent until now, leaned forward. "We've
been experimenting with the accumulations on the cells. We can extend the
stability for a few more days, but it's not enough. We need their data."

Taylor turned toward her for the first time. "Samantha." He said it the way
you say a word that isn't a greeting — flat, first name, in a room where
everyone used rank or last name. A small and specific violation. Her jaw
tightened once and then didn't again. She held his eyes and said nothing,
which was its own answer. He turned back to the room. "Extending accumulation
cycles based on experimental intuition isn't a strategy. It's a feeling. And
feelings don't hold up at a briefing table." A half-beat pause. "Or in the
field."

Sam's eyes stayed on her screen. "Might want to patch that," she said. "Your
firmware's running a few versions behind." She pulled up a new readout and
turned it toward Dr. Salazar without looking at Taylor once. "The numbers are
right here."

Dr. Salazar's expression didn't change, her gaze steady and unyielding. "Alpha
Corp has already shared everything they can. The proprietary information is
classified."

Sam's hands curled into fists. "We're all fighting the same battle. The least
they could do is give us a chance."

Taylor's voice was low, almost apologetic. "It's not that simple."

"Isn't it?" Sam snapped. "They hold all the cards, and we're just supposed to
wait until they decide to deal?"

The room fell silent, the weight of the unspoken truth settling over them. Alpha
Corp controlled the future, and without them, the Federation ran out of options.

--- --- ---

Back in his apartment, Max sat at his kitchen table, staring at the stolen fuel
cell. The crystals inside glowed faintly, a promise of power just out of reach.
He knew what he had to do. He couldn't let this go or let Alpha bury his
work.

He would go underground and find a way to continue his research outside their
control. He knew the risks, knew he was playing with fire, but he also knew that
if he could crack this, he could change everything.

Max took a deep breath, the decision settling like a stone in his gut. There was
no turning back now. He had to see this through and find a way to get this
information into the right hands.

MERIDIAN V — EMILY IN THE CAR

Three days after the farm road.

Emily's signal moving east had a particular quality in the trace
— a slight Doppler-adjacent skew Kate had learned to feel for
the way you feel for a key in your pocket. Emily was driving
fast.

"She's moving," Kate said.

Dayo was eating an uninspired sandwich and did not look up.
"East?"

"East. Indio fuel stop, continuing. The field authorization shadow
confirmed she's aware of it — she identified Sommi herself."

Petyr looked up. "She identified a surveillance tail herself?
She's not trained."

"She pulled over. Asked him directly."

A beat.

"That's actually the correct move," Petyr said slowly.

"Yes."

"Most people don't—"

"No," Kate said. "They don't."

Loa was writing something on her forearm in marker. This was
a habit she had when she was processing something. The marker
smelled like cheap solvent — the faint, chemical bite of it
carrying across the station. Kate had started to associate that
smell with the moments before Loa said something important.
She had not mentioned this to Loa.

"Are we pulling for Emily Nova?" Petyr said. He meant it as a
genuine question, which told Kate he still hadn't quite learned
to distinguish between the operations the Collective ran and the
people at the center of them.

"Sharon Teez's last confirmed communication was to Emily Nova's
relay," Dayo said. He'd finished the sandwich. He stared at the
wrapper. "She told us Emily was better than the situation required
of her. Sharon had been in the field for four years. She'd run
with people we trusted completely." He folded the wrapper. "If
that was Sharon's assessment, it's our assessment."

"We're pulling for Emily Nova," Kate confirmed.

Petyr nodded. He wrote something small on the edge of his nav
panel — the same private script Loa used on her forearm. Kate
didn't read it. Some things were for the person writing them.

---

Max worked at Alpha's materials research division for three years before he understood what they were actually working on.

The official designation was Dark Matter Stabilization. The actual designation, which nobody used in writing, was: they had something they couldn't explain and were trying to make it useful before someone else did.

The something was Dark X. It arrived at the facility in sealed magnetic containers, two kilograms at a time, from a processing site that wasn't on any map Max had been shown. It was black-gray, granular, slightly iridescent in direct light. It behaved like no material he'd worked with — it responded to magnetic field variations the way iron shavings respond to a magnet, except the responses were unpredictable, and the field gradients required were precise to a degree that made the testing equipment protest.

Alpha wanted it stable.

Dark X in its raw form held together for eleven days. After that it degraded — not catastrophically, just gradually, losing its structure, reverting to something that tested as a complex carbon compound with anomalous isotopic ratios. Eleven days was the window. Alpha had been trying for four years to extend it.

Max's job was the stabilization matrix. He ran the salts, the additives, the magnetic curing protocols. He ran them carefully, methodically, the way he'd been trained. He documented everything. He ate lunch at his desk. He was, as his supervisor noted in his last review, thorough.

He was also, by the time the Polaris disaster cleared the news cycle, bored.

The Polaris had not made the crisis better. Three hundred and sixty-nine people and one research vessel — the only large-scale non-corporate DarkX extraction attempt in Federation history. What remained was a narrative: that DarkX was volatile, that its handling required institutional oversight, that Alpha Corp's measured approach was the only responsible path forward. The narrative had served the people who needed it to.

His colleagues called him Ray. It had started as a joke about his last name and had never quite stopped being one.

---

He took a sample home on a Tuesday. This was not authorized. It was also not, in his assessment, particularly dangerous — the container was sealed, the quantity was small (thirty grams), and he had a specific idea he wanted to test that required more time than the lab schedule allowed. He put the container in his bag between his lunch box and his headphones, walked through security, and went home.

His apartment ran on a residential fuel cell array. Most did. The cells were stacked in a utility closet off the kitchen, four of them, standard civilian grade, the kind that got replaced every three years whether they needed it or not.

He set the container on the kitchen table. He made coffee. He opened his laptop.

The container was labeled with a red stripe and a lot of numbers. He'd labeled it himself that morning.

He left it on the table overnight.

In the morning, he felt it before he could name it.

Not sound. Not heat, exactly — something closer to the sensation of a radio scanning through static, searching for a frequency just out of range. A low resonance below the threshold of hearing, the feeling of a node reaching toward a network that wasn't quite in phase yet. He had been asleep. It woke him. He lay still for a moment, identifying it the way you identify something you've never felt before but somehow recognize, then got up.

The container on the table was warm. He picked it up — warmer than the room, warmer than it should have been. But the warmth wasn't the signal.

He opened the utility closet.

The fuel cell array's status panel — which he had never looked at in three years of living there because it never changed — was reading a charge level he'd never seen on a residential unit. Not a malfunction. Not an error. A charge level. The kind that residential fuel cells did not achieve.

He stood in the kitchen and listened to the resonance, which was still there — the Dark X in the container and the fuel cells in the closet doing something together at a frequency he could feel in his sternum and couldn't hear with his ears.

Eleven days later, the feeling stopped.

The panel read steady. The cells were quiet. Something had completed. He understood this later — the eleven-day sync period, the time a newly assembled fuel cell required to align its internal architecture to the Dark X frequency before it could output power. He had felt the process happen in a kitchen in the middle of the night, without instruments, without authorization, without any preparation except having been bored enough to take something home.

He got a notepad and started writing down what he was going to need.

---

He worked for thirty-three days.

He didn't call it that at the time — thirty-three days — but that was what it was. He ordered equipment through his personal accounts in small enough quantities that none of the invoices looked like a materials research project. He locked the fuel cells together. He documented the result. He ran it again. He verified the charge levels. He charted the crystal growth on the inside of the cell casings — thin at first, then accelerating, unmistakably the same isotopic signature as the Dark X he'd started with, and growing at a rate ten times faster than any unlocked cell produced.

The fuel cell was producing new dark matter. Not outside — inside. The crystals were growing in the locked space between the cells, accumulating in the dark interior of the casing at a rate that had no precedent in any stabilization model he'd ever run.

Not a lot. Not quickly. But ten times faster than it should have been, at a rate that, if it held, would compound.

He stared at the chart for a long time.

Then he went into the lab the next morning and told his supervisor he needed time on the main testbed.

---

He walked them through it in the order he'd discovered it. The eleven-day sync. The thirty-three-day lock — the point where the internal crystal growth jumped to ten times the unlocked rate and held there. The charge output. The darker matter accumulating inside the locked casings. He had photographs. He had numbers. He showed them the numbers in the clear, careful way he'd always showed them things — the same way he'd presented three years of stabilization data — and watched their faces.

His supervisor's face did something that was not surprise.

The man next to his supervisor — introduced as a representative from Alpha's executive division, whose name Ray did not catch — had no expression at all, which was its own kind of expression.

"Where did you run the initial test?" his supervisor asked.

Ray told him.

The room went quiet in a specific way.

He was fired that afternoon. Not for taking the sample — the sample was not mentioned. He was fired for, as the paperwork stated, a failure to adhere to research protocols and the unauthorized dissemination of proprietary findings. He was told, in language that was careful without being explicit, that the research he had conducted was the property of Alpha Corporation and that any further discussion of his findings — internally, externally, in any medium — would be treated as a breach of his existing non-disclosure agreement and prosecuted accordingly.

He was escorted to his desk and given a box.

He walked home.

He was still carrying the box when Red and Squirrel knocked on his door.

MERIDIAN VIII — PANAMA AND HOUSTON, SAME WEEK

On the day Ray Vision walked out of Alpha's Panama tower with a
drive in his coat and six blocks between him and the park, Kate
was running a signal sweep and eating a very good blood orange
someone had included in the last supply rotation, and she was
genuinely happy for about four minutes.

The blood orange was from the supply rotation. Someone on the
logistics side had started including small things — blood oranges
once, a very small jar of decent coffee, a bar of chocolate that
arrived broken into four pieces and was rationed accordingly.
Kate did not know who made these decisions. She had never asked.

This was unusual enough that she mentioned it to Loa.

"Ray Vision just walked out," she said.

"Already?"

"Drive in hand. Nitrogen alarm gave him the window. Six blocks."

Loa closed her eyes briefly. "He's good."

"He's good." Kate bit into the orange. "Squirrel has the drive."

"What's on it."

"Flywheel analysis, the full Protocol Eleven archive, the grant
fraud records going back seven years." A pause. "Also he named
it the Antigravity project."

"That's a good name," Loa said.

"It's a great name."

Petyr, who had been listening: "What does the flywheel mean
for—"

"It means," Kate said, "that the shortage ends. Not eventually.
As soon as the math gets out. The compound works in your favor
if you have enough of it. Alpha knew this. They sat on it for
nine months." She finished the orange. "The energy crisis has
been an inventory decision."

Petyr was quiet. His hand went to his droplet.

"Is that why—" he started, then stopped.

"Probably," Kate said. She didn't know his specific reason for
being here. She had never asked. That was how the network worked
— you didn't arrive at someone else's reason, you arrived at your
own, and then discovered you were in a room with other people
who had done the same. "Probably your reason and the flywheel
are connected somewhere upstream."

"Same week," Loa said quietly.

Kate looked up.

"Ray is out. Emily is in Houston. Same week." She gestured at
her panel, where Houston's Space Command facility was logged
as a monitoring point. "The network moves all at once or it
doesn't move."

"It's moving," Kate said.

On the wall: the droplet. Gunmetal on gray. Sharon's.

---

Ray ran his dry fingertips along the rough edges of his weathered oak table,
feeling the familiar grit beneath his skin. The surface, scarred from years of
restless nights and unanswered questions, was as uneven as his thoughts. A
single light hung overhead, casting sharp, unforgiving shadows that made the
room feel even smaller than it was. The faint hum of the lamp above him was the
only sound — its flicker a reminder that nothing around him was as stable as it
seemed.

In front of him, the stolen fuel cell gleamed dully under the light's hesitant
glow. It was untouched since the day he'd left Alpha — abandoned, like
everything else. Ray stared at it, half-expecting it to give him answers, though
he knew better by now. The thought of selling it tugged at him, a practical
solution for the bills piling up on the kitchen counter. The black market would
pay a good price. But the longer he looked at it, the more something twisted in
his gut — a strange cocktail of anger and, worse, hope.

He hadn't realized how small his apartment had become until the two people
stepped inside.

Squirrel crossed the threshold first, his heavy boots thudding against the worn
hardwood floor. Red followed, her gaze sweeping across the space with a quiet
intensity that made Ray's skin prickle. They were not the kind of people who
fidgeted. They moved through the room and stopped and waited, and the quality of
their waiting was very different from the waiting of civilians.

They didn't sit. They didn't move toward the couch half-covered in old blankets.
Instead, they positioned themselves near the table — not threatening, exactly,
but settled in the way of people who intend to conclude this conversation before
leaving.

Squirrel didn't waste words. "We have a window of about seventy-two hours before
Alpha sends someone who isn't going to knock. You're already on a list, Ray.
You've been on it since the night you told your department head."

Ray looked at them. "You people have been watching me."

"We've been keeping you alive," Red said. "There's a difference."

"Not from where I'm sitting."

"I understand that." She pulled out a chair and sat down across from him —
unhurried, like she was prepared to be here a while. "What you found in that
locker didn't stop being important because they took it from you. Alpha knows
the discovery exists. They know you made it. And they have built a significant
amount of their current business model on the fact that the rest of the world
does not know what you know."

Ray's hand moved toward the blanket on the table, then stopped. He looked at
Squirrel. "What do you actually want from me?"

"We want you alive and working," Squirrel said. "We have a place for you to
hide and a new identity to protect you."

"And in exchange?"

"You keep doing what you were doing. The research doesn't stop — it just stops
belonging to people who bury it."

Ray was quiet. Outside the window, a car passed and did not slow down and its
headlights swept the ceiling of his apartment. He had been watching headlights
for three weeks, logging which ones slowed down.

"You understand what the cell actually did," Red said. It was not a question.
"Alpha thinks they understand it. They're harvesting the output — the crystals,
the yield, the energy the conversion cycle produces. They built their whole
operation around the output." She leaned forward. "They never figured out what
they're actually dealing with. They only ever saw it as a container."

"They saw what they expected to see," Ray said, in spite of himself.

"Yes."

He looked at the cell under the blanket. "I packed the crystals in there because
it was convenient. The negative pressure, the nitrogen atmosphere — I thought it
was just a stable environment. I wasn't trying to trigger a reaction. I was
trying to avoid paperwork."

"And what you got," Squirrel said, "was a reaction that their entire R&D division
has been unable to reproduce reliably in two years of trying. Because they
started from the output side. They're backward-engineering a process they don't
have the right mental model for."

He paused. "Alpha's standard containment runs nitrogen. Open cycle — nitrogen atmosphere, no seal modification. Every lab in the program. Every researcher who ever handled Darker Matter crystals ran the same protocol." He looked at Red. "Nobody sealed a cell under negative pressure. It wasn't in any protocol. You added it as a shortcut because you were trying to avoid a walk across campus. That single variable — the seal — is why they have been failing for two years trying to reproduce what you found in a locker on a Tuesday."

Ray pulled the blanket away from the fuel cell. He hadn't meant to do it — his
hands made the decision before his head caught up. The cell sat on the table,
dull and inert in the lamplight.

"I know how the cells work at eleven days," he said. "That's the window Alpha
operates on. They found the yield threshold and they've been farming it ever
since — harvest, reset, repeat." He reached into the drawer and pulled out a
battered notebook. Several pages were covered in diagrams, heat maps, rough
models of accumulation behavior. "What I don't know is what happens if you
don't reset."

Red looked at the notebook. "You think longer cycles change the math."

"I think they change everything." Ray's voice had changed. He was not frightened
anymore. He was precise, and precision was, for him, a kind of territory. "I've
run the calculations a dozen times. I keep getting results that don't make sense
under any model I can test here. What I need—" he stopped. He had spent three
weeks trying to say this clearly and still couldn't quite get there. "I need
proper instruments. A controlled environment. I need to test this under
conditions I cannot replicate in an apartment."

He tapped the notebook. "Alpha built their entire operation on eleven days
because that's where they found the yield window. They never asked what would
happen at thirty days. Or sixty. Or longer." He looked at the fuel cell. "They
saw a harvest. I think they missed the actual discovery."

Squirrel looked at him. "What are the odds." A statement, not a question. "On any given eleven-day cycle. What does your math say."

Ray looked at the notebook. "I know what the cell does in isolation. Negative pressure, vapor vortex, eleven-day cycle, charges itself. That's what I showed Alpha and that's what got me fired." He looked at the fuel cell under the blanket. "What I don't know is what happens when you scale it. What happens when you run hundreds of cells together. What happens at thirty-three days, or sixty, or longer." He paused. "I think Alpha knows. I think they've been running those numbers for years and burying the results. But I can't prove it from out here."

Squirrel looked at Red. Something passed between them — not surprise. Recognition. The kind of look two people exchange when the person across the table has just described exactly the gap they've been trying to fill.

"That's why we're sending you inside," Squirrel said.

Red pulled a card from her pocket and set it on the table — plain, no logo, a
handwritten address. "Tonight. Flight out. New name. New country."

"Tonight."

"The window closes tomorrow morning."

Ray picked up the card. He set it back down. He picked it up again and held it.

He looked at the fuel cell for the last time in his apartment. He put his
notebook on top of it, carefully, like he was tucking something in.

"Seventy-two hours," he said.

"Less, now."

He stood up. He went to his closet and pulled out a bag from the top shelf and
stood holding it for a moment. Then he started packing. He was not fast about it
and he was not slow. He was precise. He took the notebook. He took the fuel
cell, wrapped in the blanket. He left the loan statements on the counter.

At the door, he turned back and looked at the apartment once — the lamp still
burning, the oak table still scarred, the lamp's flicker still going. Then he
followed them out into the dark.

---

The flight was three hours and forty minutes. Ray slept through one of them, not by choice but because his body had been making decisions without him since the apartment.

He woke to descent. The seatbelt light. The PA announcing something in Spanish, then English, then Spanish again, as if the English had been a courtesy they'd already moved on from.

He did not have luggage. He had a backpack Red had handed him in the car — already packed, his name not on it, things inside he did not remember owning. A change of clothes. A toothbrush, still in its packaging. A paperback in a language he couldn't read. He hadn't opened it.

He came through arrivals into the main hall of Tocumen International Airport and stood still long enough that the people behind him had to move around him, which they did without comment, having seen that before.

The heat hit him outside. Not gradual — immediate, physical, the air swapping itself for something thicker. It was nine in the morning and already hotter than he'd lived in. He was wearing a jacket.

He took the jacket off.

He stood with the jacket over his arm and looked at the street in front of the terminal. Taxis, vans, a man with a handcart full of something green, someone's grandmother in a lawn chair conducting business of some kind. At the end of the pickup zone, a line of pedicabs: three-wheeled, fringed awnings, drivers who had the particular stillness of people who know how to wait and have been doing it.

He had an address on a piece of paper. Red had written it in block letters: CALLE 50, CERCA DE LA CRESTA. Below it: ASK FOR LUCIA. Below that: DON'T TAKE A CAB.

A pedicab driver made eye contact.

Ray held the eye contact too long, which was apparently sufficient.

"You go?" the driver said.

Ray showed him the paper.

The driver glanced at it, nodded once, gestured at the seat with the efficiency of someone who had done this thousands of times and found no reason to expand it. Ray sat. The driver mounted the front and started pedaling.

They entered traffic.

Traffic in Panama City operated on principles Ray did not have access to. There were lanes, but the lanes were advisory. Signals existed but were in negotiation with everything else. There was a sound system — horn, counter-horn, acknowledgment-horn, horn of general principle — that carried specific meaning to everyone who spoke it. He did not speak it.

The driver did not seem concerned. He cut between a bus and a delivery truck at a gap Ray would not have called a gap, banked hard left on a street that appeared to be going right, threaded a roundabout by logic Ray couldn't identify, and came out onto a boulevard lined with glass towers that threw the morning back at everything beneath them.

The city was big. This was the thing he hadn't expected — that it would be this operational, this indifferent to his arrival. He had not thought about Panama as a place. It had been a word on a piece of paper for two days. Now it was a city going about its morning in heat he hadn't lived in and languages he didn't speak and at a pace the pedicab was matching without effort.

A bus pulled alongside and blew its horn for reasons that weren't apparent.

He grabbed the side rail.

The driver looked back. "It's okay," he said. "Bus always does."

They turned off the boulevard into streets that were narrower and lower, the buildings closer together, the sound differently textured — generators, music through open windows, a saw running somewhere up a side street. A woman leaned from a second-floor window and called something down to someone Ray couldn't see. A dog crossed the road with the authority of something that had done it many times and trusted cars to have figured it out by now.

The pedicab slowed on a block of storefronts — a pharmacy, a tienda, something selling fabric, something selling something he couldn't identify — and stopped in front of a door painted green. Not recently green. The green of something that had been green long enough to mean it.

"Calle Cincuenta," the driver said. "Near La Cresta. This one."

Ray got out. He gave the driver the money Red had put in his jacket pocket — he didn't know how much, he just gave him all of it. The driver looked at it, gave some back, and pedaled away.

Ray stood in front of the green door.

He was going to knock on it and ask for Lucia. He was going to do that in a moment. He was standing here first because this was the first time since the apartment that he'd been somewhere without someone telling him where to be, and he needed to use it.

He thought about the fuel cell on his table. About the number. About the look on their faces when he'd shown Alpha what he'd found — the moment they understood what it meant for them, and the speed with which they'd decided to bury it and bury him with it. He thought about Red and Squirrel in his apartment doorway at six in the morning, with the car already running.

He was in Panama. He was alive. He was standing in front of a green door in a city he'd never been to, holding nothing that belonged to him, about to knock and ask for a woman named Lucia.

He knocked.
